# Topic 4.4 - Low-Power Wire-Frame Car

> **"The first motor should reveal the mechanism, not overpower it."**

---

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- explain why deliberately limited power reveals early drivetrain faults
- match a small brushed motor, switched AA holder and drive method
- build a removable motor module that resists torque reaction
- trace and protect the electrical and mechanical energy paths
- commission the drive through disconnected, wheels-up and crawl gates
- compare one drive variable without increasing the power boundary

---

# Before We Begin

The cardboard chassis passed every push test yesterday.

Today its new motor gives one quick twitch.

The belt climbs towards the edge of the pulley.

The rear axle slows, the motor mount twists and one AA cell begins to warm.

Someone suggests tightening the belt and adding another cell.

Both changes would add load before the first fault is understood.

This is the moment when a toy builder asks for more speed.

An engineer asks where the energy stopped being useful.

Topic 4.3 proved that the chassis rolls freely under a controlled push.
Now we add only enough power to study motor mounting, drive alignment and the current path.

This is a low-power engineering vehicle, not the final RC buggy.

Use six checks:

1. **Match** - are motor, cells, holder and drive parts compatible?
2. **Mount** - can the motor resist torque reaction without being permanent?
3. **Align** - do shafts, pulleys or friction surfaces share the intended path?
4. **Route** - are wires, airflow and the stop control protected and reachable?
5. **Pulse** - does the shortest useful wheels-up pulse behave as predicted?
6. **Inspect** - what changed, moved, rubbed, slipped or warmed before the next run?

# Define Low Power for This Stage

For this topic, use a commercially made low-voltage educational system such as:

- a small 3–6 V brushed DC hobby motor
- a switched holder for two or three AA cells, matched to the motor's rating
- insulated leads or safe reusable connectors
- a simple wheel, pulley or gearbox intended for the motor

The responsible adult must check the exact component ratings and instructions.

Use cells of the same type, brand, age and state of charge as a matched set.

Install every cell in the marked direction.

Replace the complete set rather than mixing old and new cells.

Do not use:

- LiPo packs
- brushless motors
- high-current RC batteries
- mains power
- damaged cells or holders
- loose bare wires pressed directly to cell ends
- a motor with no known voltage range

The cheapest motor is not useful if its rating and connections are unknown.

# Why Low Power Teaches More

Low power makes motion slower and easier to observe.

It reduces:

- impact energy
- drivetrain damage
- card tearing
- sudden movement
- heat produced during a short fault
- pressure to make every part final

It increases the chance that you notice:

- wheel rub
- belt slip
- axle movement
- chassis twist
- motor-mount rotation
- wiring snag

Low power does not mean no hazard.
A stalled small motor can still heat quickly, and a spinning axle can catch hair or loose clothing.

# Trace the Energy Path

Remember the energy path from Topic 3.1.

The electrical circuit must be complete for current to flow.
The mechanical path must be aligned for useful torque to reach the floor.

The switch belongs where an adult or learner can reach it without touching wheels or gears.

# Select a Drive Method

Three beginner-friendly methods are possible.

| Drive method | Advantage | Limitation | Good evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Friction drive | Few parts; easy to see | Can slip and wear | Contact force and wheel alignment |
| Elastic belt and pulleys | Adjustable ratio; isolates shafts | Belt can slip or climb off | Tension and pulley alignment |
| Purchased low-voltage gearbox | Reliable reduction | More cost; internals less visible | Mounting and output-shaft interface |

Avoid fixing the motor shaft directly to a vehicle wheel unless the motor and structure are designed for that load.
Most small motors benefit from a reduction that trades speed for usable wheel torque.

> **[Figure 4.4.4, F7 comparison array: friction drive, elastic-belt drive and purchased low-voltage gearbox shown at the same scale. Each panel labels the adjustable interface, likely slip point and evidence it teaches. Caption: Choose the simplest drive that answers the present question, not the one that looks most like the final buggy. Alt text: Three low-power drive methods compare contact, belt and enclosed gearbox interfaces.]**

# Gear Ratio and Pulley Ratio

Remember the jam-jar analogy for torque.
A larger radius gives a force more turning effect.

For a simple belt drive:

```text
speed ratio = driven pulley teeth or diameter ÷ driver pulley teeth or diameter
```

If the motor pulley is smaller than the axle pulley, the axle turns more slowly but can produce more torque, ignoring losses.

## Worked Example

**Question:** What is the approximate reduction when a 10 mm motor pulley drives a 30 mm axle pulley?

**Known values:**

- driver diameter = 10 mm
- driven diameter = 30 mm

**Rule:**

```text
reduction = driven ÷ driver
```

**Calculation:**

```text
reduction = 30 ÷ 10
          = 3
```

**Meaning:**

The drive is approximately 3:1.
The axle turns about once for every three motor revolutions, with increased ideal torque at the axle.

Elastic belts can slip, so measured behaviour will not be perfect.
That difference is useful evidence.

> **[Figure 4.4.5, F6 measured drawing: 10 mm driver and 30 mm driven pulley joined by an aligned belt. Rotation marks show three motor turns for about one axle turn, with the 3:1 calculation beside it. Caption: Reduction lowers ideal output speed while increasing the available turning effect at the axle. Alt text: Dimensioned belt drive demonstrates a three-to-one pulley reduction.]**

# Build a Removable Motor Module

The motor should not be glued forever into the chassis.

Create a small module that controls:

- motor position
- shaft direction
- pulley or friction-wheel alignment
- belt tension or contact force
- wire strain relief
- airflow
- switch access
- removal path

Useful reversible attachments include:

- card slots and tabs
- elastic bands
- cable ties used with adult approval
- small screws into a reusable block
- tape straps that can be replaced

Mark the motor shaft centreline on the module.
Mark the driven axle centreline on the chassis.

Misalignment is visible when these references do not agree.

> **[Figure 4.4.6, F6 measured drawing: removable card motor module with motor shaft centreline, axle centreline, slotted tension adjustment, two restraint straps, cooling opening, wire strain-relief point, witness line and upward removal arrow. Caption: A useful motor mount controls position, reaction, cooling and service without becoming permanent. Alt text: Dimensioned removable motor module identifies alignment, adjustment, restraint and access features.]**

# Belt Tension Is a Trade-Off

A belt that is too loose slips.
A belt that is too tight pulls shafts sideways and increases friction.

The useful setting is the least tension that transmits the required low-power load reliably.

Do not use belt tension to bend the motor mount into alignment.
Align first, then set tension.

For a friction drive, the same trade-off appears as contact pressure.

> **[Figure 4.4.7, F7 comparison array: loose, usable and over-tight belt conditions viewed from above. Loose belt sags and slips; usable belt tracks centrally with minimal tension; over-tight belt bends the card mount and adds orange side-load arrows to both shafts. Caption: Use the least tension that transmits the required low-power load reliably. Alt text: Three belt settings compare slip, central tracking and harmful shaft side load.]**

# Motor Torque Pushes Back

Stand on a swivel chair and twist a heavy jar lid.
Your body tends to rotate in the opposite direction.

When the motor turns the drivetrain, the drivetrain pushes back on the motor.
This is **torque reaction**.

The motor mount must resist it.

Draw a witness line across the motor module and chassis.
If the line separates during a test, the mount moved.

Do not simply add more tape before recording the movement.

# Stall Is a Stop Signal

A **stall** occurs when the motor receives electrical power but its shaft cannot turn.

At stall:

- motor speed is zero
- current may become much higher than during free running
- electrical energy becomes heat rapidly
- wires, cells and motor can warm
- the card mount can be damaged by torque

If the motor stops unexpectedly, switch off immediately.

Do not hold the car down while continuing to power it.
Do not repeatedly flick the switch to “see if it clears”.

Find the mechanical cause with power disconnected.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Rub your hands slowly, then press harder while trying to move them.
> What happens to resistance and warmth?

A stalled drive is not identical, but the experience helps show why blocked motion can turn input energy into heat instead of useful movement.

> **[Figure 4.4.8, F4 sequence strip: four panels show normal motor rotation, wheel becoming blocked, shaft speed falling to zero with current and heat arrows rising, then the switch moving immediately to OFF before inspection. Caption: Stall is a stop signal, not a request for another pulse. Alt text: A blocked low-voltage motor stops rotating, heats and is switched off immediately.]**

# Plan the Wiring

Use a simple documented path:

```text
battery holder positive -> switch -> motor -> battery holder negative
```

The exact polarity controls motor direction.
If the motor spins the wrong way, disconnect power before changing the appropriate low-voltage connection according to the component instructions.

Provide:

- insulated joints
- strain relief near the motor
- no wires near rotating parts
- no bare conductor touching the chassis or cells
- an immediately reachable switch
- a removable battery holder

Soldering, if needed, follows Topic 2.9 and must be performed with adult supervision in the prepared workshop.
Reusable screw terminals or prewired leads are a valid alternative.

# Commission in Layers

Commissioning means checking a new system in a controlled order before normal use.

Use this sequence:

1. battery disconnected: inspect structure and wiring
2. battery disconnected: rotate drivetrain by hand
3. wheels raised: connect battery with switch off
4. wheels raised: use a very short power pulse
5. switch off and disconnect: inspect heat, rub and movement
6. wheels raised: confirm correct direction
7. on floor: clear-area crawl test
8. switch off and disconnect after every run

Do not skip directly to a floor run.

# The Wheels-Up Stand

A wheels-up stand keeps driven wheels away from the table and prevents the car launching during the first test.

Use two stable card blocks under the chassis, not under moving axles.

The stand must:

- support the chassis securely
- keep every driven wheel clear
- avoid pinching wires
- leave the switch accessible
- resist vibration

Push the unpowered vehicle gently before use.
If the stand rocks, redesign it.

# Observe Temperature Safely

Do not touch a motor immediately after a fault.

During normal short low-power tests, stop and inspect after each run.
An adult may use a non-contact thermometer if available.

Without one, use conservative short runs and allow cooling time.
Any unusual smell, smoke, discolouration, rapid heat or softening card is an immediate stop condition.

Never wrap the motor in insulating foam or sealed tape.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Feel Torque Reaction

This activity needs no motor or electricity.

## Question

Why must the motor mount resist a turning effect opposite to the driven shaft?

## Materials

- pencil or rolled paper shaft
- loose card cradle
- long paper flag
- tape

## Method

1. Place the pencil loosely in the cradle.
2. Attach the long flag to the pencil.
3. Hold the cradle lightly without clamping the pencil.
4. Twist the flag clockwise.
5. Observe the cradle’s tendency.
6. Repeat anticlockwise.
7. Draw paired arrows for shaft torque and reaction.

## What it proves

The hand-powered model demonstrates equal-and-opposite turning tendencies at the shaft and support.

It does not predict the real motor torque or mount strength.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - Pulley Ratio Predictor

## Question

Can pulley size predict the approximate turn relationship before power is added?

## Materials

- card circles in at least two diameters
- two pencils or split pins
- elastic band
- marker pen

## Method

1. Build a small and large card pulley on separate shafts.
2. Mark a radial line on each pulley.
3. Connect them with the elastic band using minimal working tension.
4. Turn the small driver exactly three revolutions.
5. Count the large pulley’s revolutions.
6. Reverse the driver and driven positions.
7. Compare both results with the diameter ratios.

## Fair-test rule

Keep the same belt, shaft spacing, turn count and observer.

Change only which pulley acts as the driver.

## What it proves

It shows the direction of the speed-and-torque trade-off.

Slip and hand error mean the result will be approximate.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Commissioning Rehearsal

Use only paper models and string.

## Question

Can another person follow the safe commissioning order and reach the stop control without approaching movement?

## Materials

- paper motor, holder, switch and axle cards
- string for wires and belt
- two card blocks for a wheels-up stand
- six commissioning cards

## Method

1. Arrange the unpowered paper system on the stand.
2. Route the string wires with bends, restraint and service slack.
3. Put the six cards in order: `MATCH`, `MOUNT`, `ALIGN`, `ROUTE`, `PULSE`, `INSPECT`.
4. Ask another person to rehearse the sequence.
5. Say “stall” during the pretend pulse.
6. Check whether they reach `OFF`, remove the holder model and inspect mechanically.
7. Record every unclear route or instruction.

## What it proves

It proves the physical stop access, route plan and written order are understandable.

It cannot prove ratings, polarity, insulation, real rotation or temperature.

# Topic Build - Low-Power Wire-Frame Car 🛠️

Convert the passing rolling chassis into a switched, low-voltage car with a removable drive module.

## Materials

- passing chassis from Topic 4.3
- small 3–6 V brushed educational motor with known rating
- matched switched two- or three-cell AA holder
- suitable AA cells of the same type and condition
- prewired leads, insulated reusable connectors or adult-supervised soldered joints
- drive parts: elastic belt and pulleys, friction wheel or matched low-voltage gearbox
- scrap card for motor module and wheels-up stand
- removable straps, elastic bands or ties
- tape and glue for structure, not electrical insulation unless rated for it
- ruler and pencil
- test markers and timer

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult or guardian the exact motor, cells, holder, wiring and test area before starting, and perform every powered test with them present.
> Use only the motor's stated low-voltage range and a proper switched battery holder; never use mains power or a LiPo pack here.
> Use a matched set of cells of the same type and condition; never mix old and new cells, mix chemistries or reverse polarity.
> Disconnect the battery before adjusting the motor, belt, wheels, axles or wires.
> Keep fingers, hair, loose clothing, jewellery and wires away from rotating parts.
> Raise the driven wheels for first power and make the switch immediately reachable.
> Stop instantly for a stall, unexpected movement, heat, smell, smoke, damaged insulation or loose cell.
> Remove cells from the prototype after testing and store them according to their instructions.

>
> - BBC Bitesize KS3 Physics: search "electric motors" for a visual explanation of current producing rotation.
> - BBC Bitesize KS3 Design and Technology: search "mechanical systems" for pulleys, gears and transmission.

## Step 1 - Write the power boundary

Record motor voltage range, holder cell count, cell type, drive method and adult approval.

## Step 2 - Recheck the rolling gate

Repeat a hand push and wheel-spin check.

Do not continue if the chassis has warped in storage.

## Step 3 - Make the drive module

Mark shaft centre, attachment points, tension adjustment and removal direction.

## Step 4 - Fit the mechanical drive

Align pulleys, friction surfaces or gearbox output.

Rotate the entire path by hand.

## Step 5 - Add wiring with no battery

Secure the holder and switch.

Protect every joint and provide strain relief.

## Step 6 - Build the stand

Prove that all driven wheels clear it and the chassis cannot rock off.

## Step 7 - Conduct pre-power inspection

An adult checks:

- ratings
- polarity plan
- insulation
- restraint
- switch position
- moving-part clearance
- stand stability
- clear test area

## Step 8 - First pulse

With wheels raised, apply the shortest useful switch-on pulse.

Switch off, disconnect and inspect.

## Step 9 - Direction and alignment

Reconnect only after the inspection passes.

Confirm intended rotation and watch the belt or friction wheel from a safe distance.

## Step 10 - Crawl test

Place the car on a clear smooth floor.

Use a short marked lane and a brief run.
Switch off before reaching the lane end.

## Step 11 - Inspect between runs

Check:

- motor module witness lines
- belt position
- wheel rub
- axle support movement
- wire restraint
- temperature warning signs

## Step 12 - Compare one drive variable

Choose one:

- small versus larger axle pulley
- two motor positions that alter belt tension
- two friction-contact pressures
- loaded versus unloaded condition

Keep voltage, surface and other variables unchanged.

## Step 13 - Reflection

Record:

- where electrical energy entered
- where torque was transmitted
- where energy was lost
- what moved under torque reaction
- why the selected low-power setup is enough for this evidence

Keep the complete low-power car and its disconnected cell holder as a supervised demonstration artifact.

# Learning Goal

Prove that the structure can carry a small power system and transmit controlled torque without binding, overheating or losing serviceability.

# Cheapest Valid Prototype

A known 3–6 V brushed motor, matched switched AA holder and simple removable friction or belt drive on the passing card chassis.

# What to Buy, Reuse and Make

## Buy now

Only if not already available:

- low-voltage motor with known specification
- switched battery holder
- matched cells
- safe connector or prewired lead set

## Reuse now

- Topic 4.3 chassis and wheels
- card, elastic bands and suitable pulleys
- workshop connectors and basic measuring tools

## Make now

- motor module
- tension or contact adjustment
- wheels-up stand
- wiring restraints
- short test lane
- commissioning and result sheets

## Buy later

Do not buy the final brushless system.
A surface radio set and suitable low-power control solution are considered for Topic 4.5 only after this stage passes.

# Cost Checkpoint

Typical planning allowance: **£5-£15**, depending on what is already owned.

Before purchase, record:

- exact motor voltage range
- holder voltage with the chosen cells
- whether leads and switch are included
- drive parts included
- delivery and small connector cost
- whether the parts remain useful for future teaching

Stop if the only available option requires an unfamiliar high-current battery.

| Interface | Controlled evidence |

# Test Plan

Use the same inspection sheet before every powered step.

## Test 1 - Unpowered Drive Fit

**Question:** Does adding the drive module preserve free movement?

**Independent variable:** Drive module absent or fitted and engaged.

**Dependent variable:** Hand-rotation effort and wheel spin behaviour.

**Control variables:** Same chassis, load, wheels and inspection method.

**Procedure:** Measure the baseline hand-rotation behaviour, fit the aligned drive module with no battery present, then repeat the same rotation and inspect witness lines.

**Pass condition:** The drivetrain turns smoothly with no hard point, wheel rub or shifting mount.

## Test 2 - Wheels-Up Commissioning

**Question:** Does the electrical and mechanical path operate correctly during short no-ground-load pulses?

**Independent variable:** Commissioning pulse number, with duration kept inside the adult-approved short limit.

**Dependent variable:** Rotation direction, smoothness, noise, belt tracking and warning signs.

**Control variables:** Same voltage, stand, wiring and drivetrain setup.

**Procedure:** Apply a brief pulse, disconnect, inspect and record before any repeat.

**Pass condition:** Correct direction, smooth operation, stable stand, no loose parts, stall, heat, smell, smoke or damaged insulation.

## Test 3 - Crawl and Ratio Comparison

**Question:** Can the car move through the short lane safely, and does one ratio change improve the chosen measure without creating a new fault?

**Independent variable:** Driver-and-driven pulley combination or the single approved drive variable.

**Dependent variables:** start reliability, lane time, drift, slip and witness-line movement.

**Control variables:** same matched cells, motor, vehicle mass, surface, start, lane, switch operator and inspection interval.

**Procedure:** Conduct at least three short runs for configuration A and three for B in alternating order, switching off, disconnecting and inspecting between every run.

**Pass condition:** Every run remains controllable, the selected option starts without stall or excessive slip, no warning sign occurs, witness lines remain aligned and the switch plus disconnect remain reachable without approaching rotating parts.

# Upgrade Path

Low-voltage switched drive -> low-power reversible ESC or suitable controller -> steering servo and surface radio -> donor drivetrain -> printed motor and electronics modules -> final rated RC power system.

Control comes next.
Higher power does not.

# Stop Point Before Topic 4.5

Do not add radio control until:

- the motor, holder and cells have documented compatible ratings
- the adult-supervised pre-power inspection is complete
- the drivetrain rotates freely by hand
- the wheels-up stand is stable
- correct rotation is confirmed
- the switch can be reached immediately
- at least five short floor runs are complete
- the motor module does not move under torque reaction
- wiring remains insulated, restrained and clear of moving parts
- no stall, rapid heat, smell, smoke or cell problem occurred
- the chosen drive variable is supported by fair-test evidence
- the battery holder and motor module remain removable
- the Build Passport and cost ledger are updated

Radio control must use components whose manuals and electrical ratings are understood.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Calling Speed the Success Measure

This stage succeeds by revealing safe, aligned power transmission.

A slower car that starts reliably teaches more than one uncontrolled sprint.

> **[Figure 4.4.11, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel celebrates a fast blurred car while its belt leaves the pulley and witness line separates; right panel shows a slow marked lane with stable tracking, inspection card and recorded result. Caption: Controlled evidence is the achievement; speed is only one later performance measure. Alt text: Uncontrolled fast run hides faults while a slow measured crawl preserves evidence.]**

## Mistake 2 - Using an Available but Unmatched Power Source

This topic deliberately uses a matched low-voltage AA holder system.

Do not substitute a LiPo, mains supply, mixed cells or an extra cell to force movement.

> **[Figure 4.4.12, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel shows undocumented pack, loose cells and crossed-out mixed old and new batteries; right panel shows a labelled switched holder with identical cells, polarity marks and motor voltage record. Caption: Availability does not prove compatibility. Alt text: Unmatched battery choices are rejected beside a documented matched AA holder and motor.]**

## Mistake 3 - Repeating Power into a Stall

Switch off immediately when the shaft stops unexpectedly.

Disconnect and find the mechanical cause by hand.

> **[Figure 4.4.13, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel shows repeated ON pulses into a blocked wheel with rising heat symbols; right panel shows immediate OFF, holder disconnected and hand-rotation inspection of the belt. Caption: Repeated pulses add heat but no diagnosis. Alt text: Stalled motor is repeatedly powered in the wrong panel and safely disconnected for mechanical inspection in the right panel.]**

## Mistake 4 - Making Alignment Permanent Too Early

Glue and extreme belt tension can trap a poor shaft relationship.

Use a removable mount, align first and apply only useful tension.

> **[Figure 4.4.14, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel shows a permanently glued tilted motor pulled sideways by a tight belt; right panel shows slotted removable mount, matching centrelines and a centrally tracking belt. Caption: Adjustment belongs in the interface until the evidence is stable. Alt text: Glued misaligned motor is compared with an adjustable aligned belt module.]**

## Mistake 5 - Adjusting a Live Mechanism

Leaving the holder connected while touching belts, wheels or wires creates an unnecessary start hazard.

Switch off, disconnect and prove zero motion before adjustment.

> **[Figure 4.4.15, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel shows fingers approaching a belt while the holder remains connected; right panel shows switch at OFF, unplugged holder placed away and a hand rotating the mechanism safely. Caption: The adjustment state is electrically disconnected, not merely quiet. Alt text: Connected motor mechanism is approached unsafely while the correct setup is switched off and disconnected.]**

## Mistake 6 - Changing Cells, Ratio and Load Together

Several changes may alter the result in opposite directions.

Change one main variable and repeat the same test.

> **[Figure 4.4.16, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel changes cell count, pulley pair and vehicle load on one test card; right panel keeps cells and load fixed while comparing only two pulley ratios in alternating runs. Caption: One controlled change creates an interpretable result. Alt text: Three simultaneous drive changes are rejected beside a fair pulley-ratio comparison.]**

# Topic Summary 📝

- Low power makes early drivetrain faults easier and safer to observe.
- This stage uses a known low-voltage brushed motor and matched switched AA holder.
- Electrical energy flows through the switch and motor; mechanical torque flows through the drive to the wheels.
- Friction, belt and purchased gearbox drives offer different learning trade-offs.
- Reduction trades rotational speed for increased ideal axle torque.
- A removable motor module controls alignment, tension, cooling and torque reaction.
- Too little belt tension slips; too much adds load and friction.
- Stall means powered zero speed and is an immediate stop condition.
- Wires require insulation, strain relief and separation from movement.
- Commissioning progresses from disconnected inspection to wheels-up pulses and short floor runs.
- Repeated inspections reveal joint movement and heat warning signs.
- Control is added only after safe low-power movement is proven.

# Looking Ahead 🔭

The wire-frame car can move under its own limited power, but a person still operates the switch and direction is mostly fixed.

In **Topic 4.5 - Radio-Controlled Prototype**, we will add a surface transmitter, receiver, steering servo and compatible motor controller without increasing the mechanical power target.

The new question is whether commands can be sent, limited, stopped and failed safely while the vehicle remains serviceable.

# Answers 🔑

1. The final RC system could overpower or damage an early card mechanism; deliberately low power keeps alignment, slip, rubbing and mount movement visible.
2. Electrical energy travels from the matched AA holder through the switch to the brushed motor; mechanical torque travels from the motor shaft through the selected drive to the axle, tyres and floor.
3. A friction drive is simple but can slip and wear; a belt drive makes ratio and alignment visible but needs useful tension; a purchased gearbox is reliable but costs more and hides its internal reduction.
4. `36 ÷ 12 = 3`, so the approximate reduction is 3:1.
5. The driven shaft applies an opposite torque reaction to the motor case, so an inadequate mount can rotate, loosen or misalign the drive.
6. The powered motor shaft stops, current rises towards the stall value and input energy becomes heat instead of useful movement.
7. Excessive belt tension pulls shafts sideways, increases support friction, bends card mounts and can reduce the useful output.
8. Check documented ratings, matched cells and polarity plan, structure, hand-rotated freedom, insulation, restraint, switch access, moving-part clearance, cooling and stand stability.
9. Raising the driven wheels prevents an unexpected launch and reduces ground load while direction, smoothness, belt tracking and stop access are checked.
10. Immediate stop signs include stall, unexpected movement, rapid heat, unusual smell, smoke, discolouration, softened card, loose cell or damaged insulation.
11. Keep the motor, cells, vehicle mass, surface, lane, operator and inspection method unchanged; change only the selected pulley or drive variable and alternate the test order.
12. Radio control adds command, steering and failsafe evidence while the proven low mechanical-power boundary remains unchanged; higher power would add energy before control is understood.
