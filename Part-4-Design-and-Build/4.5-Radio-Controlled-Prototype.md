# Topic 4.5 - Radio-Controlled Prototype

> **"Remote control is not merely making the buggy move; it is proving that every command has a safe result."**

---

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- choose one complete control path and prove compatibility before connection
- map command and energy routes using exact component manuals
- centre steering mechanically and limit travel before powered movement
- commission steering and throttle separately on a stable stand
- verify neutral throttle, stop access, failsafe and range behaviour
- produce repeatable low-speed control and service evidence

---

# Before We Begin

A transmitter wheel turns left.

The buggy’s wheels turn right.

The trigger returns to neutral, but the driven wheels creep forward.

At full steering lock, the servo buzzes against the linkage.

Then the transmitter is switched off and the motor remembers the last command.

Every individual component may be working.

The command system is not yet safe.

Topic 4.4 proved that limited electrical power can move the wire-frame car.
Topic 4.5 adds steering and remote throttle control while keeping the speed target modest.

The aim is not to drive far or fast.
The aim is to make command behaviour predictable, limited and safe.

Prove six checks:

1. **Identity** - are the exact models and manuals known?
2. **Route** - does each command and supply reach the correct port?
3. **Direction** - does each input create the intended movement?
4. **Neutral** - do steering and throttle return to their safe resting results?
5. **Limit** - do endpoints and throttle limits prevent strain and excessive speed?
6. **Loss** - does a manual-approved signal-loss test produce neutral throttle?

# Choose One Complete Control Path

Do not build a radio system from unrelated bargains.

Choose one of two valid paths.

## Path A - Matched donor control system

Reuse a complete low-power system from one working donor vehicle:

- transmitter
- receiver/control board
- steering actuator or servo
- motor
- battery system
- charger if rechargeable
- wiring and switches

This can be inexpensive and electrically compatible.
Its limitation is that connectors, steering behaviour and control board may be less modular.

## Path B - Hobby-grade modular system

Use a matched set selected by ratings and manuals:

- surface transmitter
- compatible receiver
- standard steering servo
- brushed ESC or suitable controller
- correctly rated motor
- approved battery and charger
- correct connectors

This costs more but creates reusable standard interfaces.

Do not connect the Topic 4.4 motor or battery automatically.
Recheck the complete voltage and current path for the selected controller.

# Create a Compatibility Table

Compatibility is a chain.
One correct pair does not prove the whole chain.

| Connection | Question | Evidence source |
|---|---|---|
| Transmitter to receiver | Do they use a compatible radio protocol? | Both manuals |
| Receiver to servo | Correct signal type, plug and supply voltage? | Receiver and servo manuals |
| Receiver to ESC | Correct channel and signal type? | Receiver and ESC manuals |
| ESC BEC to receiver | Correct regulated receiver supply? | ESC manual |
| ESC to motor | Brushed/brushless type and rating compatible? | ESC and motor specifications |
| Battery to ESC | Voltage, chemistry and connector compatible? | Battery and ESC manuals |
| Charger to battery | Correct chemistry, cell count and settings? | Charger and battery manuals |

The **BEC**, introduced in Topic 3.6, is the circuit that can supply regulated power to the receiver and servo from the main battery.

Some ESC manuals warn against connecting a separate receiver battery at the same time.
Follow the exact manual; two power sources can damage equipment.

# Mount the Receiver Thoughtfully

The receiver is the command hub.

Its position should provide:

- protection from impact and dirt
- access to channel ports
- wire strain relief
- removal without dismantling the drivetrain
- antenna placement required by its manual
- separation from high-current wiring and motor where practical

Do not cut, shorten, kink or modify an antenna.
Manufacturer manuals commonly warn that antenna damage or shortening reduces range.

If the receiver includes stabilisation features, orientation may matter.
Follow its exact marked direction.

# Understand Channel Mapping

Many surface systems use:

- Channel 1 for steering
- Channel 2 for throttle/ESC

But the manual is the authority.

Make a port map before plugging anything in:

| Receiver port | Device | Signal wire orientation | Checked by |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH1 | Steering servo | According to receiver marking |  |
| CH2 | ESC/controller | According to receiver marking |  |
| AUX | Unused/declared accessory | According to manual |  |

Never force a connector.
Check polarity marks and keyed shapes.

# Centre Steering Mechanically

Electronic trim is like correcting a crooked picture by tilting the entire wall.
It may hide the symptom while reducing useful adjustment on one side.

Use this order:

1. disconnect motor power or keep the drivetrain safely inactive
2. transmitter steering trim at neutral
3. power the receiver according to the manual
4. let the servo move to commanded centre
5. power off and disconnect
6. fit the servo horn as close to the intended centre as its splines allow
7. adjust linkage lengths so wheels are approximately straight
8. use small trim only for final correction

Never hold the horn while the servo is powered.

# Endpoints Protect the Servo

An **endpoint** is the transmitter setting that limits how far a servo is commanded in one direction.

The steering mechanism has mechanical limits.
If the transmitter commands beyond them, the servo may buzz, draw extra current, heat or damage gears.

Set endpoints gradually:

- raise the chassis so tyres do not scrub
- reduce steering travel before testing
- move slowly toward one side
- stop increasing before linkage bind or servo strain
- repeat on the other side
- check that nothing collides through suspension movement

The left and right endpoint values do not have to be identical.
The mechanical geometry may not be perfectly symmetrical.

# Binding Creates the Radio Link

**Binding** pairs a transmitter and receiver so they recognise one another.

The exact order and button timing vary.

Use only the official manual sequence for the exact models.

Before binding:

- vehicle is on a stable wheels-up stand
- throttle is neutral
- steering is centred
- drive power is disabled if the manual permits
- transmitter has adequate battery
- area is clear
- adult supervisor is present

Never copy a random sequence from a different radio model.

# ESC Calibration Teaches the Throttle Range

Many hobby ESCs need to learn:

- neutral
- full throttle
- full brake or reverse

This is **calibration**.

Button timing, tones and LEDs differ between models.

Follow the exact ESC instructions.
Do not treat the generic three-point idea as a programming recipe.

Before calibration, check the manual's required:

- throttle trim
- throttle channel direction
- travel setting
- braking or ABS setting
- battery type setting
- motor connection state

Horizon Hobby's Firma manuals, for example, specify neutral, throttle and brake steps and warn that a separate receiver battery must not be connected alongside the ESC's supply.
Your equipment may differ.

# Failsafe Must Produce a Safe Result

A **failsafe** is the receiver behaviour when a valid transmitter signal is lost.

For this ground vehicle, the essential throttle result is:

```text
lost signal -> neutral throttle / motor stopped
```

The steering response may hold its last position or move to a preset position depending on the system.
The manual determines what can be configured.

Never assume binding automatically stored the safe throttle value.
Test it with driven wheels raised.

Futaba surface-radio manuals describe failsafe as moving channels to preset positions when reception is lost.
The safe position still has to be chosen and verified.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> If a buggy loses its radio signal while the throttle command is forward, should it remember the last command or stop?
> What could happen in each case?

A neutral-throttle failsafe prevents an old forward command continuing without the driver.

# Use a Safe Power Sequence

For many RC surface systems, the normal sequence is:

```text
ON:  transmitter first -> vehicle second
OFF: vehicle first -> transmitter second
```

This helps ensure a valid command source exists while the vehicle is powered.

However, follow the exact equipment manual.

After operation, switch off and disconnect the vehicle battery.
Some ESCs continue drawing a small current while the switch is off, which can over-discharge a connected battery.

# Separate Steering and Throttle Commissioning

Test one command path at a time.

## Steering-only stage

- motor cannot run
- wheels raised
- verify direction
- verify neutral
- set endpoints
- check linkage through suspension movement
- listen for strain

## Throttle-only stage

- steering fixed straight
- driven wheels raised
- verify neutral
- verify direction
- verify brake/reverse behaviour
- verify throttle limit
- perform failsafe test

## Combined stage

- low-speed setting
- clear floor lane
- short distance
- spotter near accessible vehicle stop control
- no people inside the driving area

One new difficulty at a time makes faults easier to identify.

# Limit Speed by More Than Trigger Skill

An 11-year-old learner should not be expected to make a fast system safe solely by holding the trigger gently.

Use engineering controls where supported:

- transmitter throttle limit or dual-rate setting
- conservative ESC mode
- low-power compatible motor and battery
- reduction gearing
- short test lane
- physical separation from people

Record every limit and how it can be checked.

# Perform a Range Check

A range check proves control remains reliable over the intended test distance.

The exact reduced-power or full-power method comes from the transmitter manual.

Use:

- an adult supervising the stationary vehicle
- driven wheels raised or motor made safe for the first check
- a clear open area
- agreed hand signals or phone communication
- gradual increase in distance
- steering observation
- immediate stop for hesitation or loss of control

Never make the first range check by driving the vehicle away.

# Make a Command Matrix

Before floor driving, write expected responses.

| Driver action | Expected vehicle response | Actual | Pass? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steering wheel left | Front wheels turn left |  |  |
| Steering released | Wheels return near neutral |  |  |
| Gentle throttle | Driven wheels rotate forward slowly |  |  |
| Trigger neutral | Motor stops |  |  |
| Brake command | Motor brakes according to setup |  |  |
| Transmitter signal removed in test | Throttle becomes neutral |  |  |

Do not reverse a servo or channel until the motor is safe and the correct channel has been identified.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Human Command Chain

This no-equipment activity makes invisible radio logic physical.

## Materials

- cards labelled transmitter, receiver, servo, ESC, motor and neutral
- command cards for left, right, forward, brake and signal lost

## Method

1. Give each person one component card.
2. Pass a steering command through transmitter, receiver and servo.
3. Pass a throttle command through transmitter, receiver, ESC and motor.
4. Insert `SIGNAL LOST` while forward is active.
5. Require the chain to produce the failsafe result written on the Manual Card.
6. Swap CH1 and CH2 deliberately and diagnose the wrong route.

## What it proves

It distinguishes command route from energy route and makes neutral failsafe memorable.

It cannot prove real protocol compatibility or signal behaviour.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - Centre and Limit Challenge

Use the unpowered cardboard steering rig from Topic 3.5.

## Method

1. Make one linkage deliberately too short.
2. Try to straighten the wheels using a paper trim scale.
3. Record the unequal travel that remains.
4. Return trim to neutral.
5. Correct the linkage mechanically.
6. Move the rig by hand towards each mechanical stop.
7. Mark `SAFE`, `APPROACHING LIMIT` and `BIND` on separate left and right scales.

## Fair-test rule

Keep the same rig and wheel references.

Change only whether centring is mechanical or hidden with pretend trim.

## What it proves

Mechanical centring preserves more useful and balanced command range.

The paper scales cannot provide the final electronic endpoint values.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Fault-Call Rehearsal

Use cards instead of powered equipment.

## Method

1. Lay out the exact manual sequence as cards.
2. Put the paper vehicle on a card wheels-up stand.
3. Ask another person to call `START`, `STOP`, `SIGNAL LOSS` or `FAULT`.
4. Perform the written transmitter, vehicle and disconnect order.
5. For signal loss, show the expected neutral-throttle evidence.
6. For fault, reach the vehicle stop without crossing a moving zone.
7. Record every hesitation or ambiguous instruction.

## What it proves

The rehearsal proves the team understands its model-specific cards and physical stop access.

It does not prove binding, calibration, failsafe or radio range.

# Topic Build - Controlled Wire-Frame RC Car 🛠️

Add remote steering and throttle to the passing low-power vehicle while preserving modularity and low speed.

## Materials

- passing Topic 4.4 chassis and structure
- one complete compatible control path approved by an adult
- surface transmitter and matching receiver, or complete donor control set
- compatible steering servo and horn/linkage
- compatible brushed ESC/controller and motor
- correctly rated approved battery and charger if rechargeable
- manufacturer manuals for every powered or radio device
- removable mounting tape, straps, screws or ties as permitted
- card for receiver tray, servo mount and guards
- wire restraints
- stable wheels-up stand
- ruler, test markers and result sheets

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult or guardian the complete compatibility table, manuals, wiring and test plan before assembly, and perform every powered or radio test with them present.
> Follow the exact battery, charger, transmitter, receiver, servo and ESC manuals; never guess connector polarity, binding, calibration or battery settings.
> Keep the vehicle on a stable wheels-up stand for binding, calibration, direction, endpoint and failsafe tests.
> Disconnect the main battery before changing plugs, horn position, linkages, gearing or mounts.
> Keep hair, fingers, loose clothing, jewellery and wires away from moving parts.
> Never stand in front of or behind driven wheels during a powered check.
> Stop for unexpected movement, servo buzz, stall, loss of control, heat, smell, smoke, damaged wiring or swollen/damaged battery.
> Charge and store rechargeable batteries exactly as taught in Topic 3.3 and specified by their manufacturer.

> **🎬 Watch the build**
>
> - Your transmitter manufacturer: search the exact model plus "official binding failsafe manual" and watch with an adult.
> - Your ESC manufacturer: search the exact model plus "official calibration manual" and match every tone or LED to the written instructions.
> - Horizon Hobby Support: search "Firma ESC manual installation wiring calibration" for a clear example of the checks, even if your equipment differs.

# Test Plan

Keep the low mechanical-power boundary from Topic 4.4 for every test.

## Test 1 - Steering Command and Limit

**Question:** Does every steering command produce the intended movement without mechanical bind?

**Independent variable:** Commanded steering position.

**Dependent variable:** Wheel angle, return to neutral and servo strain signs.

**Control variables:** Same voltage, linkage, endpoint settings, stand and suspension position.

**Procedure:** Test centre, quarter, half and full permitted command in both directions with motor inactive.

**Pass condition:** Correct direction, repeatable neutral, no collision, buzz, heat or linkage bind.

## Test 2 - Throttle, Stop and Signal Loss

**Question:** Does the controller interpret neutral, forward and brake/reverse correctly, and does signal loss produce neutral throttle?

**Independent variable:** trigger position or the exact manual-approved signal condition.

**Dependent variables:** motor direction, speed response, stop behaviour and failsafe output.

**Control variables:** Same stand, battery state, throttle limit, calibration and drivetrain.

**Procedure:** With driven wheels raised, test small commands before larger permitted commands, return to neutral each time, then create the manual-approved signal-loss condition while an adult controls vehicle power.

**Pass condition:** Neutral stops the motor, intended commands respond correctly, signal loss produces and holds neutral throttle, documented steering behaviour matches the manual and no unexpected start, stall or warning sign occurs.

## Test 3 - Range, Crawl and Service Release

**Question:** Does the documented radio system control the low-speed vehicle across the required distance and remain serviceable afterwards?

**Independent variable:** test stage: stationary range position, crawl trial or powered-down service task.

**Dependent variables:** response reliability, gate contacts, stopping position, fault-stop response and unrelated interfaces disturbed.

**Control variables:** same exact equipment, antenna installation, battery state, throttle limit, area, course, surface, driver and written service route.

**Procedure:** First complete the official stationary range-check method; then perform at least five supervised crawl-course runs; finally power down, disconnect and remove plus reinstall the receiver tray and servo module.

**Pass condition:** The manual’s range requirement passes without unexplained interruption, every crawl remains inside the safe area with reliable stopping, no warning sign appears and service changes no drivetrain or suspension datum.

# Upgrade Path

Matched donor radio control -> hobby-grade modular radio -> measured donor steering/drivetrain -> printed servo and electronics modules -> final rated power system -> telemetry and advanced control in Part 5.

# Stop Point Before Topic 4.6

Do not buy or dismantle a donor platform until:

- the complete control path has a signed compatibility table
- every exact manual is recorded
- steering centres mechanically and returns predictably
- endpoints prevent mechanical bind
- throttle calibration matches the manual
- throttle is limited for the prototype
- the command matrix passes
- neutral failsafe is demonstrated with wheels raised
- the range check passes the manual and project requirement
- the low-speed course is completed safely
- the antenna and wires are protected
- battery removal and module service remain possible
- no unexplained heat, reset, interference or loss of control remains
- the Build Passport records what the donor platform must provide

The next purchase is evidence-led donor hardware, not more speed.

# Topic Summary 📝

- Radio control must produce predictable and safe command results.
- A complete matched donor path and a hobby-grade modular path are both valid.
- Compatibility covers protocol, voltage, signal, chemistry, current, connectors and charging.
- Exact manuals control binding, calibration, antenna placement and failsafe setup.
- The receiver and antenna need protection, access and correct orientation.
- Mechanical centring comes before electronic trim.
- Endpoints prevent servo commands from driving against mechanical limits.
- Failsafe must produce neutral throttle and be tested with driven wheels raised.
- Commission steering and throttle separately before combining them.
- Engineering limits reduce speed instead of relying only on trigger skill.
- A stationary range check comes before driving at distance.
- Serviceable radio modules can transfer into later buggy versions.

# Answers 🔑

1. A matched donor path reuses one proven set and may be inexpensive but less modular; a hobby-grade path uses separately documented standard components and is more reusable but usually costs more.
2. Connector shape does not prove voltage, polarity, signal type, current capacity or protocol compatibility.
3. Record manufacturer, exact model, official manual, voltage range, channel use, polarity, binding, calibration, failsafe, antenna rules and important warnings.
4. The powered servo must first move to commanded neutral so the horn and linkage can be fitted around the real centre instead of a guessed shaft position.
5. Servo buzz, heating, linkage strain, stalled movement or contact at full lock suggests an endpoint may be too large.
6. Binding controls, timing, LED states, power order and failsafe storage vary by model, so a different radio’s sequence can be wrong.
7. Many ESCs learn neutral, full throttle and full brake or reverse, but the exact model manual controls the procedure.
8. Loss of valid signal must produce and hold neutral throttle so the motor stops.
9. Separate commissioning prevents a steering fault and a throttle fault from appearing at the same time and makes unexpected motion easier to stop.
10. Examples include transmitter throttle limit, conservative controller mode, reduction gearing, low-power motor and battery choice, plus a short test lane.
11. A stationary made-safe vehicle prevents the first signal interruption from becoming an uncontrolled moving-vehicle test.
12. The prototype reveals required protocol and channels, servo torque and travel needs, controller and battery compatibility, mounting space, protected antenna route, service access, failsafe capability and safe speed limit.
