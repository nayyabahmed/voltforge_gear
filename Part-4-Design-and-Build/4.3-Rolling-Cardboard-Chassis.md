# Topic 4.3 - Rolling Cardboard Chassis

> **"A vehicle that cannot roll freely has no business receiving a motor."**

---

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- transfer the Topic 4.2 datums into a three-dimensional card chassis
- check squareness, axle parallelism, wheel runout and free rotation
- strengthen card through folds, depth and clear load paths
- compare unloaded and representative-load rolling behaviour
- conduct a repeatable drift test and interpret the spread of results
- approve or revise the chassis before any motor is added

---

# Before We Begin

You give the new cardboard buggy one careful push.

It rolls a metre, curves left and stops.

The front-left wheel spins freely in the air.

The rear-left wheel stops after half a turn.

One corner of the chassis lifts when the battery weights are added.

Another hard push makes it travel farther, but now nobody knows whether the chassis improved.

The motor box on the shelf seems to offer an easy answer.

It is the wrong answer.

A motor can overpower a small rub while turning wasted energy into heat.

It cannot make skewed axles parallel or a twisted chassis flat.

Topic 4.2 proved that the modules have a workable arrangement.
Topic 4.3 gives that arrangement a structure, axles and wheels.

This version has no powered drive and no radio control.

You are the temporary power source, which keeps resistance visible and the energy modest.

Your investigation follows six checks:

1. **Square** - did the packaging-board datums survive in three dimensions?
2. **Parallel** - do the axles and wheel planes point where the evidence says they should?
3. **Spin** - can each wheel and bush move without rubbing, wobbling or binding?
4. **Stiff** - does the chassis carry load through shape rather than a blanket of glue?
5. **Load** - what changes when representative component mass is added?
6. **Repeat** - does the same release produce a useful pattern of results?

# What the Rolling Chassis Must Prove

The chassis must:

- preserve the planned wheelbase and track
- keep the axles parallel
- keep all wheels clear of the structure
- roll with low resistance
- travel reasonably straight after a consistent push
- carry representative mass
- retain the packaging and service spaces from Topic 4.2
- provide interfaces that can be changed without rebuilding everything

It does not need:

- a motor
- an ESC
- a radio receiver
- final suspension
- final printed material
- high speed

Adding those now would make the experiment harder to interpret.

# A Chassis Is a Force Path

Imagine carrying a tray with a drink on it.

The tray connects the load to your hands while trying to keep its shape.

The **chassis** does the same job for the buggy.
It carries loads between the front module, rear module, battery, electronics and ground contacts.

Even card can teach this if it has:

- clear load paths
- enough depth to resist bending
- bracing against twist
- replaceable axle supports
- visible interfaces

A flat sheet of card bends easily.
Fold the edges upward and it becomes a shallow beam.

This happens because material farther from the middle of a beam contributes strongly to bending stiffness.
You do not need the full equation yet; you can prove the effect by testing two equal card strips.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Hold a sheet of paper flat between two books and press its middle gently.
> Fold a second sheet into a U-shaped channel and repeat.
> Which shape carries more load, even though both use one sheet?

The folded sheet has greater structural depth.
Shape can create stiffness without adding much mass.

> **[Figure 4.3.2, F5 force overlay: the same downward coin load acts on a flat card strip and a folded U-channel spanning two books. The flat strip has a large deflection curve; the channel has short force arrows travelling through its two raised sides. Equal material and span are labelled. Caption: Moving card away from the middle creates structural depth and a much stiffer load path. Alt text: Equal card strips under equal loads show large bending when flat and much less bending when folded into a channel.]**

# Choose a Card Chassis Form

Three useful forms are:

| Form | Strength | Access | Best use now |
|---|---|---|---|
| Folded tray | Good bending stiffness | Clear top access | Simple one-piece base |
| Twin rails with cross-members | Good modularity | Excellent between rails | Testing front/rear interfaces |
| Box beam spine | Strong for its mass | Limited central space | Narrow layouts |

For VoltForge, twin rails or a shallow folded tray are usually easiest to revise.

Do not glue every module permanently.
Use tabs, slots, tape loops, elastic bands or removable ties where possible.

# Transfer the Datums

Place the packaging board beside the new card.

Transfer:

- longitudinal centreline
- front axle line
- rear axle line
- left and right wheel reference lines
- module interface zones
- battery and electronics keep-clear areas

Check each value from the dimension table rather than tracing an uncertain edge.

Use one ruler and one unit system.
Millimetres are normally clearest for this project.

# Check Squareness with Diagonals

A rectangular picture frame can look rectangular while leaning sideways.

Measuring both diagonals reveals the error.

For a rectangular chassis datum box:

```text
If diagonal A = diagonal B, the corners are square within measurement error.
```

## Worked Example

**Question:** Is a 260 mm × 180 mm card chassis square enough for this prototype?

**Known measurements:**

- diagonal A = 316 mm
- diagonal B = 319 mm
- chosen card-build limit = 2 mm difference

**Calculation:**

```text
difference = |319 - 316|
           = 3 mm
```

**Meaning:**

The 3 mm difference is greater than the 2 mm limit.
The chassis datum box needs adjustment before axle supports are fixed.

The exact limit is a project decision.
The important point is to choose it before seeing the result.

> **[Figure 4.3.4, F6 measured drawing: rectangular chassis datum box with diagonal A and B, left and right wheelbase, width at both axle lines and a calculation panel showing 319 minus 316 equals 3 mm. One corner is shown being adjusted before axle supports are fixed. Caption: Diagonal comparison reveals a skewed rectangle that can look square by eye. Alt text: A dimensioned card chassis has unequal diagonals and is corrected before fitting axle supports.]**

# Axle Parallelism Controls Direction

Two train rails point in the same direction.
If one rail turns inward, the wheels bind.

**Parallelism** means two lines or surfaces remain the same distance apart.

For a simple rolling chassis, the front and rear axle lines should be parallel in top view.
Each axle should also sit square to the centreline unless the design intentionally uses a different geometry.

Check parallelism by measuring:

- left-side axle-to-axle distance
- right-side axle-to-axle distance
- both chassis diagonals
- axle-end distances from the centreline

| Check | Result suggesting a problem |
|---|---|
| Left wheelbase differs from right | One axle may be skewed |
| Axle ends differ from centreline | Track may be offset |
| Diagonals differ | Datum box may be out of square |
| Wheels touch base on one side | Axle support may be tilted or displaced |

Turn the chassis upside down and sight along both axles from several angles.

The visual check can reveal a large error.

Measurements decide whether the error is inside the limit chosen before the test.

A faulty setup shows one axle skewed and one bush tilted, with orange scrub and binding arrows. Caption: Straight rolling needs alignment in more than one view. Alt text: Top and rear views compare aligned axles with a skewed and tilted axle support.]**

# Use Simple Bearings or Bushes

A rotating skewer rubbed directly through card quickly enlarges the hole.

A short drinking straw lets the axle turn against a smoother, replaceable surface.
This is a simple **bush**: a sleeve supporting a rotating shaft.

For this prototype:

- axle: straight wooden skewer, pencil-sized rod or suitable reused shaft
- bush: straw or rolled paper tube
- retainer: card washer, bead, tape flag or small reusable spacer

The axle must rotate freely without excessive side-to-side movement.

Do not tighten a retainer against the wheel.
Leave a small visible end gap and adjust it during testing.

Real bearings may be used later with donor parts.
This topic is testing geometry, not bearing quality.

> **[Figure 4.3.6, F2 cross-section: a skewer axle passes through a short straw bush fixed in a removable card carrier. Labels show small running clearance, straight support, wheel end gap, card washer and covered axle tip. A side inset shows a pinched bush ovalising around the axle. Caption: A simple bush must support the axle without squeezing it. Alt text: Cross-section of a safe straw bush and wheel retainer shows clearance, end gap and a covered skewer tip.]**

# Wheel Quality Matters

A bent bicycle wheel moves sideways once per revolution.

**Runout** is the amount a rotating surface moves away from its intended path.

Check two kinds:

- radial runout: the wheel seems to bounce up and down
- lateral runout: the wheel wobbles side to side

Bottle tops, card discs or toy wheels can all work for a low-speed prototype if:

- their centres are marked carefully
- opposite wheels are similar in diameter
- the axle hole is close to the centre
- the wheel plane is close to square with the axle
- the retainer does not rub

Imperfect wheels are acceptable evidence.
Record whether drift comes from axle alignment or wheel wobble before changing both.

Use a fixed pencil or card pointer close to the rim without touching it.

Rotate the wheel slowly and watch the gap.

A changing gap makes runout visible without needing an expensive gauge.

# Rolling Resistance Steals Motion

Remember resistance from Topic 3.8.

**Rolling resistance** is the force opposing motion as wheels roll and their supports turn.

Sources in the card chassis include:

- axle rubbing on a tight bush
- wheel rubbing on card
- bent axle
- off-centre wheel
- misaligned bushes
- rough tyre surface
- chassis twisting under load

Use a gentle finger spin on each wheel before the full roll test.
One poor wheel can dominate the result.

# Toe, Camber and This Prototype

Topic 3.9 introduced **toe**, the direction wheels point when viewed from above.
Topic 3.10 introduced **camber**, the wheel tilt when viewed from the front.

For a first fixed-axle card model, aim for approximately:

- zero toe
- zero camber
- parallel wheel planes

The final donor steering and suspension may use intentional angles.
Do not imitate those angles inaccurately in a model whose job is straight rolling.

# Add Stiffness Without Hiding the Fault

When card bends, first identify the deformation:

| Deformation | What you see | Useful response |
|---|---|---|
| Bending | Middle sags | Add a fold, rail or deeper section |
| Twist | Opposite corners lift | Add cross-bracing or a torsion box |
| Buckling | Thin wall kinks sideways | Shorten unsupported length or add a flange |
| Crushing | Axle support collapses locally | Spread load with a doubler or replaceable block |

Do not cover the whole chassis with glue and another flat sheet.
That adds mass and makes the failure harder to inspect.

Use small **gussets**, triangular reinforcements joining two surfaces, at important corners.

# Preserve the Packaging Evidence

Place the Topic 4.2 component cards into the rolling chassis.

Check that strengthening folds have not stolen:

- battery width
- connector access
- servo-horn sweep
- motor cooling
- wire routes
- module-removal paths

The structure and packaging must agree.

>
> Build the base and verify its diagonals before making axle supports.
> The next stage is easier when the datum box is correct.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Shape Beats Layers

This no-equipment investigation lets you feel structural depth before building the chassis.

## Question

How much does folding change the load carried by one equal strip of card?

## Materials

- two equal card strips
- two books or small boxes
- identical coins or counters
- ruler

## Method

1. Bridge the flat strip across a measured gap.
2. Add coins one at a time at the centre.
3. Stop at a chosen deflection, such as 10 mm.
4. Record the coin count.
5. Fold the second equal strip into a U-channel.
6. Repeat over the same gap with the same load position.
7. Unload both strips and inspect permanent creases.

## Fair-test rule

Keep material, length, width, span, load position and deflection limit the same.

Change only the cross-sectional shape.

## Evidence table

| Strip | Shape | Coins at limit | Permanent damage |
|---|---|---:|---|
| A | Flat |  |  |
| B | Folded channel |  |  |

## What it proves

It demonstrates that structural depth changes bending behaviour.

It does not prove the final chassis will resist twist or local crushing.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - Find the Dragging Wheel

This activity combines bush friction and wheel runout into one diagnostic sequence.

## Question

Which wheel position contributes the clearest rolling fault?

## Materials

- rolling chassis or one axle test frame
- paper pull tab or identical small paper flags
- pencil or card pointer
- trial sheet

## Method

1. Raise the chassis so every wheel is clear.
2. Number the positions `FL`, `FR`, `RL` and `RR`.
3. Use the same paper tab or flag method to start each wheel.
4. Record approximate turns or stop time.
5. Hold a fixed pointer close to each rim.
6. Rotate slowly and observe radial gap change.
7. Move the pointer beside the rim and observe lateral wobble.
8. Inspect for wheel-to-card contact and retainer rubbing.
9. Repeat the weakest position before changing anything.

## Fair-test rule

Use the same start method, chassis orientation, load state and observation time.

Change only the wheel position being measured.

## Evidence table

| Position | Free-spin result | Radial runout | Lateral runout | Rub seen |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| FL |  | low/medium/high | low/medium/high | yes/no |
| FR |  | low/medium/high | low/medium/high | yes/no |
| RL |  | low/medium/high | low/medium/high | yes/no |
| RR |  | low/medium/high | low/medium/high | yes/no |

## What it proves

It identifies an outlying position and suggests whether the wheel, retainer or bush deserves inspection.

It does not isolate the cause until one suspected part is swapped or adjusted and the same test is repeated.

> **[Figure 4.3.8, F4 sequence strip: four panels show chassis raised, identical paper pull tab starting each wheel, fixed pointer checking up-and-down gap and side wobble, then an FL/FR/RL/RR results table with one outlier circled. Caption: Diagnose each rolling position before changing several parts at once. Alt text: A repeatable wheel test checks spin, radial runout, lateral runout and rubbing at four positions.]**

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Build a Release Lane

A heroic push creates a dramatic run but weak evidence.

This activity makes the input repeatable enough to compare revisions.

## Question

Does the chassis show a consistent drift pattern under the same release?

## Materials

- smooth, level indoor floor
- removable paper start and finish markers
- long paper centreline or low-tack tape approved by an adult
- short card ramp or straight release gate
- ruler or tape measure
- five trial markers

## Method

1. Check that the lane is clear of stairs, doors, feet and pets.
2. Mark a start line, centreline and finish line.
3. Place both starting wheels against the same release gate.
4. Use gravity from a shallow card ramp or remove the gate without pushing sideways.
5. Mark where the chassis centre crosses the finish line.
6. Record left drift as negative and right drift as positive.
7. Repeat at least five times without adjustment.
8. Find the median result and note the spread from smallest to largest.

## Fair-test rule

Keep the surface, start position, ramp height, chassis setup, load and finish distance the same.

Change only the trial number.

## What it proves

A cluster on one side suggests repeatable chassis bias.

Wide scatter on both sides suggests the release, loose joints or inconsistent wheel contact may be dominating.

> **[Figure 4.3.9, F6 measured drawing: top view of a straight release lane with shallow ramp, start line, centreline and finish line. Five finish markers form a tight cluster 35-45 mm left of centre; a small number line shows the median. Caption: Repeated results reveal whether drift is a pattern or a lucky push. Alt text: Five controlled chassis releases cluster left of a measured centreline, showing consistent drift.]**

# Topic Build - Rolling Cardboard Chassis 🛠️

Build a keepable human-powered chassis that carries the layout cards and produces measurable rolling evidence.

## Materials

- corrugated packaging card
- thin card for gussets and washers
- two straight skewers or reused rods
- two straws or rolled paper tubes
- four similar bottle tops, card wheels, spools or reusable toy wheels
- tape
- glue used sparingly
- ruler, square or folded-paper right angle
- pencil
- scissors
- coins or small sealed packets for test load
- string or elastic bands for removable module retention

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult or guardian what you plan to build before starting, and work with them nearby.
> Use scissors carefully; an adult must handle craft knives, hole-making tools or difficult cuts.
> Trim or cover sharp skewer points, and never leave them projecting beyond the wheels.
> Test on a clear floor away from stairs, roads, feet and pets.
> Do not install a battery or motor in this topic.

## Step 1 - Select the structure

Choose a folded tray or twin-rail form.

Write why it suits the approved packaging layout.

## Step 2 - Transfer the datums

Mark centreline, axle lines, wheel references and module zones.

## Step 3 - Form the chassis

Create folds, rails and cross-members.

Keep the structure removable or adjustable where uncertainty remains.

## Step 4 - Check the datum box

Measure left and right wheelbase, width at both axles and both diagonals.

Revise before continuing if the chosen limits are exceeded.

## Step 5 - Fit replaceable bushes

Align straw sections with the axle datums.

Tape them lightly first.
Pass the axle through and check freedom before final restraint.

## Step 6 - Prepare wheels

Mark centres carefully.

Fit wheels, add retainers and cover every sharp axle end.

## Step 7 - Inspect each wheel

Check spin, rub, lateral runout and radial runout.

Number the wheel positions `FL`, `FR`, `RL`, `RR`.

## Step 8 - Perform the unloaded roll test

Run at least five trials.

Record distance and sideways drift with trial numbers.

## Step 9 - Add representative load

Secure coins or small sealed packets at the heavy-component positions from Topic 4.2.

Repeat the test without changing the release method.

## Step 10 - Diagnose one main limitation

Choose the strongest evidence, such as:

- left drift
- short roll distance
- wheel rub
- chassis twist
- axle support movement

Change one main variable.

## Step 11 - Repeat the same test

Use the same surface, start, load and measurement method.

Compare before and after results.

## Step 12 - Restore module cards

Prove that the battery and major modules still fit and can be removed.

## Step 13 - Reflection

Label:

- the main load paths
- the axle datum interfaces
- the lowest-friction wheel
- the largest remaining error
- the revision that most improved performance

Keep the chassis.
It is the first VoltForge version that moves through the world.

# Modular Interfaces

| Interface | What must be controlled |
|---|---|
| Front axle carrier to chassis | Axle line, squareness, height and removability |
| Rear axle carrier to chassis | Axle line, parallelism, height and future drive space |
| Wheel to axle | Bore, retention, end gap and wheel plane |
| Chassis rail to cross-member | Datum position, right angle and gusset access |
| Load module to chassis | Location, restraint and quick removal |
| Packaging cards to structure | Preserved envelopes and service routes |

Use tape joints as temporary interfaces.
Draw a pencil witness line across each joint so movement becomes visible.

# Test Plan

Use the same three tests before and after a design revision.

Write every limit before seeing the result.

## Test 1 - Geometry and Free Rotation

**Question:** Does the chassis preserve its controlled geometry, and do all four positions rotate without rubbing or binding?

**Independent variable:** Wheel position `FL`, `FR`, `RL` or `RR`.

**Dependent variables:** wheelbase and track error, diagonal difference, spin result, runout class and observed contact.

**Control variables:** same datums, ruler, chassis state, start method and load condition.

**Procedure:** Measure both wheelbases, both track references, axle-end offsets and diagonals three times; then raise the chassis and run the same free-spin and pointer check at each position.

**Pass condition:** Geometry remains inside the limits written before testing, every wheel turns freely, no wheel touches the structure and no unexplained outlier remains.

## Test 2 - Unloaded and Loaded Roll

**Question:** What changes when representative component mass is added at the approved packaging positions?

**Independent variable:** Unloaded or representative-load condition.

**Dependent variables:** roll distance, finish-line drift, visible deflection, wheel contact and witness-line movement.

**Control variables:** same release lane, ramp height, start point, wheels, mass locations and trial count.

**Procedure:** Conduct at least five unloaded releases, add secured representative masses, then conduct at least five loaded releases without adjusting the chassis.

**Pass condition:** The loaded chassis remains intact, no wheel rub or joint movement appears, geometry stays within limits and the result spread is consistent enough to interpret.

## Test 3 - One-Change Revision

**Question:** Does one evidence-led revision improve the chosen rolling measure without breaking packaging or service requirements?

**Independent variable:** Before or after the single revision.

**Dependent variables:** selected fault measure, other roll results and pass/fail service checks.

**Control variables:** same surface, release method, load, wheels, measurement points, module cards and trial count.

**Procedure:** Choose one main limitation, change one main variable, repeat the relevant geometry, spin and roll tests, then restore the Topic 4.2 cards and service paths.

**Pass condition:** The target measure improves beyond ordinary trial variation, no new mandatory failure appears and every required module still fits and leaves by its approved route.

# Upgrade Path

Fixed card axle carriers -> adjustable slotted carriers -> low-power driven rear module -> donor suspension and drivetrain interfaces -> printed replaceable modules.

Preserve the same centreline and axle datum logic through every version.

# Stop Point Before Topic 4.4

Do not add a motor until:

- axle datums are transferred and visible
- left and right wheelbase agree within the chosen limit
- the chassis datum box passes its diagonal check
- all four wheels rotate without rubbing
- axle ends are safe and covered
- wheel runout has been inspected and recorded
- unloaded and loaded roll tests are repeatable
- sideways drift is inside the chosen limit or its cause is understood
- representative mass does not create wheel rub or joint movement
- module cards still fit and can be serviced
- one revision has been tested with the same method
- the Build Passport records the selected geometry
- a responsible adult approves the low-power plan

Only then consider a **low-voltage, low-power educational motor system** suitable for Topic 4.4.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Adding a Motor to Overcome Friction

Power hides resistance and turns a small alignment fault into heat or breakage.

Keep the first chassis human-powered until geometry and free rotation pass.

> **[Figure 4.3.11, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel shows a motor dragging a chassis with one tyre scrubbing sideways and an orange heat symbol at the bush; right panel shows the unpowered chassis raised while the tight wheel is identified and corrected. Caption: More power can hide resistance without removing it. Alt text: Powered chassis forces a rubbing wheel while the correct unpowered check finds the binding bush.]**

## Mistake 2 - Fixing Axle Supports Before Measuring Diagonals

Square the structure before locking the rolling geometry.

Use temporary tape and witness marks until the datum box passes.

> **[Figure 4.3.12, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel shows firmly glued straw bushes on a chassis with unequal diagonals; right panel shows lightly taped adjustable carriers, equal-diagonal measurement and only then final restraint. Caption: Measure and adjust the reference structure before locking the axle supports. Alt text: Glued bushes trap a skewed chassis while temporary supports allow squareness correction.]**

## Mistake 3 - Trusting One Heroic Push

One long run may come from a stronger or straighter push.

Use the same release and keep every trial.

> **[Figure 4.3.13, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel shows a hand pushing at different angles and circling the single longest result; right panel shows a shallow release ramp, five numbered finish marks, median and spread. Caption: A repeatable cluster tells more than the most exciting run. Alt text: Variable hand pushes produce selected evidence while a controlled ramp records all five trials.]**

## Mistake 4 - Changing Several Causes Together

Change one main variable so the result can be interpreted.

If wheel, bush and axle angle all change, improvement has no known cause.

> **[Figure 4.3.14, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel crosses out three simultaneous changes to wheel, bush and axle carrier; right panel changes only the bush, repeats the test and records the result before considering the next variable. Caption: One main change preserves the connection between cause and evidence. Alt text: Three simultaneous mechanical changes hide the cause while a single bush revision is tested clearly.]**

## Mistake 5 - Squeezing the Bush or Wheel Retainer

The axle needs a small running clearance.

Leave a visible end gap, check free rotation and cover every sharp axle tip.

> **[Figure 4.3.15, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel shows an ovalised straw bush, wheel clamped against card and exposed skewer tip; right panel shows a round aligned bush, small end gap, card washer and covered tip. Caption: Safe retention prevents escape without clamping the rotating parts. Alt text: Tight unsafe axle assembly binds, while correct clearance and a covered tip allow free rotation.]**

## Mistake 6 - Strengthening across Proven Space

A new rail may stiffen the chassis while trapping the battery or blocking the servo sweep.

Restore the Topic 4.2 cards after every structural revision.

> **[Figure 4.3.16, F3 right-versus-wrong pair: left panel shows a large brace crossing the dashed battery lift path and orange servo-horn envelope; right panel uses two small gussets outside the controlled packaging and service zones. Caption: Structural improvement fails if it steals space already approved for another module. Alt text: Oversized chassis brace blocks battery and servo movement while local gussets preserve both routes.]**

# Topic Summary 📝

- The rolling cardboard chassis isolates geometry and resistance before power.
- A chassis carries force between modules and ground contacts.
- Folds and structural depth make card much stiffer than a flat sheet.
- Shared datums connect the physical chassis to the packaging board.
- Equal diagonals are a useful squareness check.
- Parallel axles help wheels travel without sideways scrub.
- Simple bushes support rotating axles and can be replaced.
- Wheel runout may appear as bounce or side wobble.
- Rolling resistance can come from rubbing, tight bushes, bent axles or misalignment.
- First prototypes normally aim for zero toe and zero camber.
- Representative mass reveals sag, twist and new rubbing.
- Repeatable roll tests turn a toy-like model into engineering evidence.
- Service spaces must survive structural changes.
- A motor is considered only after the unpowered chassis passes.

# Answers 🔑

1. Without a motor, rubbing and misalignment remain visible instead of being forced through with extra power and converted into heat or damage.
2. Folding moves material away from the middle of the section, creating structural depth and a stiffer load path without simply adding layers.
3. The longitudinal centreline, front and rear axle lines, wheel references, module interface zones and controlled packaging spaces transfer from Topic 4.2.
4. Unequal diagonals can reveal that the rectangular datum box is skewed even when it looks square by eye.
5. Compare left and right wheelbase, both diagonals, axle-end offsets from the centreline and the axle directions in top and rear views.
6. A straw bush supports the rotating axle on a smoother, replaceable surface while maintaining a small running clearance.
7. Radial runout appears as the rim moving toward and away from the axle centre, like a bounce; lateral runout appears as side-to-side wobble.
8. Examples include a tight bush, wheel rubbing the chassis, a bent axle, an off-centre wheel, misaligned supports, a tight retainer or chassis twist under load.
9. Representative load reveals sag, twist, rubbing and joint movement that an empty chassis may hide.
10. A fixed start, surface, ramp height or release gate, chassis setup, load, finish distance and trial count make the drift test repeatable.
11. All trials show the typical result and its spread; the best run alone may be an unusually strong or straight release.
12. The gate needs controlled geometry, safe axle ends, freely rotating wheels, recorded runout, repeatable loaded and unloaded rolls, preserved service spaces, one tested revision and adult approval.
