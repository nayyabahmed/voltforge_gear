# Topic 4.1 - Build Strategy and Cost Plan

> **Your first successful buggy part is a decision that prevents the wrong part being bought.**

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- turn a buggy idea into a clear mission and measurable requirements
- divide the buggy into modules and record their important interfaces
- plan staged prototypes that answer one main question at a time
- decide what to buy, reuse and make using evidence and learning value
- build a cost range, spending record and evidence-based purchase gates
- create and test a reusable VoltForge Build Passport

# Before We Begin

The parts basket is open on a laptop.
A powerful motor is waiting beside four large tyres, and the battery looks right because its plug has the same colour as the ESC lead.
The total has already passed £100, but the buggy exists only as an idea.

Then someone asks three awkward questions.

Will the battery fit between the steering and drivetrain?

Can the chosen motor work safely with the ESC and battery?

Can the buggy be repaired without dismantling everything?

Nobody knows.

The problem is not a missing component.
It is a missing strategy.

Part 3 introduced the buggy's command, energy and movement systems.
Part 4 will combine them through a staircase of working prototypes.

Before spending the first pound, you will use six checks:

1. **Mission** - what useful buggy are we trying to build?
2. **Measure** - what evidence will show that it succeeds?
3. **Modules** - which groups of parts need clear boundaries?
4. **Stages** - what is the cheapest useful question to answer next?
5. **Money** - what is required, uncertain, optional or already owned?
6. **Gate** - what evidence must exist before we commit?

Your finished artifact will be the **VoltForge Build Passport**.
It will travel with the buggy from the full-size card layout in Topic 4.2 to Version 1 testing in Topic 4.10.

# Check 1 - Give the Buggy a Mission

Imagine packing for a trip.

You would choose different things for a rainy camping weekend and a sunny day at the beach.

The destination shapes the packing list.
A buggy also needs an intended destination before it needs a parts list.

A **mission** is a short statement of what the project should become, who will use it and where it will work.

Compare these two missions:

```text
Weak: Build a fast RC buggy.

Useful: Build a repairable RC buggy for supervised low-speed driving
on short grass and smooth paths, using reusable donor modules and
replaceable printed mounts.
```

The useful version does not choose every component.
It gives later decisions a direction.

Use these mission questions:

- Who will drive the buggy?
- Where will it be used?
- Which surfaces must it cross?
- What matters most: learning, repair, durability, terrain ability, low cost or speed?
- Where will controlled tests happen?
- Who will supervise powered work?
- Which tools, materials and suitable parts already exist?
- What is the maximum adult-approved budget?

Speed can become a later target.
It should not replace safe control, stopping, steering and repair.

# Check 2 - Turn Wishes into Evidence

Imagine two people judging a suitcase.

One says, “It is light.”

The other says, “It is heavy.”

A luggage scale ends the argument.
Engineering statements become useful when people can agree how to check them.

Three types of statement help us plan:

- a **requirement** says what the buggy must do or allow
- a **target** says the level we hope to reach or improve
- a **constraint** says which boundary must not be crossed

> **[F7 comparison array - Figure 4.1.2: three large cards sit above the same buggy silhouette. The MUST card shows a battery leaving through a clear route in under 60 seconds. The AIM card shows a ruler measuring a 20 mm ground-clearance target. The BOUNDARY card shows the total remaining below an adult-approved budget line. Each card includes a different evidence symbol: stopwatch, ruler or ledger. The reader should notice that requirement, target and constraint answer different questions.]**
>
> *Figure 4.1.2 - “Must”, “aim” and “stay within” create three different kinds of design decision.*
>
> *Alt text: Requirement, target and constraint cards show a timed battery removal, measured clearance and budget boundary.*

| Type | Buggy example | Planned evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Requirement | The disconnected battery must leave without removing the suspension | Demonstration |
| Requirement | The buggy must stop safely after loss of radio signal | Adult-led failsafe test in Topic 4.10 |
| Target | Aim for at least 20 mm ground clearance at stated ride height | Measurement |
| Target | Aim to remove the electronics tray in under 3 minutes | Timed service test |
| Constraint | Use only an adult-approved battery, charger and power system | Label and manual comparison |
| Constraint | Stay within the approved project budget | Cost review before purchase |

Words such as “strong”, “easy”, “good” and “fast” hide the judge.
Give the judge a test.

This wish:

```text
The battery must be easy to remove.
```

could become:

```text
R-04: With the power disconnected, the battery must be removable
in under 60 seconds using no more than one hand tool and without
removing either suspension module.
```

The label `R-04` gives the requirement an identity.
The **verification method** says how you will produce evidence that the requirement is met.

Useful methods include:

- inspection
- measurement
- demonstration
- test
- calculation

The link from a need to its feature and test is called **requirement traceability**.
It is like following a parcel with a tracking number: you can see where it came from, where it went and whether it arrived at a test.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Write “the chassis must be strong” on a scrap of paper.
> Imagine that a bumper cracks after one hard crash but the buggy still drives.
> Did the chassis pass or fail, and what missing information prevents a fair decision?

The statement has no load, event, damage limit or number of repetitions, so either answer could be defended.
Replace it with a testable statement such as completing ten low-speed passes over an agreed bump course without a chassis crack or loose module.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Mission-to-Measure Rescue

This activity needs only scrap paper and a pencil.

Write these six wish cards:

- fast
- strong
- easy to fix
- safe
- works outside
- not too expensive

Choose three cards.

For each card, add:

1. the person or situation that matters
2. the condition under which it will be judged
3. a measurement, demonstration or inspection
4. a pass boundary

Ask another person to judge the new statement without asking you what you meant.

If they cannot decide pass or fail, revise it again.

Do not invent a precise number just to fill the card.
Write `TO BE SET AFTER TOPIC 4.2 EVIDENCE` when the correct value depends on the full-size layout.

Record three rescued statements in the Build Passport.

# Check 3 - Divide the Buggy into Modules

A school bag is easier to use when lunch, books and stationery have separate places.

The contents still work together, but one item can be reached without emptying everything.
A **module** is a group of parts with one clear job and defined connections to the rest of the buggy.

A useful starting architecture is:

| Module | Main job | Possible contents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core chassis | Carry and connect other modules | Rails or plate, cross-members, mounting grid |
| Front module | Steer and support the front | Arms, hubs, links, servo mount, bumper |
| Rear module | Deliver drive and support the rear | Motor mount, gearbox or differential, arms, hubs |
| Battery module | Hold and protect stored energy | Tray, strap, stop and guard |
| Electronics module | Hold protected control parts | ESC, receiver, switch and cable guides |
| Body module | Protect parts and give access | Shell, mounts, guards and cooling openings |

This is a starting model, not a compulsory final shape.
A donor buggy may provide a complete front or rear assembly, letting the new design focus on the chassis that connects proven modules.

## Interfaces Are Handshakes

Two people can shake hands only when their hands meet in the same place.
A glove, a closed door or the wrong height can block the connection.

An **interface** is a boundary where modules connect, exchange something or need space from one another.

| Interface | Question to record |
| --- | --- |
| Mechanical | Which holes, shafts, fasteners or clips join the modules? |
| Spatial | What solid space, movement space and access space are needed? |
| Electrical | Which connector, polarity, voltage and current route are required? |
| Control | Which signal or receiver channel carries the command? |
| Service | What must move or be removed for inspection and replacement? |

In Topic 4.2, the spatial interface becomes a **packaging envelope**.
It is like a suitcase plus the room needed to open its lid and lift it by the handle.

A battery envelope includes:

- the battery body
- the strap and stop
- the connector and safe wire bend
- finger access
- its complete removal route
- clearance from steering, suspension and drivetrain movement

## Use Shared Datums

If everyone measures their height from a different step, the numbers cannot be compared.

They need the same floor.
Remember the datum from Topic 1.5: an agreed reference used for related measurements.

For the Topic 4.2 layout, begin with:

- one longitudinal centreline
- one front axle line
- one flat chassis reference surface

Wheelbase, track, module positions and left-right symmetry can then share references.

Use the smallest useful datum set.
Do not create a forest of reference lines that nobody can follow.

## Prefer Reversible Decisions

Tape can move.

A slotted card can adjust.

A bolted tray can be replaced.

These decisions are reversible because they preserve choices while evidence is still weak.
Glue, trapped fasteners and one-piece structures may become suitable later, but they are expensive ways to discover that the geometry was wrong.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - Module Handshake Map

Use scrap card, paper, a pencil, ruler and removable tape.

Cut or tear six rough module cards.

Label them:

- core
- front
- rear
- battery
- electronics
- body

Place the core in the centre.

Arrange the other cards around it.

Add one connection strip for every important handshake.

Use these marks:

```text
M = mechanical
S = spatial
E = electrical
C = control
R = service route
```

Remove the battery card.

Count how many unrelated cards or strips must move.

Repeat with the front, rear and electronics cards.

If everything must move, redraw a boundary or service route.

Mark every guessed value `UNKNOWN - NEEDS EVIDENCE`.

Keep the map for Topic 4.2.

> **☕ Good place to pause.** Put the module map beside your requirements. The next section turns them into a build staircase.

# Check 4 - Plan Stages That Teach

You would not learn juggling by throwing six balls on the first attempt.

You would begin with one object and add difficulty when the movement becomes controllable.

Part 4 uses the same idea.

A prototype, as used in Topic 1.9, is a version built to answer selected questions.

Each version should work at its own level.

| Version | Cheapest useful form | Main question | Evidence before moving on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Layout | Full-size paper and card | Does everything fit, move and remain accessible? | Approved packaging map |
| Rolling chassis | Card structure with free wheels | Does it align, carry mass and roll freely? | Roll, stiffness and access results |
| Low-power car | Simple protected low-voltage drive | Can the structure carry gentle drive force? | Controlled movement and stop evidence |
| RC prototype | Suitable radio, servo and low-power drive | Can steering and throttle be commissioned safely? | Adult-led direction, neutral and failsafe evidence |
| Donor interface plan | Measured real precision modules | Can the donor parts meet the new chassis? | Measurement sheets and fit templates |
| Printed chassis | Replaceable printed modules | Do the interfaces fit and remain serviceable? | Coupon, fit and unpowered movement evidence |
| Version 1 | Complete mechanical and electrical assembly | Does the buggy satisfy its requirements in use? | Topic 4.10 evidence pack |

Changing structure, drive, control, battery and suspension together creates a confusing result.
If the buggy improves or fails, which change caused it?

A good stage introduces one main difficulty while keeping the other conditions controlled.

## Put a Gate after Each Stage

A platform barrier opens after it checks a valid ticket.

A **stage gate** blocks the next commitment until agreed evidence exists.

A useful gate records:

- the question just tested
- the required evidence
- the pass conditions
- the remaining risks and unknowns
- the next action unlocked
- the purchases still locked
- the adult review needed for power, tools or meaningful spending

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> A £2 card model shows that the chosen battery blocks the steering link.
> Has the project lost £2, or has it saved the cost of a printed chassis and incompatible battery tray?

The model has produced valuable evidence while change is still cheap.
A failed early gate is a successful stop because it prevents the same mistake being built in expensive material.

> **[F3 right-versus-wrong pair - Figure 4.1.6: the left panel labels a failed card fit as WASTED £2 and shows the builder ordering the final chassis anyway. The right panel labels the same collision FOUND EARLY, places the order behind a locked gate and shows the card battery moving to a new position for pennies. A small cost-to-change curve rises sharply from paper to printed assembly. The reader should notice that early failure protects later money.]**
>
> *Figure 4.1.6 - Finding a collision in card is useful evidence, not a reason to ignore the gate.*
>
> *Alt text: A card-layout failure ignored before purchase is compared with the same failure stopping and cheaply redirecting the design.*

Example Topic 4.2 gate:

```text
QUESTION
Can the chosen modules fit, move and remain accessible at full size?

PASS WHEN
- wheelbase and track use named datums
- every component envelope fits
- the battery has a clear removal route
- steering and suspension envelopes do not collide
- wires have safe routes and bend space
- unknowns remain marked rather than guessed

UNLOCKS
- reused card for a rolling chassis

STILL LOCKED
- final printed chassis
- high-power system
- long or expensive prints
```

A gate is not punishment.
It protects the next version from an unsupported guess.

# Check 5 - Decide What to Buy, Reuse and Make

Making every gear does not automatically create more learning, and buying every bracket does not automatically create a better buggy.
Ask what the current stage is trying to prove.

## Buy or Reuse Precision

Proven parts are usually sensible for specialist precision or safety-critical functions.

Examples include:

- radio transmitter and receiver
- servo
- motor and ESC
- battery and suitable charger
- bearings and shafts
- gears and differential
- dampers
- connectors intended for the system

Suitability still needs checking.
“Already owned” does not mean “compatible”.

## Make Learning-Rich Structure

Useful design-and-make opportunities include:

- chassis rails or plates
- module adapters
- battery and electronics trays
- cable guides
- servo mounts
- guards and bumpers
- body mounts
- test coupons and fit templates

These parts reveal packaging, load path, service and interface decisions.

## Reuse before Rebuying

Card, stationery, safe fasteners and verified donor modules may answer the current question at no new cost.

Record a reused part twice:

- **cash cost to this project** - possibly £0
- **replacement value** - what losing or damaging it would matter

That prevents an expensive borrowed radio set from appearing “free”.

# Check 6 - Make the Money Visible

A supermarket receipt shows more than the price of the biggest ingredient.
A project estimate must also include the quiet costs that make the build possible.

A **bill of materials**, or **BOM**, is a controlled list of the items and quantities needed for a design.

Useful fields are:

| Field | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Item ID | Gives the item a stable identity |
| Description | Names the function without copying a shop title |
| Quantity | Prevents missing multiples |
| Stage needed | Stops early buying |
| Buy/reuse/make | Records the sourcing choice |
| Required/optional | Protects the minimum working build |
| Low and high estimate | Shows present uncertainty |
| Actual cost | Records what was really spent |
| Evidence gate | States what must be known before commitment |
| Reuse later? | Shows value beyond one prototype |

Early estimates should be honest ranges.
The range is wide while dimensions, compatibility and suppliers remain uncertain, then narrows as evidence improves.
Do not replace uncertainty with one very tidy guessed number.

Separate every item into:

- **required now** - needed for the approved current stage
- **required later** - likely needed but still locked
- **optional** - useful only after the base buggy works
- **not planned** - deliberately outside Version 1

Include:

- delivery
- small fasteners
- adapters and connectors
- consumables
- failed test coupons
- protective items
- plausible risk allowance

A **contingency** is money reserved for identified uncertainty.
It is not a hidden upgrade budget.

## Worked Example - Find the Planning Range

This is fictional arithmetic, not a current shopping list.

**Question:** What planning range should the team record, and does its upper safeguarded value fit a £170 limit?

**Known values:**

| Future item group | Low estimate | High estimate |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Radio and receiver | £30 | £40 |
| Steering servo | £10 | £15 |
| Donor precision module | £30 | £50 |
| Printing material allowance | £15 | £20 |
| Fasteners and adapters | £10 | £15 |
| Delivery | £5 | £10 |

**Low calculation:**

```text
£30 + £10 + £30 + £15 + £10 + £5 = £100
```

**High calculation:**

```text
£40 + £15 + £50 + £20 + £15 + £10 = £150
```

The early estimate is therefore `£100-£150`.

Suppose the adult approves a 10% contingency for the risks recorded in this example.

Use the cautious high estimate:

```text
contingency = £150 x 0.10
contingency = £15

safeguarded planning value = £150 + £15
safeguarded planning value = £165

headroom = £170 - £165
headroom = £5
```

**Meaning:**

The fictional plan fits the limit by only £5 at its cautious end.

That small headroom is a warning to reduce uncertainty before ordering, not an invitation to add an upgrade.

The 10% is an example chosen for this calculation.

Your adult-approved contingency should reflect your recorded risks rather than being copied automatically.

## Keep a Cost Ledger

An estimate looks forward.
A **cost ledger** records what actually happens.

| Date | Item ID | Action | Estimated | Actual | Difference | Evidence or lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| | | Bought, reused, returned or failed | | | | |

Do not erase a failed purchase.
Record whether it can be returned, reused or used as evidence for a changed interface.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - The Budget Gate Challenge

Make paper item cards from your draft BOM.

Write the stage, cost range and gate on each card.

Place all future purchases behind a paper gate.

Ask another person to act as gatekeeper.

Present these scenarios one at a time:

- the full-size battery envelope has not been made
- a reusable servo is found
- delivery rises by £6
- the donor module is 12 mm wider than assumed
- an optional body light looks exciting
- the rolling chassis fails its straight-roll test

The gatekeeper may answer only:

- `PROCEED`
- `REVISE`
- `STOP`

They must point to written evidence for the decision.

Update the BOM, estimate and gate after each scenario.

Keep mission and safety-critical scope unchanged.

# Topic Build - The VoltForge Build Passport 🛠️

The Build Passport is the first working artifact of Part 4.
It is small enough to use and strong enough to survive beside the project.

It should answer five questions at a glance:

1. What are we building?
2. What are we proving next?
3. What is still unknown?
4. What must not be bought yet?
5. What evidence opens the next gate?

## Materials

- one reused cereal box or piece of packaging card
- four sheets of reused or scrap paper
- pencil and ruler
- coloured pencils or pens
- removable tape or glue stick
- age-appropriate scissors
- paper clips, string or paper fasteners if already available

Nothing needs to be purchased for this build.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult what you plan to build and build with them nearby.
> Use age-appropriate scissors on a clear table and keep fingers away from the cutting path.
> Do not connect, charge or test real RC electronics during this project.
> The adult must approve the project budget and every later powered purchase.

> **🎬 Watch the build**
>
> - [NASA/JPL Engineering Design Process](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/edu/resources/image/engineering-design-process-flow-chart/): notice how a prototype is built, tested and improved rather than treated as the final answer.
> - BBC Bitesize KS3 Design and Technology: search “design specification” to see how needs become criteria.
> - BBC Bitesize KS3 Design and Technology: search “iterative design” to connect repeated modelling with improvement.

## Section 1 - Mission

Add:

- project name
- builder or team name
- start date
- adult supervisor
- one-sentence mission
- intended user and surfaces
- space for a future Version 1 photograph

## Section 2 - Measures

Create six to ten requirement rows.

For each, record:

- ID
- statement
- reason
- verification method
- pass condition or evidence needed to set it
- linked module
- status

Add up to three targets and three constraints.

## Section 3 - Modules and Interfaces

Attach the Module Handshake Map.

For each module, record:

- job
- inputs and outputs
- mechanical handshake
- spatial envelope
- electrical or control route where relevant
- service route
- known values
- unknown values
- evidence needed next

Mark the centreline, front axle line and chassis reference surface.

## Section 4 - Build Staircase

Give each Part 4 version one card.

Every card states:

- question
- cheapest useful form
- one main new difficulty
- required evidence
- pass condition
- next action unlocked

Use removable tape so a stage can be revised without destroying the history.

## Section 5 - Buy, Reuse, Make or Wait

Sort every known item into four columns.

Add the reason and evidence gate to each item.

Keep optional upgrades physically separate from Version 1 requirements.

## Section 6 - Money

Add:

- staged BOM
- low and high cost estimates
- delivery and small-item allowance
- adult-approved contingency
- maximum budget
- current headroom
- blank cost-ledger rows

Record £0 when nothing was bought.

Blank space is not the same as zero.

## Section 7 - Risks, Unknowns and Decisions

A **risk** is an uncertain event or condition that could affect the project.

Record at least five risks.

For each, note:

- possible cause
- effect
- early warning
- reduction action
- owner
- review point

Keep unknowns separate from risks.

“Battery width not measured” is an unknown.

“Battery may not fit after measurement” is a risk.

Add a **decision log** with:

- decision ID and date
- options considered
- evidence used
- choice and reason
- affected requirements or interfaces
- evidence that would trigger review

## Section 8 - Gates and Evidence Pocket

Make one gate card for each Part 4 transition.

Add a small paper pocket for:

- measurement slips
- failed ideas
- revised requirement cards
- supplier checks
- photographs printed later
- surprise-cost cards

Never destroy an old card to make the plan look perfect.
Cross it out once, date the change and keep it in the pocket.

## Review the Passport

Give the passport to someone who did not make it.

Without extra explanation, ask them:

- What is the mission?
- Which question comes next?
- Which purchase remains locked?
- Which number is still unknown?
- What would cause the plan to stop?

Revise any section that cannot answer its question.

## Reflection

Write:

- the decision that may save the most money
- the requirement that became clearer after Activity 1
- the module boundary that needs most evidence
- the most tempting locked purchase
- the first evidence Topic 4.2 must produce

Label what the passport cannot prove.

It cannot prove real fit, strength, radio behaviour or driving performance.
It controls when and how those things will be tested.

Keep the passport beside your engineering notebook.
It belongs on the showcase shelf with the finished buggy because it explains how Version 1 was earned.

# Learning Goal

The goal is to make the next useful experiment obvious before money or complex materials are committed.

The strategy succeeds when it:

- connects the mission to testable evidence
- divides the buggy into replaceable modules
- exposes interfaces and unknowns
- introduces one main difficulty per version
- separates required, uncertain and optional spending
- changes when evidence changes
- blocks unsupported purchases

# Test Plan

Planning decisions can be tested.

These tests judge the passport, not the final buggy.

## Test 1 - Requirement Judge

**Question:** Can another person decide whether each requirement passes without asking what it means?

**Independent variable:** requirement card selected.

**Dependent variables:** whether the judge can identify the evidence and make a pass/fail decision.

**Control variables:** same judge instructions, mission, card format and allowed evidence types.

**Procedure:** shuffle the requirement cards; give them to a reviewer; ask for the evidence method and pass boundary; record every question they must ask.

**Pass condition:** every card identifies a suitable method, and no critical requirement needs the author's private explanation.

## Test 2 - Module Independence

**Question:** Can a named module leave the card architecture without disturbing unrelated modules?

**Independent variable:** module selected for removal.

**Dependent variable:** number of unrelated modules and interfaces that must move or disconnect.

**Control variables:** same layout, datums, module definitions and service rules.

**Procedure:** remove the battery, electronics, front and rear cards one at a time; record every unrelated card or route disturbed.

**Pass condition:** each module has a planned route that does not require an unrelated suspension or drivetrain module to be dismantled.

## Test 3 - Budget Shock and Gate

**Question:** Does the strategy respond safely to a plausible cost or evidence change?

**Independent variable:** one scenario card.

**Dependent variables:** revised estimate, remaining headroom and gate decision.

**Control variables:** mission, safety scope, budget limit, other estimates and gate criteria.

**Procedure:** apply at least six different scenario cards one at a time; update the BOM and ledger; ask the gatekeeper for `PROCEED`, `REVISE` or `STOP`; return to the baseline before the next scenario.

**Pass condition:** missing critical evidence produces `REVISE` or `STOP`, optional items never displace safety-critical scope, and every changed total remains traceable.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Making “Fast” the Mission

Speed alone does not describe control, surface, repair, durability or learning.

Write the intended use first and treat speed as a later target.

## Mistake 2 - Calling a Wish a Requirement

“Strong” and “easy” cannot produce consistent evidence on their own.

Name the condition, method and pass boundary.

## Mistake 3 - Freezing Exact Parts before Evidence

A product page is not proof that the part fits the project.

Record the needed function and interface, then unlock selection after measurement and compatibility checks.

Several new difficulties hide the cause of success or failure.

Use stages that add one main capability and protect a baseline.

> **[F3 right-versus-wrong pair - Figure 4.1.14: the wrong panel jumps from sketch to a new chassis, suspension, motor, battery and radio with five fault arrows pointing to one failed drive. Delivery, fasteners, adapters, consumables and replacement tests can change the decision.

Contingency protects the agreed scope from recorded uncertainty.

# Topic Summary 📝

- A mission describes the intended buggy, user, environment and priorities.
- Requirements must be supported by planned evidence and pass boundaries.
- Targets express desired performance; constraints define boundaries.
- Requirement traceability follows a need into a feature and test.
- Modules group parts by job, while interfaces describe their handshakes.
- Shared datums make measurements comparable.
- Reversible decisions keep early changes cheap.
- Each prototype should answer one main question.
- Stage gates lock commitments until evidence exists.
- Buy or reuse specialist precision and make learning-rich structure.
- A staged BOM separates current, later, optional and excluded items.
- Early cost estimates are ranges that narrow as uncertainty reduces.
- Contingency protects against uncertainty; it is not an upgrade fund.
- The Build Passport records the plan, evidence, changes and purchase gates.
- Topic 4.1 succeeds when the next useful experiment is clear and premature spending is stopped.

# Answers 🔑

1. The mission states who the buggy is for, where it will work and what matters. Those decisions guide useful parts and tests, while a shopping list made first can collect incompatible items.
2. A requirement is mandatory and verifiable. A target is a desired performance level that may be traded against another target. A constraint is a boundary the project must stay within.
3. One valid answer is: “With power disconnected, the battery must be removable in under 60 seconds, using no more than one hand tool and without removing either suspension module.” Other answers earn credit if the condition, method and pass boundary are clear.
4. It follows a need into the design feature, decision and verification evidence that address it.
5. Mechanical, spatial, electrical, control and service interfaces.
6. The part also needs room to move, connect, cool, accept a tool or hand, and leave through a service route.
7. If several important variables change together, the result cannot be traced confidently to one cause. One main new difficulty makes success and failure easier to diagnose.
8. It should return `REVISE` or `STOP`, keep the purchase locked and identify the evidence needed before review.
9. Bought or reused examples include a radio set, servo, motor, ESC, battery system, bearings, gears or differential. Made examples include a chassis rail, tray, mount, guard, cable guide or test coupon. Any suitable two of each earn the answer.
10. Dimensions, compatibility, suppliers and risks are still uncertain early in the project. A range shows that uncertainty and can narrow as evidence improves.
11. `£150 × 0.10 = £15`, then `£150 + £15 = £165`. The safeguarded planning value is `£165`.
12. Mission, measure, modules, stages, money and gate.
