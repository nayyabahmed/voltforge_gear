# Topic 4.2 - Paper and Cardboard Layout

> **A component does not fit until it can enter, work, cool and leave.**

---

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- make full-size component models from reliable dimensions
- arrange modules from shared datums
- distinguish footprint, movement and service envelopes
- test wire routes, battery access and cooling space
- compare two layouts using the same evidence
- approve a packaging board for the rolling cardboard chassis

# Before We Begin

The workshop table looks ready for a celebration.
Every paper component fits inside the drawn chassis.

Then the imagined buggy tries to move.
The left tyre sweeps across the battery lead.
The servo horn reaches the receiver box.
The battery fits perfectly but cannot be lifted past the top brace.

A second builder tries to copy the arrangement and puts the motor 12 mm farther back.

Nothing was too large.
The layout failed because it answered only one question: “Can the rectangles sit here?”

Topic 4.1 gave your buggy a mission, requirements, modules, stages and spending gates.

Now you will make its first full-size physical form without buying or cutting valuable material.

Your mission is to prove six checks:

1. **Scale** - are the models genuinely full size?
2. **Datum** - can every important position be measured from the same references?
3. **Envelope** - is there space for the part, its movement and its removal?
4. **Route** - can wires, air, tools and hands reach their destinations safely?
5. **Balance** - are the heavy modules sensibly distributed?
6. **Prove** - can another person repeat the checks and reach the same conclusion?

# Check 1 - Work at Full Scale

Imagine drawing your hand half size and using the drawing to choose a glove.

The proportions may look correct, but the decision is useless.

For this topic, use **full scale**, written as `1:1`.

One millimetre on the model represents one millimetre on the intended buggy.

A small sketch can explore an idea.

It cannot prove finger access, connector clearance, wire bending or battery removal.

## Give Every Dimension a Source

A number is trustworthy only when you know where it came from.

Use one of these labels on every component model:

| Source label | Meaning | How to treat it |
|---|---|---|
| `REAL` | Measured from the actual component | Recheck important interfaces before manufacture |
| `DRAWING` | Taken from an official dimensional drawing | Confirm the exact model and drawing revision |
| `ESTIMATE` | Chosen because the real value is not yet known | Keep visible and do not freeze it silently |

Do not scale dimensions from an angled marketplace photograph.

Perspective makes nearer objects look larger.

An unverified picture can guide a question, but it cannot control a cut.

## Model the Useful Shape

Your card model does not need every cosmetic curve.

It does need the maximum body size and every important interface.

For a battery model, show:

- length, width and height
- cable exit
- connector body
- strap or retainer zone
- intended removal direction

For a servo model, show:

- case size
- mounting lugs
- output shaft position
- horn sweep
- plug and wire exit

For a motor or gearbox model, show:

- mounting face
- shaft or drive output
- cable exits
- moving region
- cooling region

## Controlled Does Not Mean Unchangeable

A **provisional dimension** is a value chosen for the present test but still open to evidence.

A **controlled dimension** is a recorded value that must not change silently.

Controlled does not mean permanent.

It means a change needs a reason, a revision and updated evidence.

Use a table like this:

| Dimension | Current value | Source | Status | Evidence still needed |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Wheelbase | 260 mm | Build Passport target | Provisional | Rolling test and donor measurements |
| Battery width | 48 mm | Actual pack | Controlled | Recheck connector side |
| Servo case height | 38 mm | Official drawing | Controlled | Confirm horn and screw access |
| Rear-module width | 72 mm | Photo estimate | Provisional | Measure donor assembly |

If a value is uncertain, expose the uncertainty.

Do not turn a guess into a fact by printing it neatly.

# Check 2 - Measure from Shared Datums

When a class measures everyone’s height, the floor is the shared starting point.

If one person measures from the floor and another from a chair, their results cannot be compared.

Engineers call the agreed reference a **datum**.

Draw three main datums on the packaging board:

1. the longitudinal centreline
2. the front axle line
3. the rear axle line

The distance between the front and rear axle lines is the **wheelbase**.

The distance across the vehicle between corresponding left and right wheel reference positions is the **track width**.

The exact wheel reference used for track must be written down.

Different manufacturers may quote track in different ways.

For your paper experiment, consistency matters more than pretending there is one universal convention.

> **[F6 measured drawing - Figure 4.2.3: top view of a blank packaging board with a bold centreline, front and rear axle datums, left and right wheel reference positions, front arrow, wheelbase dimension and track-width dimension. A battery card is located by two dimensions from the datums; no dimension starts from the rough card edge.]**
>
> *Figure 4.2.3 - Shared datums let another builder reproduce the same arrangement even if the board edge is trimmed.*
>
> *Alt text: Dimensioned top view shows a buggy layout measured from centreline and axle references instead of outer card edges.*

## Why Card Edges Are Poor References

Packaging card may not be square.

Its edge may be damaged or trimmed later.

If every component is positioned from a different edge, small errors accumulate.

This is a **tolerance stack-up**: several small differences combine into one larger mismatch.

Measure important locations from the fewest useful datums.

A component record might say:

```text
Module: battery model B1
Front face: 92 mm behind front axle datum
Centre: 18 mm left of centreline
Bottom face: chassis datum + 4 mm
Rotation: 0° to centreline
Source: REAL body; ESTIMATE connector bend
Revision: B1-r2
```

That record tells another builder where the model belongs.

“Near the middle” does not.

## Choose Wheelbase and Track Provisionally

You need axle and wheel references before you can test steering space.

You do not yet have enough evidence to freeze the final vehicle geometry.

Choose provisional values using:

- the mission from Topic 4.1
- intended wheel diameter
- the largest known modules
- steering and suspension envelopes from Topics 3.9 and 3.10
- donor-part information already available
- the size of the build area

Write the values on the board.

Do not copy a different buggy’s wheelbase simply because it looks exciting.

Topic 4.3 will test whether your provisional geometry rolls and tracks properly.

# Check 3 - Draw the Space Around Every Part

A suitcase may hold a pair of shoes.

It also needs space for the lid to close and for your hand to lift the shoes out.

The solid object is only part of the packing problem.

A **footprint** is the area occupied by a stationary part in one view.

A **packaging envelope** includes the useful space around that part.

Use three envelope types:

| Envelope | What it represents | Buggy example |
|---|---|---|
| Footprint or solid envelope | Space occupied all the time | Battery case |
| Movement envelope | Every position occupied during operation | Steering tyre sweep |
| Service envelope | Space needed to install, disconnect, remove or inspect | Battery lift path |

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Put your hand flat on scrap paper and trace around it.
> Now trace the region your hand uses while picking up a small box and lifting it clear.
> Which outline should control the design of a storage opening?

The larger working outline should control it because the task includes approach, grip and removal, not only the final hand position.

>
> *Figure 4.2.4 - Design for the complete task, not only the object’s resting position.*
>

## Treat Height as Real

A top view can hide a tall collision.

Create folded component boxes or a side-view board.

Show at least:

- top cover or body height
- plug height above the receiver
- servo horn and screw access
- battery lift height
- suspension travel above and below ride height
- motor and ESC cooling space

Two shapes can share the same top-view area and still collide at different heights.

## Trace Movement at Its Extremes

The front tyres do not stay inside neat rectangles.

They turn, rise and fall.

Trace or model:

- full left steering
- straight ahead
- full right steering
- highest planned wheel position
- lowest planned wheel position
- servo horn at both endpoints
- linkage movement
- driveshaft or gearbox movement where applicable

Use split pins to make simple pivots.

Use dotted overlays when a moving model is impractical.

The movement envelope must clear fixed components, wires, body supports and neighbouring tyres.

> **[F5 motion overlay - Figure 4.2.5: top and front views of one wheel shown at straight, full-left, full-right, highest and lowest positions. A translucent orange volume joins the extremes. Nearby battery, chassis edge and wire route are labelled with their minimum gaps.]**
>
> *Figure 4.2.5 - The wheel envelope combines steering and suspension extremes; a single straight-ahead outline is not enough.*
>
> *Alt text: Multiple wheel positions form a swept volume that must clear the battery, chassis and wiring.*

## Do Not Share Movement Space Casually

Two envelopes may appear able to share space because one part moves only occasionally.

That decision is risky unless the motions are mechanically prevented from happening together.

For a first buggy, keep moving envelopes separate where practical.

Record every uncertain gap for later measurement.

Do not hide it by moving a card during the test.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - The Envelope Reveal

This activity needs only paper, a pencil and one small household object.

## Question

How much larger is a useful packaging envelope than a simple footprint?

## Materials

- scrap paper
- pencil
- ruler
- a small box, eraser or glue stick
- a piece of string

## Method

1. Place the object on the paper.
2. Trace its footprint.
3. Mark where a connector might leave using a short piece of string.
4. Trace the space needed to grip and lift the object.
5. Add a pretend screwdriver approach from above.
6. Label solid, movement and service envelopes.
7. Ask another person to identify each envelope without your help.

## Evidence table

| Evidence | Footprint only | Complete envelope |
|---|---|---|
| Object rests in place | Yes/No | Yes/No |
| String bends without a sharp kink | Yes/No | Yes/No |
| Fingers can grip it | Yes/No | Yes/No |
| Object can be removed | Yes/No | Yes/No |
| Tool can approach | Yes/No | Yes/No |

## Fair-test rule

Keep the same object, paper, approach direction and person for both outlines.

Change only whether the extra working spaces are included.

## What the activity proves

It proves that installed size and usable space are different.

It does not prove the final clearance needed by a real RC component.

# Check 4 - Protect Every Route

A corridor is not useful if a door opens across it and blocks the way.

Buggy routes also need continuous, protected space.

The important routes are:

- battery insertion and removal
- connector disconnection
- tool approach
- wire and antenna paths
- cooling-air entry and exit
- module removal

## Prove Battery Removal

The battery must do more than fit.

Show four stages:

1. installed and restrained
2. connector accessible
3. restraint released
4. battery fully removed

The motion must not pull on a cable.

Hold connectors by their housings, not their wires.

If removing the battery requires dismantling the steering or rear drivetrain module, reconsider the layout.

> **[F4 sequence strip - Figure 4.2.7: four panels of a card battery being disconnected by its connector housing, strap released, lifted along a dashed service path and removed without disturbing the servo or rear module. A wrong inset shows a hand pulling the cable.]**
>
> *Figure 4.2.7 - A battery passes the service check only when connection, restraint and removal are all possible.*
>
> *Alt text: Four-step battery removal sequence keeps fingers on the connector housing and avoids other modules.*

## Route Wires as Physical Parts

Lines drawn on paper have no thickness and no stiffness.

Use string, wool or paper strips to represent wires.

Include:

- connector body length
- a gentle bend after the connector
- enough slack for module removal
- restraint points
- clearance from gears, shafts, tyres and steering links
- access for unplugging

Do not pull string tightly between connectors.

Real cables need some slack.

Do not leave large loose loops either.

They can reach moving mechanisms.

Label each route and its main risk:

| Route | From | To | Protect it from |
|---|---|---|---|
| Battery power | Battery | ESC | sharp edges, strain and moving parts |
| Motor power | ESC | Motor | heat, drivetrain and snagging |
| Throttle signal | ESC | Receiver | tension, wrong port and damage |
| Steering signal | Servo | Receiver | steering movement and trapped plugs |
| Antenna | Receiver | protected position | crushing, sharp bends and moving parts |

The exact wiring, antenna and connector rules depend on the selected equipment manuals.

Topic 4.2 protects physical routes; it does not invent model-specific installation rules.

## Keep Cooling Possible

The motor and ESC release unwanted heat while working.

Do not trap them in sealed cardboard pockets.

Protect:

- open space around cooling surfaces
- an entry path for cooler air
- an exit path for warmer air
- access for temperature inspection
- separation from the battery where practical
- cable routes that do not cover fans or heat sinks

This paper board cannot prove a final operating temperature.

It can reveal a layout that makes cooling impossible before power is ever connected.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - The Datum Rebuild Race

This activity compares vague placement with reproducible placement.

## Question

Do shared datums help another person rebuild a layout more accurately?

## Materials

- one sheet of scrap card
- five small paper component cards
- pencil
- ruler
- removable tape

## Method A - Edge and memory

1. Place five cards by eye.
2. Write vague notes such as “battery near the middle”.
3. Remove the cards.
4. Ask a second person to rebuild the layout.

1. Draw a centreline and two axle lines.

# Check 5 - Estimate the Balance

Balance a ruler on one finger.

Place a heavy coin near one end and a paper clip near the other.

The heavy item usually controls where your finger must move.

The **centre of mass** is the balance point of the system’s mass.

A paper layout cannot predict the finished buggy perfectly.

It can compare whether one arrangement is much more front-heavy, rear-heavy or one-sided than another.

## Use a Weighted Position

For a simple fore-aft estimate:

```text
centre position = sum of (mass × position) ÷ total mass
```

Choose one datum as position zero.

Use the same datum for every component.

## Worked Example - Compare One Layout

**Question:** Where is the estimated fore-aft centre of three heavy modules?

**Known values:**

- battery: 200 g at 150 mm behind the front axle
- motor module: 120 g at 230 mm
- steering servo: 50 g at 45 mm

**Rule:**

```text
x = Σ(m × x) ÷ Σm
```

**Calculation:**

```text
weighted total = (200 × 150) + (120 × 230) + (50 × 45)
               = 30,000 + 27,600 + 2,250
               = 59,850 g·mm

total mass = 200 + 120 + 50
           = 370 g

x = 59,850 ÷ 370
  = 161.8 mm
```

**Meaning:**

The estimated centre of those three modules is about 162 mm behind the front axle.

The chassis, wheels, fasteners and wires are missing, so this is not the final buggy centre of mass.

Use the same method for Layout A and Layout B.

The comparison is more trustworthy than either number alone.

If exact masses are unknown, place equal groups of coins on the card models.

Balance the board over a pencil and record the approximate balance line.

Label this evidence as an estimate.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Layout A puts a 200 g battery 20 mm farther back.
> Layout B moves a 20 g receiver 100 mm farther forward.
> Which change has the larger effect on the fore-aft balance moment?

The battery change has the larger effect: `200 × 20 = 4,000 g·mm`, while the receiver change gives `20 × 100 = 2,000 g·mm`.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - The Layout Showdown

You will compare two genuinely different arrangements.

## Question

Which layout best satisfies the mission without failing a mandatory requirement?

## Prepare Layout A and Layout B

Keep the same:

- component models
- wheelbase and track
- envelope rules
- test order
- scoring scale

Change the position of at least two modules for a clear reason.

For example, compare a central battery with a side battery.

Do not make a tiny cosmetic variation.

## Mandatory gates

A layout fails immediately if:

- two solid envelopes overlap
- a tyre or linkage reaches a wire route
- the battery cannot be disconnected and removed
- a required connector or fastener is inaccessible
- cooling space is blocked
- an important dimension has no source or uncertainty label

## Weighted comparison

Score only layouts that pass every mandatory gate.

| Criterion | Weight | Layout A score 1-5 | Layout B score 1-5 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Battery service | 5 |  |  |
| Steering and suspension clearance | 5 |  |  |
| Wire protection | 4 |  |  |
| Module independence | 4 |  |  |
| Balance estimate | 3 |  |  |
| Cooling route | 3 |  |  |
| Compact arrangement | 2 |  |  |

Multiply each score by its weight.

Add the weighted results.

Write one sentence explaining every score below 4.

## Fair-test rule

Change the arrangement, not the models or scoring rules.

Perform the tests in the same order.

## What the activity proves

It provides evidence for choosing one layout.

It does not prove the chassis is stiff or the wheels will roll straight.

# Check 6 - Turn the Layout into Proof

Evidence must survive after the cards are cleared away.

Your packaging board should allow another person to answer:

- what was tested?
- what stayed the same?
- what changed?
- what passed?
- what failed?
- what was revised?
- what remains uncertain?

## Use Revision IDs

Give each important model and layout a short identity.

For example:

```text
Battery model: B1-r2
Front module: F1-r1
Layout: L2-r3
Packaging board: PB-4.2-r3
```

If the battery envelope changes, update its revision.

If that change moves another module, update the layout and board revisions too.

This prevents an old photo from being mistaken for current evidence.

## Make Evidence Visible

Record:

- a direct overhead photograph with a ruler in view
- one side-view photograph or drawing
- dimension and source table
- pass/fail test sheet
- collision marks before revision
- decision-matrix result
- balance estimate
- open-issues list
- adult review at the gate

Failure marks belong in the evidence pack.

They show what the prototype taught you.

# Topic Build - Full-Size Packaging Board 🛠️

Build the keepable layout authority for Topic 4.3.

It should be useful on the workshop table, not decorative on a wall.

## Learning goal

Prove that the planned modules can occupy, move through and leave their spaces before creating a rolling structure.

## Materials

- reused packaging card
- scrap paper
- pencil and ruler
- scissors
- removable tape or reusable adhesive
- string or wool
- split pins, paper fasteners or thread
- coins or washers for optional mass models
- coloured pencils
- phone or camera for evidence, if available

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult or guardian what you plan to build before starting, and work with them nearby.
> Use scissors carefully; an adult must handle any craft knife or difficult cut.
> Keep real batteries disconnected and keep loose metal objects away from their terminals.
> Keep the floor clear of card scraps, string and tools.

## Before building

Open the eight-section Build Passport from Topic 4.1.

Copy the current mission, key requirements, module list and purchase gate onto a small board label.

Do not begin from a blank imagination.

Begin from the decisions already recorded.

## Build stage 1 - Prepare the authority board

Cut a board larger than the provisional buggy.

Draw:

- front direction
- longitudinal centreline
- front axle datum
- rear axle datum
- left and right wheel references
- scale `FULL SIZE 1:1`
- packaging-board revision ID

Check the wheelbase dimension twice.

Check the track reference definition twice.

## Build stage 2 - Make the module set

Create full-size models for every known module:

- battery
- steering servo
- receiver
- ESC
- motor
- gearbox or rear donor module
- front steering module
- switch
- major connectors

Label dimensions, datum faces, cable exits, movement features and evidence sources.

Use `ESTIMATE` where evidence is missing.

## Build stage 3 - Add envelope overlays

Use a consistent line key:

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| solid outline | physical body |
| dotted outline | movement envelope |
| dashed outline | service envelope |
| string or strip | wire route |
| orange circle | collision or uncertainty |
| green tick | demonstrated pass |

Do not rely on colour alone.

The line styles must still work in greyscale and for colour-blind readers.

## Build stage 4 - Create Layout A

Arrange every module.

Trace each controlled position from the datums.

Add wire routes, removal paths and cooling gaps.

Test the movement extremes.

Mark every problem before changing anything.

Photograph or trace Layout A.

## Build stage 5 - Create Layout B

Move at least two modules for a stated engineering reason.

Repeat the same tests.

Use the same envelopes, datums and scoring rules.

Photograph or trace Layout B.

## Build stage 6 - Select and revise

Reject any layout that fails a mandatory gate.

Complete the weighted matrix for the survivors.

Choose the stronger evidence from both layouts.

Create one approved revision rather than leaving a confusing pile of alternatives.

## Build stage 7 - Run the proof demonstrations

Ask another person to:

1. move steering and suspension models through their extremes
2. disconnect, release, remove and replace the battery model
3. reach planned fasteners with a pencil used as a pretend screwdriver
4. follow every string route without touching a moving envelope
5. rebuild at least three module positions from the recorded dimensions

Do not coach them through unclear instructions.

Record where they pause, guess or disturb another module.

## Build stage 8 - Update the Build Passport

Record:

- approved board revision
- chosen wheelbase and track status
- controlled interfaces
- provisional dimensions
- test evidence
- rejected layout and reason
- largest cheap failure discovered
- questions for Topic 4.3
- purchase decision: buy, wait or gather evidence

## Reflection

Write on the back of the board:

- “The largest problem we discovered before cutting was...”
- “The layout changed because...”
- “The dimension we still do not trust is...”
- “Topic 4.3 must prove...”

Keep the board flat or store a direct overhead image with a ruler visible.

This is the first full-size physical form of your final buggy.

# Learning Goal

The goal is to find every fit, clearance and balance problem while the whole buggy is still made of paper.

The layout succeeds when it:

- shows every module at full scale, measured from shared datums
- reserves the space each part needs to move, cool and be reached
- protects the routes wires and linkages will take
- gives a balance estimate that later topics can test against
- carries a revision ID, so a later change can be traced

A layout that is only tidy has not done its job. It has to be able to prove something wrong.

# Cheapest Valid Prototype

A full-size paper base with labelled flat cards can test top-view fit and datum placement.

Folded boxes, movable overlays and a side view are the stronger version.

They reveal height, movement and service problems that flat cards can miss.

# Modular Interfaces

The board must record these interfaces:

| Interface | Evidence required now |
|---|---|
| Front module to chassis | datum, attachment zone, movement space and tool access |
| Rear module to chassis | datum, width, drive route and removal direction |
| Battery to tray | body, connector, restraint and complete removal envelope |
| Servo to steering | output position, horn sweep, linkage route and endpoint clearance |
| ESC to motor | connector space, protected cable route and cooling route |
| Receiver to controls | plug access, signal routes and protected antenna space |
| Body to service areas | removable-cover plan and access openings |

An interface is more than a touching surface.

It includes alignment, attachment, energy, signals, movement and service where relevant.

# Test Plan

Use these three tests as the formal evidence gate.

Keep photographs, tables and marked failures in the Build Passport.

## Test 1 - Fit and Movement Comparison

**Question:** Which layout gives every solid and moving envelope usable clearance?

**Independent variable:** Layout A or Layout B.

**Dependent variables:** number of collisions, number of uncertain gaps and smallest recorded critical gap.

**Control variables:** same component models, datums, wheelbase, track, movement extremes and inspection method.

**Procedure:** Place each layout in turn, move every overlay through the same extremes and mark all contact or uncertainty before revision.

**Pass condition:** Zero solid-envelope overlap, zero contact with moving envelopes and every uncertain critical gap recorded for later measurement.

## Test 2 - Service and Route Comparison

**Question:** Can the battery and selected modules be serviced without pulling wires or disturbing unrelated systems?

**Independent variable:** Layout A or Layout B.

**Dependent variables:** completed service steps, disturbed modules, route contacts and points of confusion.

**Control variables:** same person, written sequence, card models, pretend tool and time method.

**Procedure:** Disconnect, release, remove and replace the battery model; remove the receiver model; approach planned fasteners; then trace each wire and cooling route.

**Pass condition:** Every step is completed without pulling a wire, touching a moving envelope, blocking cooling or moving an unrelated steering or drivetrain module.

## Test 3 - Reproducibility and Balance Comparison

**Question:** Can the chosen arrangement be rebuilt accurately, and does its heavy-module distribution meet the Build Passport intention?

**Independent variable:** Layout A or Layout B.

**Dependent variables:** largest rebuilt-position error and calculated or observed balance position.

**Control variables:** same builder, dimension records, component masses or token weights, board and datum zero.

**Procedure:** Remove the module cards, rebuild each layout from records, measure position errors and calculate the weighted centre or balance the identically weighted board over a pencil.

**Pass condition:** Every controlled module returns within the paper-model tolerance written before the test, and the chosen layout has no unexplained extreme front, rear, left or right bias.

# Topic Summary 📝

- A full-size layout is a physical experiment, not artwork.
- Scale, datum, envelope, route, balance and proof are the six checks.
- `1:1` models reveal access and clearance problems that small sketches hide.
- Every important dimension needs a source and uncertainty status.
- Centreline and axle datums make the arrangement reproducible.
- A packaging envelope includes body, movement, connection, cooling and service space.
- Steering and suspension must be checked at their combined extremes.
- String makes wire bends, slack, protection and snag risks visible.
- Battery removal must include disconnection, restraint release and a complete exit path.
- Cooling routes must remain possible even though paper cannot prove final temperatures.
- A weighted position gives an early comparison of heavy-module balance.
- Mandatory failures overrule decision-matrix scores.
- Revision IDs connect photographs, models, tests and decisions.
- The approved packaging board becomes the geometry authority for Topic 4.3.

# New Words 📖

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Centre of mass | The balance point of the mass in an object or system |
| Component model | A simplified stand-in preserving the dimensions and interfaces needed for a test |
| Controlled dimension | A recorded value that can change only through a documented revision |
| Datum | An agreed reference used as the start for measurements |
| Footprint | The area occupied by a stationary part in one view |
| Full scale | A representation whose measurements match the intended object, written 1:1 |
| Movement envelope | The complete region a moving part can occupy |
| Packaging board | A full-size layout used to test component space, movement, routes and access |
| Packaging envelope | The part plus the space it needs to connect, move, cool and be serviced |
| Provisional | Chosen for the current test but still open to evidence |
| Revision ID | A label identifying one documented state of a model or design |
| Service envelope | Space needed to install, disconnect, inspect or remove a part |
| Tolerance stack-up | Several small dimensional differences combining into a larger mismatch |
| Track width | Distance between stated left and right wheel reference positions |
| Wheelbase | Distance between the front and rear axle lines |

# Answers 🔑

1. Full scale lets real fingers, tools, connectors, wire bends and removal paths be tested without a scale conversion hiding a clearance problem.
2. `REAL` means measured from the actual component, `DRAWING` means taken from an official dimensional drawing, and `ESTIMATE` means the value remains uncertain.
3. The longitudinal centreline, front axle line and rear axle line.
4. Wheelbase is the distance between front and rear axle lines; track width is the stated distance across corresponding left and right wheel references.
5. A footprint shows only the stationary occupied area; a packaging envelope also includes connection, movement, cooling and service space.
6. Example: the battery case is a solid envelope, the steering tyre sweep is a movement envelope, and the battery lift path is a service envelope.
7. Because the real service task includes reaching the connector, releasing the retainer and removing the pack without pulling wires or dismantling unrelated modules.
8. Tight string ignores bends and strains connectors, while uncontrolled loops may reach moving parts; controlled slack permits movement and service while restraint keeps the route protected.
9. `[(100 × 50) + (200 × 150)] ÷ (100 + 200) = 35,000 ÷ 300 = 116.7 mm`, so the estimated centre is about 117 mm from the datum.
10. A weighted score ranks acceptable options; it must not approve a layout that is unsafe, colliding or impossible to service.
11. It reveals whether the recorded datums and dimensions let another person reproduce the arrangement accurately.
12. The approved packaging-board revision, controlled and provisional dimensions, interface records, pass/fail evidence, chosen layout, balance estimate and open questions must pass into Topic 4.3.
