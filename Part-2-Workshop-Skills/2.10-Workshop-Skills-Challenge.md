# Topic 2.10 - Workshop Skills Challenge

> **"You have learned nine separate skills.
> This is the topic where they stop being separate."**

---

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- Write an **operation plan** and follow it through a build with several stages.
- Turn a written brief into a printed part, choosing material and orientation on purpose.
- Mark out, cut, drill and finish a base to your own drawing.
- Choose and fit the right fasteners for a joint that must come apart again.
- Make a soldered lead with strain relief, and test it without any power.
- Judge your own finished work honestly against the requirements you wrote.

---

# Before We Begin

There is a moment in every workshop that separates people who have *read* about making things from people who *make* things.

It arrives when the plan is finished, the tools are laid out, the material is in front of you - and the next move is yours. Nobody is going to tell you which line to cut on. You have to decide, and then live with the decision, because material does not go back on.

That moment is what this topic is for.

So far, every skill arrived in its own topic with its own worked example. Real building is not like that. A single small part needs a decision about material, then orientation, then a hole position, then a fastener, and each decision closes some doors and opens others. Getting each one right on its own is not the skill. Getting them right *in order*, in one build, is.

You are going to make something you will use for the rest of this project.

# Why This Challenge Comes Now

Part 4 builds the buggy. That is a long project with expensive parts, and it is a bad place to discover that you have never taken a design all the way from a written brief to a finished object.

This challenge is deliberately small, cheap and forgiving. If your first tray warps, you reprint it. If your first hole is 2 mm off, you make another base out of scrap. Nothing here is irreplaceable, and everything here teaches you something you will need when the parts *are* expensive.

There is a second reason. The caddy is genuinely useful. Every session in Part 4 begins by finding your tools, and this is the last chance to build the thing that makes that quick before you actually need it.

> The best time to build your workshop is just before you need it, not during the emergency.

# What You Will Produce

By the end you will have:

1. **A written part brief** for the tray - its job, its limits and its pass conditions.
2. **An operation plan** listing every stage in order, with its safety check.
3. **A CAD model** of the tray, saved as a native file and exported for printing.

# Choosing Your Caddy

There is no single correct caddy, and that is deliberate. Choose a size that matches the tools you actually own.

| Version | Tray size | Good if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Small** | About 80 × 50 mm, two compartments | Your printer is small, or this is your first CAD part |
| **Standard** | About 120 × 80 mm, three or four compartments | You have a normal desktop printer and a few tools |
| **Split** | Two smaller trays bolted to one base | Your printer is small but you want the full caddy |

The **split** version is worth thinking about even if you do not need it. Topic 2.5 said that two simple parts often beat one complicated one, and this is a real chance to test that claim with your own hands.

# Stage 1 - Write the Part Brief

Topic 2.5 was clear: design begins with the job, not the shape.

Write this in your notebook before you open any software:

```text
Part:            bench caddy tray
Job:             hold ______ and ______ where I can reach them without looking
Must:            sit flat and not tip when the heaviest tool is at one end
Must:            bolt to a base and come apart with one tool
Should:          keep small screws from escaping if it is knocked
Could:           have a slot for the fit library card
Must not:        be so large it does not fit my build plate
Pass condition:  I can find any listed tool in under 5 seconds
```

Fill in the blanks with your real tools. Then measure them. A tray designed for imaginary hex keys will hold imaginary hex keys perfectly.

**Measure and record:** the longest tool, the widest tool, and the height of the tallest thing you want the tray to hold. These three numbers drive the whole design.

---

# Stage 2 - Write the Operation Plan

An **operation plan** is a list of every stage of a build, in the order you will do them, with the safety check for each one. Professionals write one before touching material, because the expensive mistakes are almost always ordering mistakes.

Here is the shape. Fill in your own version:

```text
Op  What                          Zone       Adult?   Check before starting
1   CAD model + export            clean      no       units correct, brief met
2   Slice + first-layer watch     clean/hot  agreed   four-check rule
3   Print tray                    hot         -       ventilated, never alone
4   Mark out base                 clean      no       datum chosen, waste shaded
5   Cut base                      cutting    yes      clamped, waste side, mat
6   Drill base                    cutting    yes      backing board, clamped
7   Deburr + finish base          cutting    no       eye protection, wet sand
8   Fit fasteners                 clean      no       driver fits, no crushing
9   Make test lead                hot        yes      extraction, unpowered
10  Inspect + test                clean      no       nothing powered
```

Two ordering rules are worth noticing, because both are the kind of thing that only hurts once:

- **Drill before you finish.** Drilling raises burrs, so finishing first means doing it twice.
- **Slide the heat-shrink on before you solder.** Topic 2.9's rule, and there is no way to fix it afterwards.

> **🤔 Think about it.** Look at your plan and find the first operation that
> would be *impossible to undo* if you got it wrong. Now look at what comes
> immediately before it. Is there a cheap check you could add there - a
> measurement, a dry run, a second look at the drawing?

That check is worth more than any amount of care during the operation itself. You cannot be careful enough to un-drill a hole, but you can measure twice for free.

# Stage 3 - Design the Tray

Open your CAD tool and work from the brief, not from a picture in your head.

Follow the order Topic 2.5 gave you:

1. **Packaging envelope first.** Your longest tool, plus room for fingers to lift it out. Fingers need more space than you think - try picking a hex key out of a tight box.
2. **Choose a datum.** For a symmetrical tray, the centre plane is usually right. Every dimension comes from there.
3. **Base and walls before detail.** Get the box right before adding compartments.
4. **Then the features:** dividers, the card slot, the bolt holes.
5. **Then edge treatment:** a small chamfer on the bottom edge against elephant's foot, and fillets in the internal corners.

**Wall thickness:** several printed lines, not one. Topic 2.5 suggested roughly 1.3–1.8 mm with a 0.4 mm nozzle - check what your slicer actually produces in the preview.

**Bolt holes:** clearance holes, not tapped holes. Remember the rule from Topic 2.5 - leave at least one screw diameter of material around each hole. For M3 that means about 3 mm of tray material before you reach an edge.

# Stage 4 - Choose the Material and Orientation

Two decisions, both made on evidence rather than habit.

**Material.** Use the routine from Topic 2.6. What does this part actually have to survive? A bench caddy is not structural, is not hot, and takes no impact - so PLA is genuinely the right answer, and saying so is not laziness. Write your reason down:

```text
Material chosen:  ______
Because:          ______
Rejected:         ______ because ______
```

If you write "PETG because it is stronger", stop and ask what force you are protecting against. A caddy that sits still on a bench does not need toughness. Matching the material to the job is the skill, not always choosing the best material.

**Orientation.** The tray prints flat, open side up - almost certainly. But say *why*, using Topic 2.2's rule:

- Which way will the force act? Downward, from tools resting inside.
- Which face must be flat? The bottom, so it does not rock.
- Where would supports go? Nowhere, if the walls rise straight from the base.

Write the decision in your build log. In Part 4, when a part snaps, this habit is what lets you work out whether orientation was to blame.

---

# Stage 5 - Slice and Print

Run the four-check rule from Topic 2.3 out loud: printer, plate, filament, process.

Then inspect the preview like an X-ray before you commit:

- Do the walls close all the way round each compartment?
- Are the bolt holes actually holes, or has the slicer filled them?
- Does anything appear as an island?
- Is the first layer complete, with no gaps?

Watch the first layer. If it is not right, stop - the next three hours will not fix it.

**While it prints,** do not stand and watch for two hours. Move to the clean zone and mark out your base, which is the next operation on your plan anyway.

# Stage 6 - Make the Base

The base is a flat piece of material - plywood, hardboard, thick card or an offcut of plastic sheet. Ask an adult what is available and safe to cut.

Work exactly as Topic 2.8 taught:

1. **Mark from one datum.** Choose one edge and one end. Every measurement comes from those two, never from the previous mark.
2. **Shade the waste side.** Before any blade touches it. This one habit prevents the single most annoying mistake in the whole challenge.
3. **Clamp, then cut.** Never hold the work. Cut on the waste side of the line so the kerf eats the scrap.
4. **Drill over a backing board.** Centre punch or mark first, pilot hole, then final size. Ease off as you approach breakthrough.
5. **Deburr both faces** of every hole, without enlarging them.
6. **Finish last**, coarse grit to fine. Wet sand if you are sanding plastic.

Then check it against the tray *before* assembling anything: do the holes line up? A base whose holes are 3 mm out is a base you make again, and that is a completely normal outcome the first time.

---

# Stage 7 - Assemble

This is a joint that must come apart, because you will want to change the tray later. That rules out glue and rules out self-tapping screws into plastic.

Choose from Topic 2.7:

- **Through-bolt and nut** - simplest, needs access to both sides
- **Captive nut in a printed pocket** - tidier, needs the pocket designed in
- **Heat-set insert** - best if you will take it apart often, needs adult help

Whichever you choose:

- Use a **washer** under any screw head bearing on plastic. The head sinks in otherwise, and the joint loosens as the plastic creeps.
- Check the **driver fits properly** before applying force.
- Tighten to **firm, not maximum**. On a printed part the plastic gives up long before the screw does.
- **Start every screw by turning it backwards** until it clicks into the existing thread.

# Stage 8 - Make the Continuity Test Lead

This is a real tool you will use in Part 3, and it is made entirely from Topic 2.9's skills.

A **continuity test lead** is simply two lengths of wire, each with a soldered joint at one end, that let you check whether a path exists between two points. In Part 3 you will use it constantly when tracing which wire goes where.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Soldering requires a responsible adult present, fume extraction pulling the
> plume away from your face, safety glasses, and a stable heat-resistant
> surface. Everything here is unpowered scrap wire. Nothing in this stage
> connects to a battery, a charger or the mains - not now, and not as a
> "quick test" afterwards.

Build it in this order:

1. Cut two lengths of stranded wire, about 300 mm each, ideally one red and one black.
2. Strip about 5 mm from each end, without nicking the strands.
3. **Slide the heat-shrink on now.** Both pieces, both wires, before any solder exists.
4. Tin each stripped end.
5. Solder each wire to its clip or pin, heating both parts and feeding solder to the joint.
6. Hold each joint still while it cools.
7. Inspect: shiny, concave, no blobs, no bridges, wire visible inside the fillet.
8. Slide the heat-shrink over and shrink it.
9. Add a strain relief anchor a short distance back from each joint.
10. Test continuity end to end, and check there is no continuity between the two separate leads.

> **[F3 right-versus-wrong array - Figure 2.10.5: two side views of the finished lead where it enters its clip. Left, wrong: the wire soldered and sleeved but with no anchor, a pulling arrow running straight into the joint and a crack starting at the solder edge. Right, correct: the same lead with a small anchor a short distance back, the pulling arrow stopping at the anchor and the joint carrying no load. A magnified inset in each shows the solder fillet]**
>
> *Figure 2.10.5 - The joint conducts. The strain relief is what keeps it conducting after a month in a toolbox.*
>

# Stage 9 - Inspect, Test and Revise

Do not skip this because the caddy already looks finished. Inspection is where the learning is.

## Inspect

| Check | Pass condition | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tray sits flat | No rocking when pressed at each corner | ___ |
| Tray does not tip | Stays down with the heaviest tool at one end | ___ |
| Joint comes apart | One tool, under 30 seconds | ___ |
| No sharp edges | Runs a cloth over every edge without snagging | ___ |
| Holes undamaged | Deburred, still the designed size | ___ |
| Lead continuity | Beeps end to end on each lead | ___ |
| No short between leads | Silent between the two separate leads | ___ |
| The real test | Any listed tool found in under 5 seconds | ___ |

## Then make one revision

Something will be wrong. That is the expected result, not a failure - Topic 1.9 taught that a first version exists to be tested, not admired.

Pick the **one** thing that most affects whether the caddy actually gets used, change only that, and test again. Changing one thing at a time is what makes the result mean anything.

Record it:

```text
Problem:     ______
Root cause:  ______
Change:      ______
Result:      ______
```

# Cheapest Valid Prototype

Before printing anything, you could answer several questions for free:

- Cut the tray outline from cardboard and drop your tools in. Does the size work? Do fingers fit?
- Lay the tools on paper and draw round them. Are three compartments right, or two?
- Fold a card wall to the height you planned. Is it tall enough to stop screws escaping?

Ten minutes of cardboard can save a three-hour reprint. This is the ladder from Topic 2.2, and this challenge is a good place to actually use it rather than nod at it.

# Thinking Like an Engineer - The Order Is the Design

Most people think design means deciding what a thing looks like. In a real build, an equally large part of it is deciding **what order to do things in**.

Look back at your operation plan. Drilling before finishing, heat-shrink before soldering, cardboard before filament, marking before cutting - none of those are about skill. They are about sequence, and getting them wrong costs you material and time no amount of careful work recovers.

> A good plan removes mistakes that care cannot.

In Part 4 the sequence problems get much bigger: which sub-assembly to build first, when to buy the expensive parts, what to test before committing. The habit you build here - writing the order down and checking it before starting - is the one that scales.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Designing for tools you do not own

The tray fits a beautiful imaginary set of hex keys. Measure your real ones first.

## Mistake 2 - Starting the build without an operation plan

The first time you notice the ordering problem should not be when the heat-shrink will not fit over a finished joint.

## Mistake 3 - Choosing PETG because it sounds stronger

A caddy sits on a bench. Match the material to the job, and write down the reason.

## Mistake 4 - Marking each hole from the previous hole

Errors accumulate. Mark everything from one datum, as Topic 2.8 taught.

## Mistake 5 - Cutting on the line

The kerf has width and it has to come from somewhere. Shade the waste side first.

## Mistake 6 - Finishing before drilling

Drilling raises burrs. Finish last, or finish twice.

## Mistake 7 - No washer under the screw head

The steel head sinks into printed plastic, and the joint is loose within a week.

## Mistake 8 - Skipping the inspection because it looks finished

Looking finished and being finished are different states. The table exists for a reason.

# Topic Summary 📝

In this topic we learned that:

- Nine separate skills become one skill when they are used **in order**, on one object.
- An **operation plan** is written before touching material, because the expensive mistakes are ordering mistakes.
- A part brief comes from **measured** tools, not imagined ones.
- Material and orientation are decisions to be **justified in writing**, not habits.
- Some sequences are one-way: drill before finishing, heat-shrink before soldering, cardboard before filament.
- A joint that must come apart needs the right fastener strategy, and a washer wherever steel meets printed plastic.
- Inspection is part of the build, and a first version exists to be tested rather than admired.
- The caddy and the test lead are real tools you will use for the rest of the project.

# Looking Ahead 🔭

# Answers 🔑

1. Because the most expensive mistakes in a build are ordering mistakes, not skill mistakes. Once you have drilled, cut or soldered in the wrong sequence, no amount of care recovers the material - but noticing the problem on paper costs nothing.
2. Any two of: cutting the base, drilling a hole, sanding a surface, or soldering a joint once the heat-shrink is no longer able to be fitted. All remove material or close off an option permanently.
3. Because the sleeve has to be threaded onto the wire, and once the joint is soldered to a clip or pin the end is blocked. There is no way to get it on afterwards short of unsoldering the joint.
4. Because drilling raises burrs around both faces of the hole. If you finish first, the drilling undoes the finish and you have to do it again.
5. Because a compartment exactly as long as the tool leaves no room to get your fingers in and lift it out. The packaging envelope is the tool *plus* the access it needs - which is the lesson from Topic 2.5.
6. Because the caddy sits still on a bench. It takes no impact, gets no hotter than the room, and carries almost no load. PETG's toughness solves a problem this part does not have, and PLA is easier to print and cheaper. Matching the material to the job is the skill.
7. Because errors accumulate. Measuring each mark from the previous one adds every small error to the next, so the last hole can be several millimetres out. From a single datum, each error stays where it happened.
