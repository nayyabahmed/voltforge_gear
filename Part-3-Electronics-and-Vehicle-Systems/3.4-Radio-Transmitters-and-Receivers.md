# Topic 3.4 - Radio Transmitters and Receivers

> **"A fast buggy is exciting.
> A buggy that obeys the correct command every time is engineered."**

# Before We Begin

The VoltForge buggy is raised safely above the bench.
Its battery remains disconnected and its wheels cannot drive the floor.

You turn the transmitter wheel left.
The buggy's front wheels point right.

Then you pull the throttle trigger.
The steering twitches instead of the motor responding.

Nothing is random.
Two commands have reached the wrong places, and one is travelling in the wrong direction.

Your mission is to repair the command system before any drive power is allowed.
You will prove six things:

1. **IDENTITY** - Is this receiver listening to the intended transmitter?
2. **ROUTE** - Does each command reach the correct channel?
3. **DIRECTION** - Does left mean left and forward mean the intended forward request?
4. **NEUTRAL** - Do released controls produce the planned resting commands?
5. **LIMITS** - Does movement stop before the mechanism strains?
6. **LOSS** - Does the system enter its documented safe state if the radio link disappears?

Every hands-on activity in this topic uses people, paper, card or string.
Real batteries and powered RC equipment are not required.

# The Six-Check Command Mission

Think of a parcel delivery.
The sender needs the correct address, the delivery route and the correct recipient.
A parcel reaching the wrong house quickly is still a failed delivery.

An RC command also needs a complete, correct route:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A["Driver input"] --> B["Transmitter"]
    B -. "coded radio link" .-> C["Receiver"]
    C --> D["Channel"]
    D --> E["Servo or ESC"]
```

The **transmitter** is the hand-held unit that turns driver inputs into coded radio messages.

The **receiver** is the small unit in the buggy that recognises compatible messages and sends each command through an output channel.

The six checks surround that route.
One successful steering movement does not prove all six.

Remember Topic 3.1.
The radio link carries **information**, not the energy that turns the motor.

| Path | Main question | Typical route |
|---|---|---|
| Command path | What does the driver want? | Driver → transmitter → receiver → servo or ESC |
| Energy path | What supplies electrical energy? | Battery → designed power route → electronics and motor |
| Movement path | What physically moves? | Servo linkage or motor → drivetrain → wheels |

Mixing these paths makes diagnosis confusing.
Trace the command first, then trace power.

# Meet the Driver's Controls

A game controller turns finger movement into information.
An RC transmitter does the same job, but its controls are shaped for a moving vehicle.

A common surface transmitter has:

- a steering wheel
- a throttle trigger
- trim controls
- switches or buttons
- an antenna inside or outside the case
- a battery compartment
- a power control and status indicators

The exact layout varies.
Labels in this topic are examples, not instructions for an unknown transmitter.

## Steering input

Turning the wheel changes the steering request around a centre position.
Releasing it normally returns the transmitter input to centre.

## Throttle input

Pulling the trigger commonly requests forward throttle.
Pushing it may request brake and, depending on the electronic speed controller's mode, reverse.

The electronic speed controller, or ESC, is the motor controller studied fully in Topic 3.6.
Do not assume that every trigger movement produces the same response on every ESC.

# Frequency Is the Room; Protocol Is the Language

Imagine two teams standing in the same sports hall.
One team speaks English and the other uses a private code.
They share the same room, but they cannot automatically understand each other.

Many modern surface-RC systems operate in the `2.4 GHz` frequency band.
**Frequency** tells us how often a radio wave repeats each second.
`GHz` means billions of cycles per second.

The **protocol** is the manufacturer's set of rules for formatting and interpreting the radio message.

Two products can both say `2.4 GHz` and still use incompatible protocols.
Even products from one brand may belong to different radio families.

Compatibility is proved by the manufacturer's transmitter-receiver compatibility information.
It is not proved by frequency, connector shape or appearance.

In the UK, suitable model-control equipment can operate without an individual radio licence only when it meets the applicable licence-exempt technical conditions.
Buy compliant equipment from a reputable supplier and keep its instructions.

# Channels Are Labelled Delivery Lanes

A school corridor may have one door for each classroom.
Putting the right pupil through the wrong door creates a routing problem, not a pupil problem.

A **channel** is one separate control route through the radio system.

A simple surface system commonly uses:

| Receiver output | Common job | Connected subsystem |
|---|---|---|
| CH1 | Steering | Steering servo |
| CH2 | Throttle and brake request | ESC |
| CH3 or AUX | Optional function | Light or accessory controller |

These assignments are common examples, not universal rules.
Read the exact receiver and transmitter manuals.

>

Many servo-style leads contain three conductors:

- electrical supply
- return or ground
- control signal

Colours vary between products.
Use receiver markings such as `S`, `+` and `-`, plus the equipment instructions.

Never force a plug.
Never rely on one remembered colour rule.

# Binding Establishes Identity

Imagine agreeing on a private call sign in a crowded room.
Your teammate responds only to the agreed identity.

**Binding** is the equipment-specific process that links a compatible receiver with a transmitter.

Binding does not choose what CH1 and CH2 do.
It establishes which compatible radio partner the receiver should recognise.

The exact steps can include:

- selecting the correct model memory
- placing the receiver in bind mode
- using a button or bind plug
- holding controls in defined safe positions
- following a specified power sequence
- checking an indicator-light pattern
- storing failsafe positions

These details vary between products.
Never copy a button sequence from a different receiver because it looks similar.

>
> *Figure 3.4.4 - Binding is a documented identity process with preparation and verification, not a universal button trick.*
>

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Every real binding, setup, failsafe or range test must be led by a responsible adult using the exact transmitter, receiver, ESC and vehicle instructions.
> Keep the battery disconnected while planning and inspecting.
> Before any powered setup, remove drive capability using the manufacturer's safe method, such as disconnecting the motor, removing driven wheels or securely raising the vehicle where permitted.
> Keep fingers, hair, loose clothing and tools away from steering linkages, gears, shafts, tyres and other moving parts.
> Stop immediately for unexpected movement, heat, smell, smoke, damaged insulation, loose parts or an unexplained indicator.
> Real ground testing waits until identity, route, direction, neutral, limits, failsafe and range checks have passed.

The exact manual is the authority.
If it is missing or unclear, the powered test stops.

# Receiver Power Is a Separate Requirement

A phone can receive the correct message only while it has suitable electrical power.
A receiver also needs a supply within its stated voltage range.

In many electric RC systems, the ESC contains a **battery eliminator circuit**, or BEC.
The BEC supplies regulated electrical power to the receiver and often the steering servo.

The name comes from removing the need for a separate receiver battery.

Check the complete chain:

```text
Battery → ESC/BEC → receiver power rails → steering servo
```

The battery decision gates from Topic 3.3 still apply.
The receiver, servo and ESC/BEC voltage ranges must agree.

Never connect two active receiver power sources unless the exact instructions explicitly permit and explain it.

Topic 3.6 explores ESC and BEC operation in detail.

# Direction, Neutral and Limits

Three adjustments are often confused because all three change what the buggy does.
They solve different problems.

| Adjustment | Question it answers | It should not hide |
|---|---|---|
| Channel reverse | Does output move in the intended direction? | Wrong linkage or wrong channel |
| Trim | Is the neutral position slightly offset? | Bent, loose or unequal steering geometry |
| Endpoint | How far may the output move safely? | A linkage that binds too early |

## Channel reverse

If a checked steering installation turns right when the driver requests left, **channel reverse** changes the commanded direction.

For throttle, direction and calibration affect safety.
Follow both transmitter and ESC manuals rather than experimenting with powered wheels.

## Trim

Imagine a bicycle that drifts slightly when the handlebars appear straight.
A small correction can restore the neutral direction.

**Trim** makes a small change to a channel's neutral command.
It is for final correction, not structural repair.

## Endpoints

Imagine a door that reaches a wall at `90°`.
Pushing harder does not create useful travel; it loads the hinge and handle.

An **endpoint** limits how far a channel commands an output in one direction.

Steering endpoints should stop movement before:

- a linkage binds
- a servo horn strikes the chassis
- a steering knuckle reaches a hard stop under force
- a tyre rubs the body or another part
- the servo strains, buzzes or heats

Left and right limits may need separate settings.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Hold a door gently just before it reaches a wall, then imagine continuing to push after it touches. Which part of that extra effort creates useful movement?

None of the effort after contact creates useful travel; it becomes load in the door, hinge and your arm. A servo commanded beyond a steering stop faces the same problem, so the electronic endpoint must sit just before the mechanical limit.

Advanced transmitters may also offer dual rate or exponential response.
Those are tuning tools, not repairs, and their directions can vary by manufacturer.
They are outside this first-control mission.

# Failsafe Plans for a Missing Message

Imagine a moving walkway whose control wire is cut.
Continuing at an unknown command would not be a safe plan.

A **failsafe** is the receiver or radio system's documented response when it loses a valid radio signal.

Failsafe behaviour varies.
Depending on the equipment and setup, a channel may move to a stored position, hold its last command or stop producing a command.

For an electric buggy, the throttle outcome must prevent unintended drive according to the exact radio and ESC instructions.
The steering outcome must also be known.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Put a paper buggy on a drawn path towards a wall. Cover the dotted radio link on your command diagram. If you have never tested the saved response, which throttle position should you assume the real receiver will choose?

You should assume none. A label such as "failsafe fitted" does not prove the saved position or whole-system response, so the real behaviour must be configured and observed with drive capability disabled before the buggy touches the ground.

It does not replace receiver power, sound wiring, a safe driving area or an attentive driver.

# Range and Antenna Installation

A quiet conversation may work across one room but fail through a wall or from a different direction.
Radio reliability also depends on distance, orientation, installation, power and surroundings.

A **range check** is a controlled test carried out using the manufacturer's specified method and distance.
Some transmitters provide a reduced-power test mode; others specify a different procedure.

Never invent a distance.
Never discover the limit by driving until control disappears.

The **antenna** is the receiver's radio-energy collecting structure, not spare wire.

Install it exactly as specified, including instructions about:

- orientation
- active section
- antenna tube or support
- distance from motor and high-current wiring
- distance from carbon fibre or metal shielding
- protection from gears, shafts and tyres
- bending, coiling, crushing or cutting

>
> *Figure 3.4.7 - Antenna routing follows the receiver manual, because neatness alone does not create a reliable radio link.*

A range-check plan includes:

- a suitable open area away from roads and people
- adult leadership and an observer if the manual calls for one
- the exact model-specific method and distance
- charged transmitter and vehicle batteries in acceptable condition
- the installed antenna and final receiver position
- drive disabled for the first control check
- a written pass condition
- an immediate stop for glitches, delay or lost response

# Start-Up, Shut-Down and First-Control Check

Many surface systems instruct the user to switch the transmitter on before the vehicle, then switch the vehicle off before the transmitter.
That keeps a command source present while the vehicle is energised.

It is a common intention, not permission to ignore the manual.
Binding, calibration and specialist systems can use different steps.

Before any low-speed ground test, verify in the documented order:

1. correct model memory and receiver identity
2. secure receiver and antenna installation
3. correct channel and plug orientation
4. steering direction
5. stable steering and throttle neutral
6. endpoints clear of hard stops
7. failsafe response with drive disabled
8. manufacturer-defined range check

Record the result.
Do not change several settings at once.

# Diagnosing by Tracing the Path

When one function fails, follow its route instead of replacing random parts.

| Symptom | First evidence to check |
|---|---|
| No steering or throttle | Receiver power, correct model memory, binding and plug orientation |
| Steering works; throttle does not | CH2 route, ESC neutral or calibration, and motor-energy path |
| Throttle works; steering does not | CH1 route, servo lead, receiver supply and steering linkage |
| Trigger moves steering | Channel assignment |
| Left request produces right steering | Linkage installation, correct channel and channel reverse |
| Servo buzzes near full lock | Endpoint and mechanical binding |
| Response glitches or range is short | Battery state, receiver power, antenna condition and final installation |

Use the six checks in order.
A visible symptom is a clue, not a verdict.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Human Command Relay

This activity needs no equipment.
Two people are useful; three make the routing clearer.

## Roles

- DRIVER/TRANSMITTER
- RECEIVER
- OUTPUT, acting as steering servo and ESC

## Steps

1. Agree four spoken commands: `STEER LEFT`, `STEER RIGHT`, `THROTTLE` and `NEUTRAL`.
2. The driver says one command to the receiver.
3. The receiver repeats it with `CH1` or `CH2` added.
4. The output turns their hands for steering or moves one hand forwards for throttle.
5. Deliberately send steering through CH2.
6. Name the failed check: identity, route, direction, neutral, limits or loss.
7. Deliberately reverse the output response.
8. Name the new failed check.
9. End every sequence with `NEUTRAL`.

## Upgrade

Make blue steering cards and orange throttle cards.
Use symbols as well as colour so the routes remain clear in greyscale.

## What you discovered

One link can carry several commands only when the receiver keeps their routes separate.

# Topic Mini Project - Radio Command Mission-Control Console 🛠️

Build a keepable card transmitter, receiver and two moving outputs.
The console turns the six checks into a fault-finding game.

It does not transmit radio waves or power a motor.
You act as its encoding, radio and decoding system.

## Materials

- packaging card
- scrap paper
- pencil and pens
- ruler
- age-appropriate scissors
- tape or glue
- two lengths of string, wool or paper strip
- drinking straws or rolled-paper guides
- paper fasteners, or folded-paper pivots
- one envelope

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult or guardian what you plan to build before starting, and build with them nearby.
> Use age-appropriate scissors on a clear table and cut away from fingers.
> Do not use a craft knife.
> Keep real batteries, motors and RC electronics disconnected from this model.

> **🎬 Watch the build**
>
> - Science Buddies: search "cardboard automata" to see how sliders, pivots and guides create repeatable movement.
> - Exploratorium Tinkering Studio: search "cardboard mechanisms" for household-material linkage ideas.
> - Before any later powered setup, search the exact transmitter and receiver model plus "manual binding failsafe" with a responsible adult.

## Step 1 - Make the base

Cut a card base about `450 mm × 280 mm`.

Mark `DRIVER` on the left and `BUGGY` on the right.

## Step 2 - Make the transmitter

Add a rotating steering wheel and sliding throttle trigger.

Mark:

- left, centre and right
- forward request, neutral and brake/reverse request

## Step 3 - Make the receiver

Add removable ports labelled `CH1`, `CH2` and `AUX`.

Add example `S`, `+` and `-` marks.
Write `CHECK THE ACTUAL MANUAL` beside them.

## Step 4 - Route steering

Run one string from the steering wheel through the radio gap to CH1.
Continue it to a card steering arm.

The arm should rotate left and right around a paper pivot.

## Step 5 - Route throttle

Run the second string from the trigger through the radio gap to CH2.
Continue it to a three-position throttle pointer.

Moving throttle must not move steering.

## Step 6 - Mark the radio gap

Leave a physical gap between transmitter and receiver.

Bridge it with a dotted card strip labelled:

```text
CODED RADIO LINK - INFORMATION, NOT DRIVE POWER
```

## Step 7 - Add identity cards

Make matching transmitter and receiver ID cards.
Only a matching pair completes the radio link.

## Step 8 - Add neutral and direction controls

Add a small trim slider near the steering neutral mark.

Add a two-sided `NORMAL / REVERSED` direction card.

## Step 9 - Add endpoints

Add adjustable stop tabs just before the steering arm's hard limits.

The left and right tabs should move independently.

## Step 10 - Add the signal-loss gate

Make a red `SIGNAL LOST` card that covers the radio gap.

When inserted, the operator must move the throttle pointer to the written safe-state card.

## Step 11 - Add the antenna route

Draw a receiver antenna following a fictional manual route.

Keep it clear of the model motor zone and moving card mechanism.

## Step 12 - Make six check cards

Create:

- IDENTITY
- ROUTE
- DIRECTION
- NEUTRAL
- LIMITS
- LOSS

Place each card beside the part it tests.

## Step 13 - Make fault cards

Create at least eight:

- incompatible radio protocol
- wrong identity pair
- steering in CH2
- plug turned around
- steering direction reversed
- trim hiding a bent-link card
- endpoint beyond hard stop
- unknown failsafe response
- damaged antenna
- guessed range distance

Store them in the envelope.

## Step 14 - Run a normal mission

Move steering left, centre and right.

Move throttle through its three positions.

Confirm each output follows only its intended input.

## Step 15 - Run a signal-loss mission

Insert the `SIGNAL LOST` card from three different starting commands.

Move the throttle pointer to the planned state and explain why a real system still needs a drive-disabled test.

## Step 16 - Run hidden faults

Ask another person to add one fault card.

Test the six checks in order until the first failure is found.
Change only the failed item.

## Step 17 - Reflect

Write on the back:

1. Which two checks are easiest to confuse?
2. Why does `2.4 GHz` not prove compatibility?
3. Why is failsafe a test result rather than a promise?
4. What can your paper console never prove about real radio range?

## Step 18 - Keep the artifact

Store the check and fault cards in the envelope.

Keep the console on your showcase shelf.
It will connect to the steering-servo model in Topic 3.5 and the ESC model in Topic 3.6.

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Predictable Control

A control system is useful when the intended input produces the intended output reliably.

Radio-system evidence answers separate questions:

- Has the correct receiver linked to the intended transmitter?
- Has each command reached the correct output?
- Does every response move in the intended direction?
- Is neutral stable when controls are released?
- Do limits protect the mechanism?
- Is signal-loss behaviour known and safe?
- Does the installed system pass its specified range check?

Engineers do not replace all of those questions with "it worked once".

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Independent channel routing

**Question:** Does each input move only its intended output?

**Independent variable:** Steering or throttle input selected.

**Dependent variable:** Output pointer that moves.

**Control variables:** Same console, channel map, string tension and starting neutral.

**Procedure:** Apply six mixed steering and throttle commands.

**Pass condition:** Steering moves only the steering output and throttle moves only the throttle output every time.

## Test 2 - Endpoint protection

**Question:** Do the endpoint tabs stop useful movement before the hard stops?

**Independent variable:** Endpoint-tab position.

**Dependent variable:** Clearance between steering arm and hard stop.

**Control variables:** Same arm, pivot, input movement and base.

**Procedure:** Compare conservative, correct and excessive tab positions on both sides.

**Pass condition:** The selected position gives useful travel with visible clearance at each hard stop.

## Test 3 - Signal-loss reasoning

**Question:** Can the operator apply and explain the written safe state when the model link is removed?

**Independent variable:** Radio-link card present or covered.

**Dependent variable:** Throttle-pointer position and spoken explanation.

**Control variables:** Same console, channel map, safe-state card and starting commands.

**Procedure:** Cover the link from three different throttle positions.

**Pass condition:** The pointer moves to the documented model state every time and the operator states that real behaviour needs a drive-disabled test.

# Topic Summary 📝

- The transmitter converts driver input into coded radio messages.
- The receiver recognises compatible messages and routes commands through channels.
- The command path carries information; the energy path supplies electrical energy.
- `2.4 GHz` identifies a frequency band, not a guaranteed compatible protocol.
- Binding establishes radio identity; channel assignment chooses each output route.
- Servo-style ports commonly carry signal, supply and return, but markings and manuals decide orientation.
- Channel reverse changes response direction, trim makes a small neutral correction and endpoints limit travel.
- Failsafe behaviour varies and must be observed with drive capability disabled.
- Range checks use the exact manufacturer's method and distance.
- Antenna position, receiver power and installation can affect control reliability.
- Real setup and testing are adult-led and manual-specific.
- Six checks make control predictable: identity, route, direction, neutral, limits and loss.

# New Words 📖

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Antenna | The part of a radio system that sends or receives radio-frequency energy |
| Battery eliminator circuit (BEC) | A circuit, often inside an ESC, that supplies regulated power to the receiver and servo |
| Binding | Linking a compatible receiver with the identity of a transmitter |
| Channel | One separate control route through a radio system |
| Channel reverse | A setting that changes the direction of a channel's response |
| Endpoint | A configured limit on how far a channel commands an output |
| Failsafe | The documented control response when a valid radio signal is lost |
| Frequency | The number of repeating wave cycles each second |
| Neutral | The planned command when a spring-centred control is released |
| Protocol | The rules and message format used by a radio system |
| Range check | A controlled test of radio operation using the manufacturer's method |
| Receiver | The unit in the buggy that recognises radio messages and outputs channel commands |
| Transmitter | The hand-held unit that turns driver inputs into coded radio messages |
| Trim | A small adjustment to a channel's neutral command |

# Answers 🔑

1. Driver input → transmitter → coded radio link → receiver and its selected output channel. The connected servo or ESC then receives that channel command.
2. The command path carries information about what the driver wants. The energy path supplies the electrical energy used by the electronics and motor.
3. `2.4 GHz` identifies a frequency band. The products must also use compatible radio protocols and be listed as compatible by their manufacturer.
4. Binding links a compatible receiver to a transmitter's identity. Channel assignment decides which receiver output carries steering, throttle or another function.
5. CH1 commonly serves steering and CH2 commonly serves throttle, but the exact transmitter and receiver manuals must confirm the assignment.
6. The conductors commonly provide electrical supply, return or ground, and control signal.
7. Channel reverse changes response direction. Trim makes a small change to neutral. Endpoints limit the maximum commanded movement in each direction.
8. Once the steering reaches a hard stop, extra command creates load without useful movement. The stalled or heavily loaded servo can buzz, draw more current and heat.
9. A failsafe may be stored incorrectly or behave differently from an assumption. Disabling drive capability lets an adult observe the response without allowing the buggy to propel itself.
10. Losing control of a moving buggy creates the hazard the test is meant to prevent. A valid range check uses the manufacturer's controlled mode, distance, place and pass condition.
11. Check the steering command path: CH1 assignment, plug orientation, receiver power, servo lead and steering linkage. Change one item at a time.
12. Identity, route, direction, neutral, limits and loss.
