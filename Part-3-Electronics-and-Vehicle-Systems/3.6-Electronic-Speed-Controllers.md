# Topic 3.6 - Electronic Speed Controllers

> **"The trigger requests motion; the ESC decides how battery power reaches the motor."**

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- Explain how an ESC joins the throttle-command path to the motor-energy path.
- Explain how rapid switching changes average motor drive without wasting most energy as heat.
- Distinguish brushed and brushless ESC interfaces and match key electrical ratings.
- Explain BEC supply, throttle calibration, braking, reverse and protection modes.
- Plan a safe, drive-disabled ESC commissioning sequence using the exact equipment manual.
- Build and fair-test a reusable cardboard ESC commissioning console.

# Before We Begin

The VoltForge buggy is safely supported above the bench.
Its driven wheels cannot touch anything.

An adult switches on the radio system using the manufacturer's instructions.
The steering responds normally.

Then three strange things happen.

- A tiny trigger movement makes the motor surge.
- Reverse will not begin when expected.
- A stronger throttle command makes the steering twitch and the receiver restart.

The transmitter is sending commands.
The battery contains energy.
The motor can turn.

Yet the buggy still cannot control speed reliably.

The missing detective is the **electronic speed controller**, usually shortened to **ESC**.
It listens to a small throttle signal and controls a much larger flow of energy to the motor.

Your mission is to diagnose the three symptoms without changing settings at random.
You will prove six checks:

1. **TYPE** - Does the ESC suit the motor technology?
2. **SUPPLY** - Do the battery, current path and BEC ratings match the system?
3. **SIGNAL** - Is the receiver sending the intended throttle command?
4. **NEUTRAL** - Does the ESC recognise stop and the full command range?
5. **MODE** - Are braking, reverse and battery settings appropriate?
6. **PROTECTION** - What caused the ESC to reduce or stop output?

Every activity and build in this topic uses hands, paper or card.
No real battery, ESC or motor is required.

# The ESC Joins Two Different Paths

Imagine a theatre lighting desk.
A small movement of one slider can control a powerful stage lamp.

The slider does not supply the lamp's energy.
It tells another device how much energy to deliver.

An ESC performs a similar job in the buggy.

Remember the paths from Topic 3.1:

| Path | What travels? | Route through the ESC |
|---|---|---|
| Command | Throttle information | Transmitter → receiver → ESC signal input |
| Energy | Electrical energy | Battery → ESC power stage → motor |
| Control supply | Regulated electrical energy | ESC's BEC → receiver and servo, in many systems |
| Movement | Rotation and torque | Motor shaft → drivetrain → wheels |

The receiver lead carries a control signal and often a regulated supply for the radio system.
The thick battery and motor leads carry much more current.

That is why the receiver cannot power the drive motor directly.
Its job is to communicate, not to become the motor's high-current route.

# Switching Power Instead of Throwing It Away

Imagine pedalling a bicycle using short pushes.
Two pushes in every ten feel gentle; nine pushes in every ten feel much stronger.

The pushes are either happening or not happening.
The average effort changes because the on-time changes.

An ESC uses electronic switches in a related way.
It turns motor voltage on and off very rapidly.

This method is called **pulse-width modulation**, or **PWM**.
The fraction of one switching cycle spent on is the **duty cycle**.

| Simplified duty cycle | Switch on during ten equal slices | Model meaning |
|---:|---|---|
| `0%` | none | no commanded drive |
| `20%` | 2 slices | low average drive |
| `50%` | 5 slices | medium average drive |
| `90%` | 9 slices | high average drive |

The switching is far faster than the paper model in this topic.
The motor's electrical and mechanical behaviour smooths the pulses into useful rotation.

The exact switching strategy depends on the ESC and motor system.
Our model explains the principle, not one product's waveform.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Tap the table twice during ten steady counts. Then tap nine times during the same ten counts. You did not make each tap stronger, so what changed?

The fraction of time spent tapping changed. PWM follows the same central idea: rapid full switching can change average drive by changing on-time, rather than behaving like a simple resistor that must waste the unwanted energy as heat.

## A worked duty-cycle model

**Question:** A paper PWM wheel divides one pretend cycle into `10` equal sectors.
If `7` sectors are marked ON, what is the duty cycle?

**Known values:**

- on sectors = `7`
- total sectors = `10`

**Rule:**

```text
duty cycle = on time ÷ total cycle time × 100%
```

**Calculation:**

```text
duty cycle = 7 ÷ 10 × 100% = 70%
```

**Meaning:**

The paper model requests drive during `70%` of the pretend cycle.
It does not prove a real motor will run at exactly `70%` of its unloaded speed.

# Brushed and Brushless Are Different Systems

A relay race works only when runners pass the baton in the correct order.
Changing the number of runners changes how the hand-offs must happen.

A **brushed motor** performs its winding hand-offs mechanically through brushes and a commutator inside the motor.
It commonly connects to a brushed ESC with two motor wires.

A **brushless motor** relies on the ESC to energise several motor phases in a timed sequence.
That sequence is **electronic commutation**.
It commonly uses three phase leads.

| Feature | Brushed system | Brushless system |
|---|---|---|
| Common motor-power connections | Two | Three phase leads |
| Where commutation happens | Brushes and commutator inside motor | Electronic switching in ESC |
| Sensor lead | Not normally used | May be present in a sensored system |
| ESC must state support | Brushed | Brushless |

A connector that physically fits does not prove electrical compatibility.
The ESC documentation must support the motor type and planned battery.

Some specialist controllers support more than one type or mode.
Treat that as a documented exception, not a universal rule.

# Meet the Physical ESC

An ESC may include:

- a protective case or circuit-board coating
- thick battery input leads
- two or three motor output leads
- a thin receiver lead and plug
- input capacitors
- a heat sink
- a cooling fan on some models
- an on/off or control switch
- a set button or programming port
- status LEDs and audible codes through the motor

Product layouts vary.
Never identify a lead by position alone.

Use the markings, wiring diagram and exact model manual.
Previous ownership makes the manual and known history even more important.

The ESC also needs a **packaging envelope**.
Remember the suitcase analogy from the style of engineering used in Part 2: fitting the case is not enough.

Allow room for:

- cable bends
- connector access
- airflow
- fan clearance
- button or switch access
- removal without dismantling the whole buggy

# Match the Supply as a Chain

Choosing an ESC is like checking every door on an obstacle course.
Passing five doors does not cancel one locked door.

A candidate must pass every non-negotiable interface:

1. supported motor type
2. supported battery chemistry
3. supported battery voltage or cell-count range
4. realistic continuous-current capability
5. relevant short-duration or peak conditions
6. wire and connector capability
7. BEC voltage and current capability
8. physical fit and cooling conditions
9. required running and protection modes

**Continuous current** is a current claimed for continuing operation under stated conditions.

**Peak current** is allowed only for a stated short time and under stated conditions.

Never compare one product's peak figure with another product's continuous figure.
The words beside the number matter.

A large printed current number does not repair:

- poor airflow
- unsuitable gearing
- a stalled motor
- weak connectors
- damaged wires
- an overloaded drivetrain

## A worked selection example

This is a fictional paper decision, not permission to connect equipment.

**Buggy requirement:**

- brushed motor
- `2S LiPo` battery already approved in Topic 3.3
- estimated `25 A` continuing current in the planned test
- one receiver and one servo requiring a compatible `6 V` supply
- forward, brake and reverse required

**Candidate label:**

- brushed motor support
- `2-3S LiPo` support
- `40 A continuous` under the maker's stated cooling conditions
- `6 V, 3 A BEC`
- LiPo low-voltage cut-off
- forward/brake/reverse mode

**First decision:**

The label passes the first paper gates and gives `15 A` of stated continuous-current headroom:

```text
headroom = 40 A - 25 A = 15 A
```

**Still unknown:**

- actual motor current in the real buggy
- servo peak-current demand
- connector and wire capability
- temperature in the intended enclosure
- whether all manufacturer rating conditions are met

The correct result is **candidate for later testing**, not **proved safe**.

# The BEC Feeds the Control Side

A building may receive high-voltage power at its entrance but supply lower, controlled voltages to different rooms.
The rooms do not take the full incoming supply directly.

Many ESCs include a **battery eliminator circuit**, or **BEC**.
It provides regulated power to the receiver and servo through the receiver connection.

The name comes from removing the need for a separate receiver battery in many systems.
It does not remove the main drive battery.

Check both:

- BEC output voltage
- BEC current capability under stated conditions

The voltage must suit the receiver and every connected device.
The current capability must support their real demand, including short servo loads.

Remember Topic 3.5.
A steering mechanism that binds can increase servo current demand.

If the control-supply voltage falls far enough under load, the receiver may reset.
This temporary loss of adequate supply is often called a **brownout**.

The buggy may then show confusing symptoms:

- steering twitch
- receiver restart
- temporary loss of control
- repeated reconnecting

These can resemble a radio-link fault even when the cause is power delivery.

Do not add a second receiver supply without checking the equipment instructions.
Two supply sources connected incorrectly can damage equipment.

# Calibration Teaches the ESC Your Trigger Range

Imagine a translator who has never been told which word means stop.
Even a clear message can then produce the wrong action.

An ESC must recognise the receiver commands representing positions such as:

- full forward
- neutral
- full brake or reverse request

**Throttle calibration** records the relevant command endpoints for a particular ESC and radio setup.

The exact procedure varies.
One controller may detect the range automatically.
Another may require a set button, trigger positions, lights or tones in a stated order.

If the transmitter, receiver, model memory, throttle travel or relevant setup changes, the manual may require recalibration.

Never substitute a remembered sequence from another model.

## Neutral comes first

Neutral is the safe stop reference recognised by the ESC.
It is not merely where the plastic trigger appears to rest.

Throttle trim, channel direction, travel settings or a mismatched model memory can change the received command.

Some ESCs will refuse to arm when they cannot detect a valid neutral.
That refusal is useful evidence.
Do not defeat it by pulling the trigger until something happens.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Place three cards marked FULL FORWARD, NEUTRAL and FULL BRAKE face up. Ask someone to choose the safe starting card before you reveal the fictional manual. Why is guessing a poor commissioning method even if they choose neutral?

Neutral is a sensible expectation, but the correct procedure still comes from the exact manual because equipment differs. Engineering safety means checking the documented sequence and status indications, not turning a common pattern into a universal rule.

## The universal reasoning sequence

The buttons vary, but the reasoning can stay consistent:

1. identify the exact ESC and radio manuals
2. confirm motor, battery and BEC compatibility
3. inspect polarity, insulation, connectors and mounting
4. make propulsion unable to cause movement by the approved method
5. select the correct transmitter model memory
6. set channel direction, trim and travel as instructed
7. place the throttle control at the instructed safe position
8. follow the exact calibration sequence
9. recognise the documented LED or tone response
10. test direction and stop response at the lowest practical energy
11. disconnect the battery after the test
12. record the result and any unknowns

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> A real ESC, battery and motor must be set up only with a responsible adult directly supervising and the exact equipment manuals open.
> Use the manufacturer's approved drive-disabled method before calibration or direction checks.
> Keep hair, fingers, clothing, jewellery and tools away from shafts, gears, fans and tyres.
> Treat the battery connector as live whenever the battery is connected; an ESC switch may not isolate it fully.
> Stop immediately for smoke, a sharp or sweet chemical smell, swelling, damaged insulation, large unexpected sparks, unusual heat or uncontrolled movement.
> If a battery smokes or burns, move away, get out, stay out and call `999`.

# Running Mode Changes Trigger Behaviour

A lift button can mean "close the door" before it means "go down".
The same press produces different actions depending on the machine's state.

An ESC's **running mode** defines how throttle, braking and reverse requests are interpreted.

Common possibilities include:

- forward with brake
- forward, brake and reverse
- forward and reverse without a separate brake stage
- crawler modes with **drag brake** at neutral
- competition modes without reverse

Some forward/brake/reverse controllers require a stop and a second reverse request before the vehicle reverses.
Others use different logic.

That explains why a buggy can brake normally but appear to "refuse" reverse.
The mode may be doing exactly what it was configured to do.

**Brake force** controls how strongly the motor resists rotation during a braking command.

**Drag brake** is braking applied at or near neutral on some systems.
It can help a crawler hold position, but an aggressive setting may increase heat or make a fast buggy unstable.

Select the mode for the vehicle, test space and learner.
Confirm the expected trigger sequence before ground use.

# Protection Modes Are Clues

A fire door protects a building, but repeatedly slamming shut tells you something is wrong.
You investigate the cause instead of wedging the door open.

ESC protection behaves similarly.
It is a last line of defence and a source of diagnostic evidence.

## Low-voltage cut-off

A **low-voltage cut-off**, or **LVC**, reduces or stops motor output when the measured pack voltage reaches the configured condition.

Its purpose in a correctly configured LiPo system is to help prevent over-discharge.
It does not make a damaged battery, wrong charger or incompatible cell count safe.

When LVC activates:

1. stop driving
2. return the throttle to neutral
3. disconnect and inspect according to Topic 3.3 and the equipment manual
4. do not repeatedly restart to extract the last energy

Battery chemistry and cell-count settings must match the actual pack.
Never disable LiPo protection merely to continue a run.

## Thermal protection

An ESC converts some electrical energy into heat.
High current makes heating especially important.

Contributors can include:

- repeated hard acceleration
- unsuitable gearing
- a stalled or overloaded motor
- binding drivetrain parts
- weak or damaged connectors
- poor airflow
- blocked fan or heat sink
- high surrounding temperature

**Thermal protection** may reduce or stop output when the controller reaches its programmed condition.

Repeated activation is a fault to diagnose.
It is not a normal temperature-control strategy.

## Signal-loss and over-current protection

Some ESCs also document responses to missing throttle signal or excessive current.
The exact trigger, delay, output and recovery behaviour vary.

Record four things for each documented protection:

- what triggers it
- what the ESC does
- how the driver recognises it
- what must happen before operation resumes

Never test a protection by deliberately creating a short circuit, stall, overheat or over-discharged battery.
Use paper fault cards now and safe manufacturer-approved tests later.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Commissioning Mystery

Ask another person to choose one hidden fault card:

- brushless-only ESC paired with a brushed motor
- wrong battery chemistry mode
- receiver lead placed in the wrong channel
- throttle trim moved away from neutral
- forward/brake mode when reverse is expected

- symptom

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Diagnose Before You Reset

An unexplained stop is evidence.

Immediately changing settings or reconnecting the battery can erase the clues.

Record first:

- what the driver commanded
- what moved and what did not
- LED or tone pattern
- battery state
- whether steering still responded
- ESC, motor and connector condition
- what happened immediately before the fault

Then use the six checks.

A receiver restart during throttle may point towards BEC supply or wiring.
A motor that brakes but will not reverse may point towards running-mode logic.
A motor that surges from a tiny trigger movement may point towards calibration or transmitter settings.

These are hypotheses, not verdicts.
Change one thing and test again.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Choosing by the Largest Amp Number

One impressive figure cannot prove motor type, battery range, BEC capability or cooling suitability.
Check the complete rating chain and the conditions beside every number.

## Mistake 2 - Treating Peak as Continuous

Peak and continuous ratings refer to different durations and conditions.
Compare like with like and mark missing conditions as unknown.

## Mistake 3 - Assuming a Matching Plug Means a Matching System

A plug can fit while polarity, voltage, current capability or motor technology is wrong.
Use the exact wiring diagram and ratings before connection.

# Topic Summary 📝

- An ESC joins a low-power throttle-command path to a high-current motor-energy path.
- PWM changes average motor drive by changing the fraction of each switching cycle spent on.
- Brushed and brushless motor systems require different ESC output arrangements.
- Motor type, battery range, current conditions, BEC, connectors, cooling and modes form one compatibility chain.
- Peak current and continuous current must never be compared as if they mean the same duration.
- A BEC supplies regulated power to the receiver and servo in many RC systems.
- An overloaded or unsuitable control supply can cause a receiver brownout or reset.
- Calibration teaches the ESC the radio system's throttle reference positions.
- The exact calibration, arming and status sequence comes from the exact equipment manual.
- Running mode controls how braking, reverse and drag brake behave.
- LVC helps protect a correctly configured LiPo system from over-discharge.
- Thermal and other protection responses are clues, not normal operating targets.
- Type, supply, signal, neutral, mode and protection form a repeatable diagnostic route.

# New Words 📖

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Battery eliminator circuit (BEC) | A circuit that supplies regulated power to receiver-side electronics from the main battery system |
| Brake force | The strength of the motor-resisting action requested during braking |
| Brownout | A temporary voltage drop that can reset control electronics |
| Continuous current | Current claimed for continuing operation under stated conditions |
| Drag brake | Braking applied at or near neutral in some ESC modes |
| Duty cycle | The fraction of one switching cycle spent on |
| Electronic commutation | Timed switching of brushless motor phases by electronic control |
| Electronic speed controller (ESC) | A controller that interprets throttle commands and regulates motor power |
| Low-voltage cut-off (LVC) | A protection that reduces or stops output at a configured low battery condition |
| Peak current | Current allowed only briefly under stated conditions |
| Pulse-width modulation (PWM) | Control by rapid switching with adjustable on-time |
| Running mode | The configured rules for forward, braking, reverse and related behaviour |
| Thermal protection | A response that reduces or stops output at a configured high-temperature condition |
| Throttle calibration | Recording the radio commands that represent the ESC's throttle reference positions |

# Looking Ahead 🔭

The ESC can now interpret a throttle command and regulate electrical power.

# Answers 🔑

1. The receiver carries low-power control information and supplies small control devices. The drive motor needs a separate high-current energy path through an ESC designed for that load.
2. The low-power throttle-command path and the high-current battery-to-motor energy path meet at the ESC. A regulated BEC supply may also leave the ESC towards the receiver and servo.
3. The on-time rises from two of ten equal slices to eight of ten. Average commanded motor drive therefore increases in the simplified model, although real motor speed is not guaranteed to match that percentage exactly.
4. A brushed motor performs commutation mechanically inside the motor, so two external motor leads are common. A brushless ESC must energise several motor phases electronically in sequence, so three phase leads are common.
5. They refer to different durations and rating conditions. Treating a short peak allowance as a continuing capability can overload and overheat the controller.
6. In many systems, the BEC supplies regulated electrical power to the receiver and servo from the main battery system.
7. A heavy or binding servo load can pull more current than the control supply can provide. The voltage may fall and reset the receiver, producing twitching or temporary loss of control even when the radio signal itself is present.
8. Calibration records which received commands represent the required throttle reference positions, such as full forward, neutral and full brake or reverse request.
9. ESCs differ in buttons, trigger order, automatic detection, lights, tones and transmitter settings. A sequence copied from another model may be wrong or unsafe.
10. Its running mode may require the first reverse request to brake the moving buggy, followed by neutral and another request before reverse begins.
11. Stop operating, preserve and record the status indication, disconnect and inspect according to the manuals, then diagnose the underlying battery, load, gearing, cooling, wiring or setup cause. Do not repeatedly restart or disable protection to continue.
12. Type, supply, signal, neutral, mode and protection.
