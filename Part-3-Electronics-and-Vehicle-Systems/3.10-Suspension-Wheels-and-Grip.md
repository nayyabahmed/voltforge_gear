# Topic 3.10 - Suspension, Wheels and Grip

> **A tyre can only drive, brake or steer while the suspension helps it maintain useful contact with the ground.**

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- explain the different jobs of springs and dampers
- distinguish ride height, ground clearance, droop and suspension travel
- trace forces from the tyre contact patch into the chassis
- explain how alignment, surface and load influence usable grip
- design fair settle, clearance and grip tests
- build and test a keepable suspension-and-grip station

# Before We Begin

The buggy leaves the start line cleanly.
Its drivetrain delivers torque and its steering points both front tyres through the corner.

Then it crosses one small wooden strip.

The chassis bounces twice.

One front tyre skips away from the ground.

The buggy slides wide even though the transmitter command has not changed.

On the next run, someone fits very stiff springs and winds the collars down.

The chassis now sits high and hardly moves on the bench.

It also loses grip more sharply on the same uneven corner.

The fault is not simply “the suspension is too soft”.

We need evidence about what each part is doing.

This final Part 3 investigation uses six checks:

1. **Contact** - does each tyre remain usefully connected to the surface?
2. **Spring** - is load supported while movement remains available?
3. **Damping** - does bouncing settle instead of repeating?
4. **Travel** - can the wheel move without topping out, bottoming out or colliding?
5. **Alignment** - does the wheel keep a suitable direction through movement?
6. **Grip** - can the tyre generate the force demanded on this surface?

At the end, you will combine a moving suspension corner, a settle test and a grip sled into one evidence station.

# Check 1 - Protect the Contact

Imagine running across a bumpy field in shoes with rigid wooden soles.

Every ridge knocks your whole body upwards.

Bend your knees and ankles, and your feet can follow more of the ground while your body moves less.

A buggy's suspension performs a similar isolation job.

The small region where a tyre meets the ground is the **contact patch**.

Driving force, braking force and cornering force all pass through it.

The route is:

```text
surface -> tyre -> wheel -> hub and bearings -> knuckle or upright -> suspension links -> chassis
```

The route also works in reverse when the chassis pushes the tyre into the surface.

Suspension is not decoration between wheel and chassis.

It controls how those forces are carried while allowing relative movement.

A useful system must:

- support the buggy's load
- let wheels move over bumps and into dips
- control repeated bouncing
- preserve safe clearances
- keep wheel alignment within a useful range
- allow the tyre to suit the surface

# Check 2 - Let the Spring Store Energy

Press down on a plastic ruler hanging over the edge of a desk.

It bends, pushes back and springs upwards when released.

It temporarily stores energy in its deformation.

A **spring** is an elastic part that supports load and stores energy when compressed or stretched.

In an RC suspension, a coil spring commonly sits around the damper body.

As the wheel rises, the mechanism compresses the spring.

The spring then pushes the suspension back towards its supported position.

A stiffer spring needs more force for the same change in length.

The **spring rate** describes that force-per-distance relationship.

For a simple linear spring:

```text
spring force = spring rate x compression
F = kx
```

The model is most useful over the spring's intended working range.

## Worked example - compare spring force

**Question:** A spring rate is `0.8 N/mm`.

What ideal force does it produce after `10 mm` of compression?

**Known values:**

```text
k = 0.8 N/mm
x = 10 mm
```

**Rule:**

```text
F = kx
```

**Calculation:**

```text
F = 0.8 x 10
F = 8 N
```

**Meaning:**

In the simple model, the spring pushes back with `8 N` at that compression.

If the same spring compresses another `5 mm`, the force rises by another `4 N`.

The calculation does not by itself predict wheel force because suspension leverage also matters.

## Spring rate is not preload

Imagine placing a book on a bathroom scale before you step on it.

You changed the starting load, not the internal design of the scale.

**Preload** is initial spring compression already present at the chosen suspension position.

On many RC buggies, a threaded collar changes preload and therefore changes the settled ride height.

For an ideal linear spring, turning the collar does not change the spring's rate.

It changes where the suspension sits within its available movement.

That can reduce available droop or alter how much compression remains.

A different spring rate requires a different spring or a different effective geometry.

# Check 3 - Make the Bounce Settle

A spring stores energy and gives it back.
That is useful for supporting the buggy.

It can also create repeated bouncing.

Think about a playground swing.

Without friction or a person dragging their feet, it keeps passing through the middle and rising again.

A suspension needs a way to resist movement and reduce that oscillation.

A **damper** resists motion and changes movement energy into heat.

In many hobby RC dampers, a piston moves through oil.

Oil flows through holes or around passages in the piston.

That flow resistance slows compression and rebound.

The complete unit is often called a shock absorber or shock.

Keep the two jobs clear:

| Part | Main job | What happens without enough of it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spring | supports load and stores energy | suspension sags or cannot return |
| Damper | controls movement rate and dissipates oscillation energy | repeated bouncing continues |

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Press a flexible ruler down and release it.
>
> Repeat while lightly touching one finger against its side.
>
> Which test changes the ruler's springiness, and which simply removes movement energy more quickly?

The ruler remains the same spring in both tests.

Your finger adds resistance, so the oscillation settles sooner without turning the ruler into a different spring.

Too little damping allows repeated bounce.

Too much damping can prevent the wheel responding quickly enough to closely spaced bumps.

The useful amount depends on:

- spring rate
- vehicle mass
- suspension leverage
- piston design
- oil viscosity
- temperature
- bump size and spacing
- driving surface

Do not copy an oil number without matching the damper, piston, temperature and vehicle.

Real oil-filled dampers are model-specific assemblies.

Do not open, fill or bleed one unless a responsible adult follows the correct manual with you.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Settle-Time Challenge

## Question

How does movement resistance change the number of bounces after one repeatable release?

## Materials

- flexible ruler or card strip
- books or tape to hold one end
- small paper marker
- folded paper tab for gentle rubbing contact

## Safety

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult the setup before starting and work with them nearby.
>
> Keep faces and fingers away from the free end of a bent ruler.
>
> Use only a small deflection and never launch objects from it.

## Fair test

**Independent variable:** no rubbing tab or one light rubbing tab.

**Dependent variable:** number of centre-line crossings before the motion becomes too small to see.

**Controls:** same ruler, overhang, start mark, release method and viewing position.

## Procedure

1. Mark the ruler's resting position.
2. Mark one small safe release position.
3. Pull to the mark and release without pushing.
4. Count centre-line crossings.
5. Repeat three times.
6. Add the light paper rubbing tab.
7. Repeat from the same mark three times.
8. Compare the median result.

## Evidence

The rubbing tab is a friction analogy for damping.

It is not a model of oil flow or a real damper specification.

If the results overlap, improve the release method before making a stronger claim.

# Check 4 - Use the Available Travel

Open a drawer halfway.

Its position above the floor is not the same thing as how far it can still move.

Suspension measurements also name different ideas.

**Ride height** is the settled chassis height above a stated surface and datum.

**Ground clearance** is the smallest useful gap beneath the vehicle at a stated condition.

**Suspension travel** is the available wheel movement between its limits.

**Droop** is downward wheel movement from a reference position, commonly the settled position or a defined setup condition.

**Compression travel** is upward wheel movement remaining before the intended upper limit.

**Bottoming out** occurs when the suspension or chassis reaches its lower usable limit under compression.

**Topping out** occurs when downward travel reaches its extension limit.

These terms must include a datum and condition.

“The ride height is 22 mm” is incomplete unless the measurement point and settling method are known.

On a real buggy, use the manufacturer's method.

A common repeatable process is to compress the suspension, release it, let it settle and then measure at named chassis points.

Do not lift one corner into place by hand and call that the ride height.

## The movement envelope returns

Topic 3.9 checked tyre and driveshaft clearance through steering and suspension together.

That movement envelope is still required here.

At each wheel, inspect:

- tyre to chassis
- tyre to body
- arm to chassis
- damper shaft and body limits
- spring seating
- driveshaft plunge and joint angle
- steering-link clearance
- wiring near moving parts

An adult-led real check begins unpowered.

Move one corner slowly by hand.

Stop at the first resistance or contact.

Do not use the motor or servo to force past it.

# Sprung and Unsprung Mass

Carry a backpack while walking over a low step.

Your body is supported above your bending legs.

Your shoes and lower legs must move more directly with the ground.

Vehicle mass can be divided in a similar useful way.

**Sprung mass** is the mass mainly supported by the suspension springs.

It includes much of the chassis, battery and electronics.

**Unsprung mass** moves more directly with the wheel.

It includes tyres, wheels and parts of the hub, knuckle, driveshaft and suspension links.

The boundary is approximate because some parts span both sides.

Lower unsprung mass can help a wheel respond to surface changes.

It does not justify weakening hubs or using unsuitable wheels.

Strength, stiffness, durability and cost remain requirements.

# Check 5 - Preserve Useful Alignment

The wheel does more than rise and fall.

Its orientation may change through travel and steering.

Topic 3.9 introduced toe and bump steer.

Viewed from the front, **camber** is the wheel's lean from vertical.

Negative camber means the top of the wheel leans towards the chassis.

Positive camber means the top leans away.

Static camber is measured at a stated ride height.

**Camber gain** is camber change as the suspension moves.

Some change can help a tyre keep a useful orientation while the chassis rolls.

Too much or uncontrolled change can reduce contact and make behaviour inconsistent.

Wheel alignment is a system:

- toe affects direction viewed from above
- camber affects lean viewed from the front
- steering changes both front-wheel directions
- suspension travel can change toe and camber
- free play can make all measurements wander

Settle the vehicle before measuring.

Use rigid references where possible.

Change one adjustment at a time.

Return to the model's baseline if the evidence becomes confusing.

# Check 6 - Ask the Tyre for Only the Grip It Has

Rub a shoe gently across a clean floor.

Try the same shoe on a dusty tray or smooth plastic sheet.

The shoe has not changed, but the available friction has.

**Grip** is the usable force the tyre can generate against the surface before excessive sliding occurs.

For driving, this forward grip is often called **traction**.

Grip depends on a system, not one headline number:

- tyre compound
- tread pattern and depth
- insert or foam support
- tyre temperature and condition
- surface material
- dust, water and contamination
- normal load on the tyre
- wheel alignment
- suspension movement
- demanded acceleration, braking or cornering force

A wider tyre does not automatically produce more grip in every condition.

It may change pressure distribution, temperature, deformation, mass and tread behaviour.

Only a controlled test on the relevant surface can answer the actual question.

## Grip has a limit

Imagine sharing a small tray between three jobs.

If one job uses most of the space, less remains for the others.

A tyre also has limited combined force available.

Hard acceleration uses much of it for driving.

Hard steering uses much of it for cornering.

Asking for both at once can exceed the available grip.

The result is wheelspin, understeer, oversteer or a slide.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Push an eraser lightly across a table in a straight line.
>
> Now push it forward while also trying to move it sideways.
>
> Is it easier to keep one clean direction or demand two strong directions together?

The same contact must provide both force components.

When their combined demand exceeds what the surface can support, sliding begins.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - Relative Grip Sled

## Question

Which sample surface requires the greatest repeatable pull before the same sled begins to slide?

## Materials

- small piece of card
- identical coins or washers as mass
- one rubber band or strip of elastic already available
- paper ruler with equal marks
- three safe surface samples, such as paper, corrugated card and a clean tray
- tape

Do not test on furniture that could be marked.

## Safety

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult the surfaces and setup before starting and work with them nearby.
>
> Keep the elastic close to the table and away from faces.
>
> Stop if the elastic is cracked, damaged or likely to snap.

## Build

1. Fold a flat card sled.
2. Tape the same small mass securely at its centre.
3. Attach the rubber band to the front.
4. Place a paper scale beside the band.

## Fair test

**Independent variable:** surface sample.

**Dependent variable:** rubber-band extension at the first continuous slide.

**Controls:** same sled, mass, elastic, pull angle, starting position, pull rate and test distance.

## Procedure

1. Place the sled on Surface A.
2. Pull slowly and horizontally.
3. Mark the extension when continuous sliding begins.
4. Repeat five times from a fresh position.
5. Use the median mark.
6. Repeat for Surfaces B and C.
7. Rank the relative results.

## Limit of the model

Rubber-band extension is not a calibrated force unless the elastic is characterised.

It supports a relative comparison only when the same band and method are used.

A spring scale is an equipment upgrade, not a requirement.

# Weight Transfer - The Load Changes During Motion

Hold a shopping bag close to your body.

Step forward quickly.

You feel your feet manage a changing load even though the bag remains in your hands.

When a buggy accelerates, brakes or corners, tyre loads change.

This is **load transfer**, often called weight transfer in vehicle setup.

During forward acceleration, rear tyre load tends to increase while front tyre load decreases.

During braking, front tyre load tends to increase.

During a left turn, the right-side tyres generally carry more load.

The mass does not need to slide across the chassis for load transfer to occur.

Forces acting through the centre of mass and contact patches create moments that redistribute tyre loads.

The amount and rate depend on:

- acceleration
- centre-of-mass height
- wheelbase or track width
- suspension stiffness and damping
- anti-roll behaviour
- tyre and surface response

Load transfer can change available grip at each tyre.

It does not create extra total grip for free.

A balanced buggy manages the change predictably.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Clearance and Contact Map

## Question

Can one suspension corner move through its intended range without losing contact or colliding?

## Materials

- two card strips
- folded-paper pivots
- one card wheel
- paper spring strip or gentle rubber band
- pencil
- scrap-paper bump profile

## Method

1. Build one moving arm and wheel beside a fixed chassis card.
2. Mark droop, ride height and compression.
3. Trace the wheel outline at each position.
4. Add full-left and full-right outlines if the wheel also pivots.
5. Slide a low paper bump under the wheel.
6. Watch whether the wheel can follow it while the chassis card stays steadier.
7. Mark any collision or loss of contact.

## Evidence

Your map shows a movement envelope and contact sequence.

It does not prove strength, damper oil choice or high-speed grip.

Press the chassis down by a small repeatable amount.

Remove the resistance tab.

Repeat three times.

## Step 13 - Run the damping comparison

Fit Resistance 1 and repeat.

Fit Resistance 2 and repeat.

## Step 14 - Compare Springs A and B

Use the same chassis mass, release mark and resistance setting.

Record settled pointer position and travel remaining.

## Step 15 - Build the grip sled

Fold a small flat sled.

Secure the same light test mass at its centre.

Attach one gentle rubber band or thread for pulling.

## Step 16 - Add three surface lanes

Use safe scrap surfaces approved by the supervising adult.

Label each material and condition.

## Step 17 - Run the relative-grip test

Keep mass, pull angle, elastic and pull speed controlled.

Record the extension mark at first continuous slide.

Repeat five times per lane.

## Step 18 - Add six fault cards

Make cards labelled:

- spring without damping
- excessive resistance
- raised preload mistaken for spring rate
- no compression travel
- alignment change through travel
- two variables changed together

Ask someone to fit one fault card and alter the station to match.

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Tune a System, Not a Part

A suspension change affects more than one result.

Raising ride height may change:

- available droop and compression
- driveshaft angle
- steering bump steer
- centre-of-mass height
- load transfer behaviour
- body and tyre clearance

Changing a spring may change:

- settled position
- response to a bump
- chassis roll and pitch
- damper match
- load variation at the tyre

Changing a tyre may change:

- grip
- wheel mass
- effective gearing through diameter
- clearance
- steering load
- suspension response

That is why engineers use setup sheets.

Record the baseline, change one variable and state the test condition.

“It feels better” becomes useful only when “better” is tied to a requirement and repeatable evidence.

# Cheapest Valid Prototype

The card suspension-and-grip station costs `£0` using household scraps.

It is cheaper to discover a missing movement envelope in card than after a chassis has been printed.

# What to Buy, Reuse and Make

## Reuse

- sound donor suspension arms and uprights
- straight hinge pins
- free-moving bearings
- leak-free dampers that match the donor system
- known springs in labelled pairs
- wheels and tyres in safe condition
- original spacers and setup sheets

## Make

- chassis mounting points
- clearance envelope templates
- ride-height gauge
- alignment pointer or setup board
- setup ledger
- protective routing for nearby wires

## Buy only after measuring

- matched replacement springs of a known rate or manufacturer code
- correct damper seals, pistons and oil specified for the assembly
- wheels with the required diameter, width, offset and hex interface
- tyres and inserts suitable for the intended surface
- hinge pins, bearings or joints that match measured interfaces

Do not buy a mixed bag of tuning parts before the baseline buggy exists.

A setup option is useful only when there is a controlled test for it.

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Settle response

**Question:** Which resistance setting lets the model return quickly without continuing to bounce or sticking away from centre?

**Independent variable:** damper-analogy resistance setting.

**Dependent variables:** centre-line crossings and return error.

**Control variables:** spring, mass, release displacement, structure and release method.

**Procedure:** perform five releases per setting from the same mark and use the median crossing count.

**Pass condition:** the model settles within the chosen count and returns inside the pre-marked position band.

## Test 2 - Travel and clearance

**Question:** Does the wheel move through the intended envelope without collision or exhausted travel?

**Independent variable:** suspension and steering position.

**Dependent variables:** minimum clearance and remaining movement at ride height.

**Control variables:** wheel card, chassis, pivots, ride-height datum and measurement method.

**Procedure:** inspect droop, ride height and compression at straight, left and right steering positions.

**Pass condition:** no part enters a no-go zone, no joint locks and the selected ride height retains both droop and compression movement.

## Test 3 - Relative surface grip

**Question:** Which surface requires the greatest relative pull before the same sled slides continuously?

**Independent variable:** surface sample.

**Dependent variable:** elastic extension mark at first continuous slide.

**Control variables:** sled, mass, elastic, pull angle, pull rate, starting position and surface cleanliness.

**Procedure:** perform five trials per surface and compare median marks.

**Pass condition:** repeated marks form a reasonably tight group and the conclusion is limited to the tested sled, surfaces and conditions.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Calling the Spring a Damper

The spring supports load and returns stored energy.

The damper resists movement and reduces oscillation.

>
> *Figure 3.10.7 - A coil spring and damper often sit together, but they do not perform the same job.*

## Mistake 2 - Treating Preload as a Stiffer Spring

Preload changes initial compression and commonly changes settled position.

It does not change the rate of an ideal linear spring.

> **[F3 right-versus-wrong pair - Figure 3.10.8: wrong panel turns a preload collar down and claims a steeper force-versus-compression line. Right panel shows the same parallel spring-rate line beginning from a different preload point, plus two chassis ride heights and changed droop/compression balance. The reader should notice unchanged slope but changed starting position.]**
>
> *Figure 3.10.8 - Preload moves the working point on the same ideal spring-rate line rather than changing its slope.*
>
> *Alt text: An incorrect changed spring-rate graph is compared with correct equal-slope graphs showing preload changing the starting point.*

## Mistake 3 - Measuring Ride Height Before Settling

Lifting or pressing one corner leaves friction and free play approached from different directions.

Use one repeatable settle procedure and named datum.

> **[F3 right-versus-wrong pair - Figure 3.10.9: wrong panel lifts one corner by hand and measures immediately at an unnamed chassis edge. Right panel compresses front and rear together, releases, waits for settling and measures a labelled flat chassis datum on a level board. Three repeated readings are shown. The reader should notice controlled preparation before the ruler appears.]**
>
> *Figure 3.10.9 - A ride-height number becomes comparable only when the settling method and datum are repeated.*
>
> *Alt text: An arbitrary one-corner ride-height check is compared with a repeatable full-chassis settle and named measurement point.*

## Mistake 4 - Making Damping Extremely Heavy

Resistance that stops visible bounce on the bench can also stop the wheel following rapid bumps.

Judge settling and contact together.

> **[F3 right-versus-wrong pair - Figure 3.10.10: wrong panel shows an almost locked damper holding the wheel above a second closely spaced bump while the chassis appears calm. Right panel shows controlled movement over both bumps with the tyre path following the surface and the chassis trace settling. Identical speed arrows and bump profiles appear in both.]**
>
> *Figure 3.10.10 - The calmest-looking chassis is not useful if excessive damping prevents the tyre following the surface.*
>
> *Alt text: An over-damped wheel losing contact over repeated bumps is compared with controlled suspension movement maintaining contact.*

## Mistake 5 - Assuming a Wider Tyre Always Has More Grip

Grip depends on tyre, surface, load, temperature, alignment and condition together.

Test the requirement rather than judging width alone.

> **[F3 right-versus-wrong pair - Figure 3.10.11: wrong panel crowns a wide smooth dusty tyre as maximum grip based only on width. >
> *Figure 3.10.11 - Tyre width is one design variable, not a universal grip ranking.*

## Mistake 6 - Changing Springs, Dampers and Tyres Together

Several simultaneous changes may alter the result, but they hide the cause.

Record a baseline and change one variable at a time.

# Topic Summary 📝

- The tyre contact patch carries driving, braking and cornering forces.
- Springs support load and store energy; dampers control movement and reduce oscillation.
- Spring rate and preload are different ideas.
- Ride height describes settled position, while travel describes available movement.
- Droop, compression, bottom-out and top-out need clear reference conditions.
- Sprung and unsprung mass respond differently to surface movement.
- Toe, camber and bump steer connect suspension geometry to wheel alignment.
- Grip depends on the tyre, surface, load, alignment and demanded force together.
- Driving and cornering share the grip available at one contact patch.
- Dynamic load transfer changes the load carried by individual tyres.
- One-variable tests and setup records turn tuning into evidence.
- Part 3 is complete when the systems can be explained, measured and safely prepared for integration.

# Answers 🔑

1. One route is surface, tyre contact patch, tyre, wheel, hub and bearings, knuckle or upright, suspension links and chassis.
2. A spring supports load and stores energy as it deforms. A damper resists movement and dissipates oscillation energy, commonly as heat in the fluid and structure.
3. `F = kx = 0.6 x 15 = 9 N`, so the simple linear model predicts `9 N`.
4. Preload changes the spring's initial compression and operating position. For an ideal linear spring, the force-versus-compression line keeps the same slope, so the rate is unchanged.
5. Ride height is the settled chassis height at a named datum. Ground clearance is the smallest useful gap beneath the vehicle at a stated condition. Suspension travel is the wheel movement available between intended limits.
6. Bottoming out is reaching the lower usable limit during compression. Topping out is reaching the extension limit during downward wheel movement.
7. If movement resistance is too large, the wheel may not rise and return quickly enough to follow successive bumps. The tyre can then lose useful contact even if the chassis appears calm.
8. Camber is wheel lean from vertical viewed from the front. Toe is the direction paired wheels point towards or away from each other when viewed from above.
9. Factors include tyre compound, tread, insert, temperature, condition, surface material, contamination, tyre load, alignment, suspension movement and demanded force. Any six earn the answer.
10. Acceleration forces acting through the vehicle's centre of mass and tyre contact patches create a pitching moment. That redistributes tyre loads even when every component remains fixed to the chassis.
11. The station can compare relative movement, settling, clearance, alignment change and surface pull under controlled model conditions. It cannot prove real spring rate, oil-damper behaviour, tyre compound performance, structural strength or high-speed handling.
12. Contact, spring, damping, travel, alignment and grip.
