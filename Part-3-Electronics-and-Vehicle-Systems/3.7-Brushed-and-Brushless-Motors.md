# Topic 3.7 - Brushed and Brushless Motors

> **"A motor makes rotation by changing magnetic forces at the right time; the difficult part is controlling heat while producing useful torque and speed."**

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- identify a motor's rotor, stator, shaft, bearings and windings
- compare brushed, sensorless brushless and sensored brushless systems
- explain how commutation maintains rotation
- calculate an idealised no-load speed from voltage and kV
- explain back EMF, stall current, current-squared heating and gearing load
- select a motor from electrical, mechanical and thermal requirements
- build and test a card commutation model

# Before We Begin

Bring two magnets close with matching poles facing.
They push apart.
Turn one around and they pull together.

A motor arranges magnetic fields so attraction and repulsion create a turning effect.
It then changes the field sequence before the rotor settles.

That timed change is **commutation**.
The motor in our buggy must repeat it thousands of times while surviving current, vibration and heat.

# Motor Anatomy

The stationary part is the **stator**.
The rotating part is the **rotor**.
Coils of wire form **windings** that become electromagnets when current flows.

Bearings support the shaft.
The can or case carries structure and helps transfer heat.

> **[Signature visual: brushed motor and brushless motor cutaways side by side. Brushed has permanent-magnet stator, wound rotor, commutator and brushes. Brushless has wound stator, permanent-magnet rotor and three phase leads to ESC. Rotating magnetic-field arrows show the shared principle.]**

# Brushed Motors

A brushed DC motor commonly uses:

- two external wires
- carbon brushes
- a segmented commutator
- windings on the rotor
- permanent magnets on the stator

The brushes press against the rotating commutator and switch current mechanically.

Advantages include simple control and low entry cost.
Limits include brush wear, electrical noise, friction and maintenance.

# Brushless Motors

A brushless motor normally uses:

- three phase wires
- permanent magnets on the rotor
- windings on the stator
- electronic commutation in the ESC

Removing brushes can improve efficiency, speed capability and life.
It also requires the correct brushless ESC.

| Feature | Brushed | Brushless |
|---|---|---|
| Commutation | Brushes and commutator | ESC switching |
| Main wires | Usually two | Usually three |
| Wear item | Brushes/commutator | Bearings remain wear items |
| Typical cost | Lower | Higher |
| Control | Brushed ESC | Brushless ESC |

# Sensored and Sensorless Brushless Systems

A **sensorless** ESC infers rotor position from electrical behaviour.
A **sensored** system uses sensors and a cable to report rotor position.

Sensored control can improve smooth low-speed response.
Sensorless systems are simpler and common.

Motor, ESC and sensor wiring must be explicitly compatible.

# Torque, Speed and Power

Torque is turning effect.
Angular speed describes how quickly the shaft rotates.

Mechanical power is:

```text
Power = torque x angular speed
```

A motor can produce high torque slowly or lower torque quickly and still transfer similar power.
The drivetrain trades speed and torque again in Topic 3.8.

# RPM and kV

Motor speed is often stated in revolutions per minute, or **RPM**.

Brushless RC motors often carry a **kV rating**.
It estimates no-load revolutions per minute per applied volt.
It does not mean kilovolts.

```text
No-load speed estimate = kV x voltage
```

## Worked Example

A fictional `3000 kV` motor is supplied at `7.4 V` nominal.

```text
speed = 3000 x 7.4
speed = 22,200 RPM
```

This is an idealised no-load estimate.
Real speed is lower under load and changes with actual battery voltage.

Higher kV is not automatically faster on the track.
It may draw more current, produce less torque per ampere and require different gearing.

# Back EMF

A rotating motor also behaves partly like a generator.
It creates a voltage opposing the applied voltage called **back electromotive force**, or **back EMF**.

As speed rises, back EMF rises and limits current.
At stall, speed and back EMF fall toward zero, so current can become very high.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Why is a locked motor often in more electrical danger than a freely spinning motor even though the locked shaft does no useful movement?

High current becomes copper loss and heat while useful mechanical output is zero.

Never perform a deliberate stall test.

# Heat and Losses

Motor losses include:

- winding resistance
- magnetic losses
- brush friction in brushed motors
- bearing friction
- air movement
- drivetrain load

Copper heating grows approximately with current squared:

```text
P_loss = I² x R
```

Doubling current can create roughly four times the resistive heating in the same winding resistance.

Temperature limits depend on magnets, insulation, bearings and adhesives.
Use the manufacturer's limit and a suitable measurement method.

# Gearing Changes Motor Load

A large pinion can demand more wheel speed but load the motor heavily.
A smaller pinion usually reduces load and increases torque multiplication at the wheels.

If a motor overheats, gearing is one of the first system checks.
Do not assume a larger motor is the only solution.

# Motor Labels and Selection

Check:

- brushed or brushless type
- sensored compatibility
- voltage or cell-count range
- kV or turns designation
- shaft diameter and length
- can diameter and length
- mounting-hole pattern
- current and power guidance
- temperature limit
- connector type
- suitable vehicle mass and gearing guidance

Motor turns labels are not directly comparable across every design.
Use manufacturer application data.

# First Motor Test

The first real test is brief, unloaded and mechanically contained.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> A responsible adult must supervise.
> Secure the motor in a proper mount; never hold it by hand.
> Remove the pinion or disconnect it from the drivetrain.
> Use a compatible battery and ESC with conservative settings.
> Keep the shaft clear, start at minimum throttle and stop for vibration, noise, odour or heat.
> Never stall the shaft or fit an improvised propeller.

Record rotation direction, smoothness, current if suitable equipment exists, and temperature after the brief test.

# Topic Mini Project - Motor Commutation and Selection Trainer 🛠️

Build a rotating card motor with changeable brushed and brushless commutation layers.

## Materials

- card, paper, pens, scissors and glue
- paper fastener or rolled-paper pivot
- three coloured strips
- one envelope

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult what you plan to build and build with them nearby.
> Use age-appropriate scissors.
> Use no real motor, magnet, battery or wire.

> **🎬 Watch the build**
>
> - Explain That Stuff: search "electric motors" for illustrated motor principles.
> - BBC Bitesize KS3 Physics: search "electromagnets and motors".

## Build steps

1. Make a round rotor with north and south markings.
2. Mount it on a low-friction pivot.
3. Draw stator poles around it.
4. Make a rotating field selector with six positions.
5. Turn the field ahead of the rotor and demonstrate continuous torque.
6. Add a brushed overlay with two brushes and segmented commutator.
7. Add a brushless overlay with three phase cards.
8. Trace which part performs commutation in each version.
9. Add a kV calculator flap.
10. Add a heat flap showing `I²R`.
11. Add motor-envelope and shaft cards.
12. Create faults: wrong ESC type, excessive pinion, blocked shaft, unsupported motor, voltage too high and missing cooling.
13. Diagnose every fault.
14. Reflect on what the paper model cannot show: real field strength, current, speed, losses and temperature.
15. Keep the model on your showcase shelf.

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Commutation sequence

**Question:** Can the model maintain one rotation direction?

**Independent variable:** Field sequence.

**Dependent variable:** Rotor direction.

**Controls:** Same rotor and step size.

**Procedure:** Run correct and reversed sequences.

**Pass condition:** Learner predicts and demonstrates direction.

## Test 2 - Selection

**Question:** Which candidate meets every requirement?

**Independent variable:** Motor card.

**Dependent variable:** Pass/reject result.

**Controls:** Same battery, ESC, mount and drivetrain requirements.

**Procedure:** Compare three motors.

**Pass condition:** All incompatibilities are found.

## Test 3 - Heat diagnosis

**Question:** Can likely overheating causes be prioritised?

**Independent variable:** Fault scenario.

**Dependent variable:** Safe diagnostic action.

**Controls:** One fault at a time.

**Procedure:** Trace current, gearing, cooling and friction.

**Pass condition:** No scenario recommends continued hot running.

# Upgrade Path

Paper model -> unpowered donor-part measurement -> securely mounted no-load test -> drivetrain test at conservative gearing -> low-speed temperature test -> evidence-based gearing change.

# Stop Point Before the Next Purchase

Stop until motor type, ESC, battery range, kV/turns, shaft, mounting pattern, gearing, cooling and temperature plan are documented.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Reading kV as kilovolts

In this context it means RPM per volt.

## Mistake 2 - Treating no-load speed as driving speed

Load, gearing and losses reduce it.

## Mistake 3 - Choosing the highest kV

Current, heat and gearing may worsen.

## Mistake 4 - Mixing brushed and brushless controllers

Commutation methods differ.

## Mistake 5 - Holding a motor during testing

Use a proper mount.

## Mistake 6 - Deliberately stalling the shaft

Stall can cause extreme current and heat.

## Mistake 7 - Ignoring pinion size

Gearing strongly affects load.

## Mistake 8 - Continuing after overheating

Stop, cool and diagnose.

# Topic Summary 📝

- Motors create torque through changing magnetic interactions.
- Brushed motors commutate mechanically; brushless systems commutate electronically.
- Sensored systems report rotor position directly.
- kV estimates no-load RPM per volt.
- Real loaded speed is lower.
- Back EMF rises with speed and helps limit current.
- Stall removes useful rotation and can produce extreme current.
- Copper heating grows with current squared.
- Gearing, cooling and drivetrain friction affect motor temperature.
- Selection includes electrical, mechanical and thermal interfaces.

# New Words 📖

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Back EMF | Voltage produced by motor rotation that opposes applied voltage |
| Brush | A sliding electrical contact in a brushed motor |
| Commutator | Segmented rotating contact that switches current in a brushed motor |
| kV rating | Approximate no-load RPM per applied volt |
| Rotor | Rotating part of a motor |
| Sensored motor | Brushless motor with sensors reporting rotor position |
| Stator | Stationary part of a motor |
| Winding | Coil of conductor that creates a magnetic field when current flows |
