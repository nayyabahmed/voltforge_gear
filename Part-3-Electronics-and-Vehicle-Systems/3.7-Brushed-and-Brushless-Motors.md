# Topic 3.7 - Brushed and Brushless Motors

> **A motor does not win by spinning fastest on the bench. It wins by turning the buggy's wheels without cooking itself.**

# Learning Objectives 🎯
By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- explain how changing magnetic forces keep a motor turning
- compare brushed, sensorless brushless and sensored brushless motors
- interpret RPM and kV without treating them as road speed
- connect motor load, current, back EMF and heat
- check **type, supply, speed, load, fit and heat** before selecting a motor
- build and test a keepable paper motor investigation rig

# Before We Begin
The workshop buggy launches sharply from the start line.
For two seconds, it sounds fierce.
Then it slows.
The steering still works, but the motor and ESC become hot.
One learner points to the shiny label on the motor.
“It says `5000 kV`, so it must be the fast one.”
Another learner spins the drivetrain by hand and feels it dragging.
Which learner has found useful evidence?
The number on a motor cannot tell the whole story.
The motor must suit the controller, battery, gear ratio, vehicle mass, available space and cooling.
Our mission is to solve the hot-buggy mystery through six checks:

1. **Type** - how does this motor switch its magnetic fields?
2. **Supply** - are the motor, ESC and battery allowed to work together?
3. **Speed** - what does the speed label actually predict?
4. **Load** - how hard is the motor being asked to turn?
5. **Fit** - can the motor mount, shaft, pinion and wires connect correctly?
6. **Heat** - where is energy being lost, and what evidence says stop?

By the end, you will know why “highest kV wins” is not engineering.
You will also build a paper motor rig that lets another learner diagnose the same mystery.

# From a Push to Continuous Rotation
Imagine trying to pull a friend around on a swivel chair.
If you stand in one place and pull once, the chair turns towards you and stops.
To keep it rotating, you must move your pull around the chair at the right time.
A motor does something similar with magnetic pushes and pulls.
A wire carrying current produces a magnetic field.
Inside a motor, that field interacts with another magnetic field and creates a turning effect.
Remember from Topic 1.1 that **torque** is a turning effect.
The motor must change which coils are energised before the rotating part simply lines up and stops.
That timed electrical switching is called **commutation**.
Commutation is not an optional extra.
It is the repeating handover that keeps torque acting in a useful direction.

# Meet the Parts Inside a Motor
Think of a washing machine drum inside its cabinet.
The cabinet stays still while the drum rotates.
Engineers use equally useful names inside a motor.
The stationary part is the **stator**.
The rotating part is the **rotor**.
A **winding** is a coil of insulated conductor that becomes an electromagnet when current flows.
The **shaft** carries rotation out of the motor.
Bearings support the shaft while allowing it to turn with low friction.
The motor case holds the structure and provides a route for heat to leave.
These jobs exist in both brushed and brushless motors, but the parts exchange roles.
In a common brushed motor, the windings rotate and permanent magnets remain stationary.
In a common brushless RC motor, the permanent magnets rotate and the windings remain stationary.

# Check 1 - Type
The first question is not “How fast?”
It is “What motor system is this?”

## Brushed motors switch mechanically
Imagine a rotating railway points mechanism that changes a train from one track to another.
In a brushed motor, sliding contacts and copper segments redirect current as the rotor turns.
A **brush** is a sliding electrical contact.
The segmented rotating switch is the **commutator**.
Together, they perform commutation inside the motor.
A common brushed RC motor therefore has two external motor wires.
Reverse their polarity through a suitable controller and the motor can reverse direction.
Common advantages include:

- simple two-wire motor connection
- lower entry cost in many beginner systems
- straightforward low-speed control with the correct brushed ESC

Common limitations include:

- brush and commutator wear
- sliding friction
- electrical noise and tiny sparks at the contacts
- a practical speed and service-life limit set partly by the brush system

The word “brushed” describes the switching method, not the quality of the motor.

## Brushless motors switch electronically
Imagine replacing the rotating railway switch with a controller that changes each track electrically.
In a brushless motor, there are no sliding brushes performing commutation.
The brushless ESC switches current through the stationary windings in sequence.
Each separately switched winding path is a **phase**.
Many surface-RC brushless motors use three phase connections.
Common advantages include:

- no brushes to wear
- high speed capability
- good efficiency in a suitable operating range
- less maintenance at the commutator because there is no commutator

Common limitations include:

- a compatible brushless ESC is essential
- setup and wiring rules depend on the exact system
- high-performance combinations can draw damaging current very quickly
- bearings, magnets, insulation and adhesives still have limits

Brushless does not mean “cannot wear out” or “cannot overheat”.

## Sensorless and sensored brushless systems
Imagine trying to push a playground roundabout at exactly the right moment with your eyes closed.
You might infer its position from how it pushes back through your hands.
A **sensorless** brushless controller estimates rotor position from the motor's electrical behaviour.
Now imagine that a friend calls out the roundabout's position.
A **sensored** brushless motor supplies position information through sensors and a separate sensor connection.
Sensored control can provide smoother, more predictable low-speed behaviour in a compatible system.
Sensorless systems are common and use fewer connections.
Neither label guarantees that any motor will work with any ESC.
Some sensored systems require exact phase order, matching sensor wiring or a named controller.
The manuals decide.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Be the Commutator
This activity uses only paper and your hands.

## Question
Can a correctly timed field sequence keep a paper rotor turning in one chosen direction?

## Make the pieces

1. Cut or tear one paper circle for the rotor.
2. Draw an arrow across it and label the ends `N` and `S`.
3. Draw six equally spaced positions around a second sheet.
4. Make three field cards labelled `A`, `B` and `C`.
5. Add a clockwise arrow to one side and an anticlockwise arrow to the other.

## Run the sequence

1. Put the rotor circle in the centre.
2. Point field card `A` slightly ahead of the rotor.
3. Turn the rotor towards it.
4. Before the rotor fully aligns, replace `A` with `B` farther ahead.
5. Continue with `C`, then repeat.
6. Reverse the card order and observe the predicted direction.

## Evidence
Record:

- the successful card order
- the unsuccessful order
- where the rotor stopped when you changed the field too late
- one sentence explaining what performed commutation

The paper does not reproduce real magnetic field strength, electrical timing or high-speed behaviour.
It isolates the switching idea so you can reason about it safely.

# Check 2 - Supply
A plug that fits is only a physical clue.
It is not proof of electrical compatibility.
Remember the battery passport from Topic 3.3 and the ESC gates from Topic 3.6.
The motor sits inside one compatibility chain:

```text
battery voltage range -> ESC input range -> ESC motor type -> motor allowed range
```

For a candidate motor, confirm from the exact documents:

- brushed, sensorless brushless or sensored brushless type
- supported battery voltage or cell-count range
- ESC compatibility
- continuous and short-duration current or power conditions where published
- required connectors and phase order
- timing or mode requirements where published
- cooling and temperature instructions

Use the fully charged battery voltage when checking an upper electrical limit, not only its nominal label.
Do not compare a motor's current figure with an ESC number unless the definitions and time conditions match.
“Maximum”, “continuous”, “peak”, “burst” and “no-load” do not mean the same thing.
If a manufacturer sells a tested motor-and-ESC combination, that is stronger evidence than two attractive numbers from unrelated products.

# Check 3 - Speed
Imagine counting how many wheel turns a bicycle wheel makes while it is lifted off the ground.
That number does not tell you how fast the bicycle will climb a hill with a rider.
Motor speed labels need the same caution.
**RPM** means revolutions per minute.
A revolution is one complete turn.
Many brushless RC motors carry a **kV rating**.
In this context, kV means an approximate no-load speed constant in revolutions per minute per volt.
It does not mean kilovolts.
A simple estimate is:

```text
idealised no-load RPM = kV x applied voltage
```

The result is an estimate of motor-shaft speed under no load.
It is not loaded motor speed, wheel speed or road speed.

## Worked example - reading a kV label
**Question:** What idealised no-load speed does a fictional `3000 kV` motor suggest at `7.4 V`?
**Known values:**

```text
kV = 3000 RPM/V
voltage = 7.4 V
```

**Rule:**

```text
idealised no-load RPM = kV x voltage
```

**Calculation:**

```text
idealised no-load RPM = 3000 x 7.4
idealised no-load RPM = 22,200 RPM
```

**Meaning:**
The label suggests about `22,200 RPM` at the shaft under the simplified no-load condition.
It does not predict how fast the buggy will travel.
We still need the loaded motor speed, gear ratio, tyre size and losses.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Two motors fit the same buggy. One says `3000 kV`; the other says `5000 kV`. Without changing anything else, can you prove from those labels alone that the `5000 kV` buggy will complete a lap faster?

No. The higher label predicts a higher no-load speed per volt, but lap performance also depends on allowed voltage, torque production, current, gearing, acceleration, grip, cooling and whether the system slows through heat protection. A number can rank one characteristic without ranking the whole buggy.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - The kV Label Trap

## Question
Can you separate what a kV calculation proves from what it cannot prove?

## Candidate cards
Make three fictional cards:

| Card | kV | Test voltage | Other evidence |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| A | 2200 | 7.4 V | Geometry passes |
| B | 3300 | 7.4 V | Geometry passes |
| C | 5000 | 7.4 V | Allowed voltage unknown |

## Method

1. Calculate idealised no-load RPM for each card.
2. Write `SHAFT - NO LOAD` beside every result.
3. Reject Card C from selection until its allowed voltage is known.
4. List four more facts needed before predicting road speed.
5. Write one reason why the highest calculated RPM might lose a real test.

## Evidence table

| Claim | Proven by the calculation? | Further evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Approximate no-load shaft RPM | Yes, in the simplified model | Actual voltage and specification conditions |
| Loaded shaft RPM | No | Measurement under defined load |
| Wheel RPM | No | Gear ratio and loaded motor RPM |
| Road speed | No | Wheel size, slip and losses |
| Lap time | No | Acceleration, braking, cornering, temperature and driver |

This table is more valuable than the largest answer on the calculator.

# Check 4 - Load
Imagine cycling on level ground, then climbing a steep hill without changing gear.
Your legs slow and push harder.
The hill has increased the load.
A motor reacts to load through speed, torque and current.
For many permanent-magnet motors, more motor torque requires more current.
As load rises:

- shaft speed tends to fall
- current tends to rise
- copper heating tends to rise
- the motor moves closer to stall

**No-load speed** is the high-speed condition with very little external shaft load.
**Stall** is the condition where the shaft is prevented from rotating.
The current drawn at that condition is **stall current**.
Stall is not a normal operating target.
Many small motors can be damaged if held there.
Do not deliberately stall a real motor.

## Back EMF - the motor pushes back
Spin a toy wheel with your finger.
Once moving, it has a state that tells you it is no longer stationary.
A rotating motor also develops electrical evidence of its motion.
It acts partly like a generator and produces a voltage opposing the applied voltage.

This is **back electromotive force**, usually shortened to **back EMF**.

As motor speed rises, back EMF rises.

That opposing voltage helps reduce current.

At stall, speed is zero and back EMF falls towards zero.

The winding resistance then becomes a major current-limiting factor, so current can be very high.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Hold the edge of your paper rotor so it cannot turn. It now produces no movement and no useful mechanical output. Why can that zero-movement condition be more dangerous electrically than free rotation?

When a powered motor stops turning, its back EMF collapses while the supply still acts on the windings. Current can rise towards the stall value, useful mechanical power at the shaft is zero, and much of the electrical input becomes heat. Zero movement does not mean zero electrical stress.

## Gearing changes the load seen by the motor

Remember the bicycle hill.

Changing gear changes the relationship between leg speed and wheel torque.

The buggy's pinion and spur gear do the same job.

A larger motor pinion often aims for more wheel speed, but it can demand more torque from the motor and raise current and heat.

A smaller pinion usually reduces motor load and increases torque multiplication at the wheels, at the cost of some theoretical top speed.

Topic 3.8 will calculate this relationship properly.

For now, remember:

```text
motor temperature is a system clue, not only a motor clue
```

Possible causes include:

- overly demanding gearing
- binding bearings or gear mesh
- tyres rubbing the chassis
- excessive vehicle mass
- repeated hard starts and stops
- unsuitable motor or ESC settings
- restricted cooling
- operation outside the manufacturer's conditions

# Check 5 - Fit

A powerful replacement engine is useless if it cannot bolt into the vehicle or connect to its drivetrain.

The motor has mechanical interfaces as well as electrical ratings.

Before purchase, record:

- can diameter and length
- mounting-hole spacing and thread
- permitted mounting-screw depth
- shaft diameter
- usable shaft length
- shaft flat where required by the pinion
- pinion bore and fixing method
- clearance around wires and connectors
- motor removal direction
- cooling-air route

The motor's **packaging envelope** is the space needed by the component plus room for wires, cooling and removal.

Remember the suitcase analogy from Part 2.

A suitcase needs more room than its closed shell if you must open the lid and lift it out.

So does a motor installation.

A screw that is too long can reach inside a motor and damage the windings or rotor.

Use the motor manufacturer's specified screw size and maximum insertion depth.

Do not guess from the visible hole depth.

# Check 6 - Heat

Imagine carrying shopping upstairs.

Some of your energy lifts the shopping, while some warms your muscles and the air.

The motor also divides input energy between useful output and losses.

**Efficiency** compares useful mechanical output with electrical input.

No motor is perfectly efficient.

Losses include:

- electrical resistance in the windings
- brush friction and contact loss in a brushed motor
- bearing friction
- magnetic losses
- air movement
- controller switching effects

Remember from Topic 3.2 that resistive heating can be modelled as:

```text
P_loss = I² x R
```

If the same winding resistance carries twice the current, resistive heating becomes about four times as large.

## Worked example - current-squared heating

**Question:** A fictional winding resistance is `0.05 Ω`. How does its simplified copper loss change from `10 A` to `20 A`?

**Known values:**

```text
R = 0.05 Ω
case A: I = 10 A
case B: I = 20 A
```

**Rule:**

```text
P_loss = I² x R
```

**Calculation:**

```text
case A: P_loss = 10² x 0.05 = 5 W
case B: P_loss = 20² x 0.05 = 20 W
```

**Meaning:**

Current doubled, but the simplified resistive loss became four times larger.

This is why a small increase in mechanical load can create a large thermal problem.

The example models only copper loss.

It does not calculate total motor temperature.

Temperature also depends on operating time, airflow, case design, ambient temperature and how heat reaches the mount.

## Temperature evidence

There is no universal safe-touch test and no universal motor temperature limit.

Use the limit, location, method and timing stated for the exact equipment.

A real temperature test needs:

- direct adult supervision
- a defined short test run
- the manufacturer's measurement location
- a suitable measuring instrument
- a written stop limit
- cooling time before handling
- the same route, surface and starting condition when comparing changes

Stop immediately for smoke, burning smell, unusual noise, sudden loss of power, damaged wires or repeated thermal protection.

Disconnect using the approved procedure and let the equipment cool in a safe location under adult control.

Do not keep restarting a hot system to “see if it clears”.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Heat Detective

This activity uses fictional evidence cards, not powered equipment.

## Question

Can you rank likely causes of a hot motor without changing several things at once?

## Make six fault cards

Write one fault on each scrap of paper:

1. `larger pinion fitted`
2. `gear mesh became tight`
3. `rear bearing feels rough`
4. `cooling opening blocked`
5. `battery voltage changed`
6. `motor screw may be too long`

## Make six evidence cards

Write:

- loaded speed fell
- current rose
- temperature rose sooner
- drivetrain drags with power disconnected
- noise began after assembly
- rating check is incomplete

## Diagnose

1. Choose one fault card.
2. Predict which evidence could follow.
3. Name the safest first check.
4. Name one variable to keep unchanged.
5. State a stop condition.
6. Reset before choosing another fault.

## Reflection

The aim is not to guess the component to replace.

The aim is to choose the next safe observation that separates one explanation from another.

# Choosing a Motor as a System

The cheapest motor is not the one with the lowest price.

It is the lowest-cost option that meets the verified requirements without forcing unnecessary changes elsewhere.

Use this order:

| Gate | Question | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Is it brushed, sensorless brushless or sensored brushless? | Motor and ESC manuals |
| Supply | Are battery voltage and controller conditions supported? | Published ranges and matched-system guidance |
| Speed | What does kV or the speed constant predict? | Label plus worked estimate, marked no load |
| Load | Can it handle the vehicle, gearing and duty pattern? | Application guidance and conservative test plan |
| Fit | Do can, holes, screws, shaft, pinion and wires fit? | Measured drawing or physical template |
| Heat | How will temperature be limited and measured? | Manual limit, location, method and stop rule |

Reject a failed gate before comparing price or headline speed.

Unknown is not the same as pass.

Record unknown evidence honestly.

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Preserve the Fault

When the buggy becomes hot, changing the motor, pinion, timing, battery and airflow together destroys the evidence.

If the next run improves, which change helped?

You cannot know.

Use a disciplined sequence:

1. Stop at the written limit or abnormal symptom.
2. Record the exact setup and conditions.
3. Check for mechanical drag with power safely disconnected.
4. Compare the setup with the exact manuals.
5. Change one justified variable.
6. Repeat the same short test under adult supervision.
7. Compare evidence.

The aim is not to keep the buggy moving at any cost.

The aim is to learn which requirement or interface failed.

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Commutation Sequence

**Question:** Can the paper field sequence keep the rotor turning in a predicted direction?

**Independent variable:** Order of the `A`, `B` and `C` field cards.

**Dependent variable:** Direction and continuity of paper-rotor movement.

**Control variables:** Same rotor, pivot, starting position, movement step and person operating it.

**Procedure:** Run `A-B-C`, then reverse to `C-B-A`, then deliberately switch one step late.

**Pass condition:** The learner predicts both directions and explains why the late sequence stops producing useful continuous torque.

## Test 2 - Candidate Selection

**Question:** Which fictional motor candidate passes every known system requirement?

**Independent variable:** Candidate motor card.

**Dependent variable:** `PASS`, `FAIL` or `UNKNOWN` result at each gate.

**Control variables:** Same battery, ESC, buggy load, mounting envelope, gearing requirement and six questions.

**Procedure:** Move each candidate through type, supply, speed, load, fit and heat without changing the requirements.

**Pass condition:** Every incompatible candidate is rejected, every missing fact remains `UNKNOWN`, and price is compared only among candidates that pass.

## Test 3 - Hot-Motor Diagnosis

**Question:** Can one hidden fault be narrowed using evidence without powering equipment?

**Independent variable:** Hidden fault card.

**Dependent variable:** First safe check and final diagnosis.

**Control variables:** Same fictional buggy, route, battery, starting condition and diagnostic order.

**Procedure:** Reveal one symptom at a time and require the learner to record why each clue supports or weakens a hypothesis.

**Pass condition:** The learner stops at unsafe symptoms, chooses evidence before replacement and changes no more than one variable in the proposed follow-up test.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Reading kV as Kilovolts

In an RC motor label, kV commonly describes approximate no-load RPM per volt.

It is not a high-voltage unit in this context.

## Mistake 2 - Treating No-Load RPM as Road Speed

The calculation stops at the unloaded motor shaft.

Gearing, tyre size, load, losses and slip still stand between that shaft and the road.

## Mistake 3 - Choosing the Highest kV

A higher speed constant can increase current and heat or demand different gearing.

Select for the whole buggy mission.

## Mistake 4 - Mixing Motor and ESC Types

A matching connector does not prove a matching commutation system.

Confirm the exact motor–ESC relationship.

## Mistake 5 - Holding or Stalling a Powered Motor

A motor can twist, eject a loose part or draw damaging current.

Real testing needs an approved secure mount, disabled drivetrain and direct adult supervision.

Never perform a deliberate stall test.

Tight gears, rough bearings, oversized pinions, blocked airflow and repeated hard acceleration can all increase motor load.

Inspect the system before replacing the motor.

# Topic Summary 📝

- Motors produce torque through interactions between magnetic fields.
- Commutation switches the energised windings so useful torque can continue.
- A brushed motor performs commutation with brushes and a commutator.
- A brushless ESC performs electronic commutation through motor phases.
- Sensorless control infers rotor position; sensored control receives position information.
- kV is an approximate no-load speed constant in RPM per volt, not road speed.
- Higher kV does not automatically mean a faster or better buggy.
- Back EMF rises with speed and helps oppose applied voltage.
- At stall, speed and back EMF fall while current can become very high.
- Current-squared copper loss explains why load increases can produce rapid heating.
- Gearing, friction, mass, driving pattern and cooling all affect motor temperature.
- Motor selection must pass type, supply, speed, load, fit and heat checks.

# New Words 📖

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Back EMF | Voltage produced by motor rotation that opposes the applied voltage |
| Brush | A sliding electrical contact used for commutation in a brushed motor |
| Commutation | Timed switching of current so motor torque continues in a useful direction |
| Commutator | Segmented rotating switch contacted by brushes inside a brushed motor |
| Efficiency | Useful output compared with total input |
| kV rating | Approximate no-load revolutions per minute per applied volt in this RC context |
| No-load speed | Motor speed with very little external shaft load |
| Phase | One separately switched winding path in a brushless motor system |
| Rotor | The rotating part of a motor |
| Sensored | Using sensors to report rotor-position information to a compatible controller |
| Sensorless | Inferring rotor position from the motor's electrical behaviour rather than a sensor cable |
| Stall | A powered condition in which the motor shaft cannot rotate |
| Stall current | Current drawn when the powered motor is prevented from rotating under stated conditions |
| Stator | The stationary part of a motor |
| Winding | A coil of insulated conductor that produces a magnetic field when current flows |

# Answers 🔑

1. A fixed magnetic relationship would pull the rotor towards one aligned resting position. Commutation changes which windings are energised before the rotor settles, keeping torque acting in a useful direction.
2. The rotor is the rotating part of a motor. The stator is the stationary part.
3. Brushes sliding on a segmented commutator switch current through the rotating windings in a common brushed motor.
4. A compatible brushless ESC switches current electronically through the motor phases in the required sequence.
5. A sensored system receives rotor-position information from sensors through an additional connection. A sensorless system infers rotor position from the motor's electrical behaviour.
6. It means an approximate no-load speed constant of `3000 revolutions per minute per applied volt` under the relevant rating conditions. It does not mean `3000 kilovolts`.
7. It estimates only unloaded motor-shaft speed. Road speed also depends on loaded speed, gear ratio, wheel size, losses and tyre slip.
8. A rotating motor develops back EMF that opposes the applied voltage and helps limit current. At stall, speed and back EMF fall towards zero, so winding resistance must limit a potentially much larger current.
9. It becomes about four times as large if resistance remains the same, because current is squared in `P_loss = I²R`.
10. Any three valid examples include an oversized pinion, tight gear mesh, rough bearing, rubbing tyre, excessive vehicle mass or blocked cooling path.
11. Check can diameter and length, mounting-hole spacing and thread, permitted screw depth, shaft diameter and length, pinion interface, wire and connector space, cooling clearance and removal direction.
12. Type, supply, speed, load, fit and heat.
