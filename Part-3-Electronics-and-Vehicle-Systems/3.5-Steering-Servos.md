# Topic 3.5 - Steering Servos

> **"A steering servo does not simply spin. It moves to a commanded position, checks where it is, and corrects the error."**

# Before We Begin

Push a door close to its hinge.
Now push the same door near its handle.

Your hand travels farther at the handle, but it creates a larger turning effect about the hinge.
That turning effect is **torque**.

The steering servo in our VoltForge buggy faces a similar trade-off.
Its horn must move the steering linkage far enough, yet still apply enough force to turn the tyres against the ground.

A longer horn gives more linkage travel for the same angle.
It also demands more torque from the servo for the same linkage force.

That single relationship connects electronics, mechanics and geometry.

---

# What Makes a Servo Different from a Motor?

A simple motor keeps rotating while suitable power is applied.
A standard steering servo normally moves to a requested angular position and holds near it.

Inside a typical servo are:

- a small electric motor
- reduction gears
- an output shaft and spline
- a position sensor
- control electronics
- a case and mounting lugs

The **output spline** is the toothed shaft that engages the servo horn.

The **servo horn** is the removable arm or disc that transfers rotation into the linkage.

> **[Signature visual: cutaway servo. Command enters the control board, motor turns reduction gears, output spline moves horn, position sensor reports actual angle, and a feedback arrow returns to the controller. Beside it, short and long horn radii show low travel/high force and high travel/lower available linkage force.]**

---

# Closed-Loop Position Control

Think about riding a bicycle toward a painted line.
You look at your direction, compare it with the line and correct the handlebars.

That repeated compare-and-correct process is **feedback**.

In a servo:

1. the receiver sends a position command
2. the position sensor reports the actual output position
3. the controller compares requested and actual positions
4. the motor turns in the needed direction
5. the error becomes smaller
6. the servo holds near the target

This is a **closed-loop control** system because information about the output returns to the controller.

If the horn is blocked, the controller keeps detecting an error.
The motor may continue trying, drawing current and producing heat.

That is why a buzzing servo at full steering lock is not harmless sound.

---

# The Command Signal

The receiver sends a repeating control signal to the servo.
The signal represents the requested position.

Many hobby servos use pulse-width-based commands, but exact timing and acceptable ranges vary.
You do not need to generate the signal yourself.

Use a compatible receiver, servo tester or controller designed for the servo.

The three common lead functions are:

- signal
- positive supply
- return or ground

Check the actual pin order and voltage range.

---

# Angle and Steering Travel

Servo angle is the rotation available at the output shaft.
Steering travel is the movement produced at the wheels.

They are not the same quantity.

Travel depends on:

- commanded servo angle
- horn-hole radius
- linkage length
- steering-arm radius
- pivot locations
- joint clearances

For a simple small-angle picture, a point farther from the servo centre travels through a larger arc.

The arc length relationship is:

```text
arc length = radius x angle in radians
```

## Worked Example - Horn-Hole Travel

### The question

A horn hole is `12 mm` from the centre and rotates through `30°`.
What arc length does the hole travel approximately?

### Convert the angle

```text
30° = 30 x pi / 180
30° ≈ 0.524 radians
```

### Calculate

```text
arc length = 12 x 0.524
arc length ≈ 6.3 mm
```

### Meaning

The horn hole travels about `6.3 mm` along its arc.
The linkage's useful straight movement will be similar only in a suitable geometry and may differ through the travel.

---

# Torque - Turning Effect

Torque is force multiplied by perpendicular distance from a pivot.

```text
Torque = force x perpendicular distance
```

Servo torque is often quoted in `kg·cm` in hobby catalogues.
That label is convenient but is technically a kilogram-force centimetre.

For engineering calculations, newton-metres or newton-millimetres are clearer.

Approximately:

```text
1 kgf ≈ 9.81 N
```

## Worked Example - Linkage Force

A servo can provide `0.20 N·m` at a stated condition.
The horn hole is `20 mm`, or `0.020 m`, from the centre.

```text
Force = torque / radius
Force = 0.20 / 0.020
Force = 10 N
```

Ignoring losses and geometry, the theoretical tangential force is `10 N`.

Move the linkage to a `10 mm` radius:

```text
Force = 0.20 / 0.010
Force = 20 N
```

The shorter horn can apply more linkage force but moves it a shorter distance for the same angle.

Real systems lose force through angle, flexibility, friction and servo limits.

---

# Speed

Servo speed is often stated as the time required to rotate through a defined angle, such as seconds per `60°`.

A label of `0.15 s/60°` means the unloaded servo is claimed to move `60°` in `0.15 s` under the stated voltage and test conditions.

Lower time means faster movement.

Speed under load may be slower.
A fast servo does not guarantee accurate steering if the linkage bends or has free play.

---

# Voltage and Current

Servo performance depends on supply voltage.

A specification may list different torque and speed at different voltages.
Never use the higher figures without confirming that the servo, receiver and BEC all support that voltage.

A servo draws more current when:

- accelerating
- carrying more load
- correcting a disturbance
- stalled against a stop
- holding a loaded position

The BEC must supply the receiver and servo without excessive voltage drop or overheating.

A servo's **stall current** is the current drawn when it is commanded to move but cannot rotate normally.
Do not deliberately stall a servo to measure it.

---

# Servo Size and Fit

Names such as micro, mini and standard describe broad size families, not guaranteed dimensions.

Measure or obtain a drawing for:

- body length, width and height
- mounting-lug spacing
- hole diameter
- output-spline location
- horn clearance envelope
- connector and cable exit
- removal path

The case can fit while the horn collides with the chassis.
Remember the packaging envelope from Topic 1.2.

---

# Centre Before Fitting the Horn

Imagine fitting clock hands while the mechanism is showing an unknown time.
The hands may look centred but the movement has unequal range left and right.

Use this conceptual sequence:

1. remove the horn
2. set transmitter trim and sub-trim to the intended neutral
3. power the compatible radio system safely with drive disabled
4. allow the servo to move to commanded centre
5. switch off as instructed
6. fit the horn as close as possible to the designed neutral angle
7. use small trim or linkage adjustment for final correction
8. set endpoints before full movement

The exact powered steps require adult supervision and the equipment manuals.

Do not force the horn onto the wrong spline alignment.
Use the correct retaining screw.

---

# Horn Length Is a Gear-Ratio Choice

The horn and steering arm act like levers.

A longer servo horn generally gives:

- more linkage travel
- faster wheel movement for the same servo speed
- less available linkage force
- more load on the servo

A shorter servo horn generally gives:

- less linkage travel
- slower wheel movement
- more available linkage force
- easier fine control

Neither is automatically better.
Choose the shortest radius that still provides the required steering travel without binding.

---

# Linkage Geometry

A linkage transfers movement between pivots.

A good steering linkage should:

- move freely through the full range
- avoid severe angle changes
- avoid rubbing the chassis
- keep ball joints or pins seated
- allow length adjustment
- transmit push and pull without bending
- remain accessible for service

At extreme angles, some horn movement may produce less useful side-to-side travel.
This changing relationship is one reason to test the complete geometry, not only calculate one position.

---

# Free Play and Backlash

Turn a loose door handle slightly before the latch begins moving.
That lost movement is similar to **backlash** or free play.

Possible sources include:

- gear clearance inside the servo
- loose horn spline
- worn ball joints
- oversized pivot holes
- flexible steering arms
- loose mounting screws

Some clearance is necessary for movement.
Too much makes steering vague.

Measure wheel response after reversing direction, because free play is easiest to see there.

---

# Servo Saver

When a front wheel hits an obstacle, the shock can travel back through the linkage into the servo gears.

A **servo saver** is a compliant mechanism that yields under excessive steering load.
It can protect the servo while transmitting normal commands.

Too soft creates vague steering.
Too stiff provides little protection.

Use a proven servo saver rather than inventing one for a high-speed buggy.

---

# Mechanical Stops and Electronic Endpoints

Mechanical stops define the physical limit.
Electronic endpoints should stop commanded motion just before damaging contact.

The safe order is:

1. inspect the mechanism unpowered
2. identify physical stops
3. reduce transmitter endpoints
4. centre the servo
5. increase travel gradually
6. stop before binding, buzzing or tyre contact
7. test under gentle steering load

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> If the steering knuckle reaches its stop at `28°` but the servo keeps trying toward `35°`, where does the extra movement go?

It becomes deformation, gear load, current and heat.
The wheels do not gain useful angle.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Real servo tests require a responsible adult and a current-limited, compatible power arrangement.
> Keep drive power disconnected and wheels clear of the bench.
> Keep fingers away from horns, linkages and pinch points.
> Stop immediately for binding, repeated twitching, smoke, odour or unusual heat.

---

# Choosing a Steering Servo

Start with requirements, not the largest number.

| Requirement | Question |
|---|---|
| Size | Does the body and horn envelope fit? |
| Torque | Can it steer the loaded tyres with margin? |
| Speed | Is response suitable for the buggy and driver? |
| Voltage | Does it match the receiver/BEC supply? |
| Current | Can the BEC supply realistic peaks? |
| Gears | Is durability suitable for the load? |
| Spline | Are replacement horns and saver available? |
| Environment | Is dust or water protection required? |
| Serviceability | Can it be removed without dismantling the chassis? |

Metal gears may improve durability but add cost and do not prevent all failures.
Water-resistant labels have conditions and do not mean unlimited submersion.

## You will need

- card arm
- ruler
- paper pivot
- pencil

## Steps

1. Mark holes at `10 mm`, `20 mm` and `30 mm` radii.
2. Rotate the arm through the same angle.
3. trace each point's arc
4. compare travel
5. use a rubber band as a gentle load
6. compare how hard each radius feels to hold

The farther point travels more and demands more torque for the same tangential force.

# Topic Mini Project - Adjustable Servo Steering Demonstrator 🛠️

Build a working card servo horn, tie rod and steering arm.
Three horn radii will let you compare travel and force.

## Materials

- corrugated card
- scrap paper
- ruler and protractor if available
- pencil and pens
- scissors suitable for card
- tape or glue
- paper fasteners or folded-paper pivots
- string or wool
- one rubber band
- paper clips used only with adult approval, or rolled-paper pins

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult or guardian what you plan to build before starting, and build with them nearby.
> Use age-appropriate scissors and keep rubber bands away from faces.
> Cover sharp paper-clip ends or use paper pivots.
> Do not connect a real servo, battery or receiver to this model.

> **🎬 Watch the build**
>
> - Science Buddies: search "cardboard linkage" for pivot-and-link examples.
> - BBC Bitesize KS3 Design and Technology: search "mechanical systems levers and linkages".

## Step 1 - Build the base and servo case

Cut a base about `350 mm x 240 mm`.
Make a removable card rectangle representing the servo.

Mark mounting lugs, cable exit and spline centre.

## Step 2 - Make the horn

Create a stiff card arm with linkage holes at `10 mm`, `20 mm` and `30 mm` from the centre.

Mount it on a low-friction pivot.
Mark `-30°`, `0°` and `+30°`.

## Step 3 - Make the steering arm

Create a second pivoting arm representing a steering bellcrank or knuckle arm.

Add one linkage hole and a pointer showing wheel angle.

## Step 4 - Make an adjustable tie rod

Use string with folded-paper end loops.
Add several knots or holes so its length can change.

It must push and pull the steering arm without excessive slack.

## Step 5 - Add mechanical stops

Place stops at the maximum safe steering-arm angles.
Make them removable.

## Step 6 - Add endpoint flags

Place green flags before each stop.

The horn must stop at green without forcing the red mechanical limit.

## Step 7 - Add a feedback pointer

Create a second pointer connected to the horn centre.

Label one scale COMMANDED and the other ACTUAL.
When the arm is blocked, show the remaining error.

## Step 8 - Test three horn radii

Keep horn angle, steering arm and tie-rod length as controlled as possible.

For each radius, record:

- steering-arm angle
- whether a stop is reached
- visible free play
- rubber-band load that can be held

## Step 9 - Find the shortest valid radius

Choose the shortest radius that reaches the required fictional wheel angle without hitting the stop.

Explain why extra travel is not free.

## Step 10 - Test centring

Move left, release to your hand-set centre, then move right and return.

Mark the returned pointer position five times.
Measure the spread.

## Step 11 - Test backlash

Approach centre from the left and mark the output.
Approach from the right and mark it again.

The difference represents lost motion in the model.

## Step 12 - Add fault cards

Create:

- horn too long
- tie rod loose
- endpoint past stop
- horn fitted off-centre
- servo case loose
- trim hiding unequal links

## Step 13 - Reflection

Write:

1. The selected horn radius is...
2. It provides enough travel because...
3. A longer horn would change force by...
4. The model does not include a real motor, gears or electronic feedback.

## Step 14 - Keep the artifact

Store the radii results and fault cards with the model.
Keep it on your showcase shelf for Topic 3.9 steering geometry.

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Protect the Weakest Link

The servo, horn, saver, tie rod, pivots and steering arms form one force path.

Making only the servo stronger can transfer crash load into a cheaper plastic arm or chassis mount.

The system needs:

- adequate normal strength
- controlled compliance
- a replaceable sacrificial part
- accessible fasteners
- safe endpoints

Good design chooses where overload is allowed to cause repairable damage.

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Horn-radius travel

**Question:** How does horn radius affect steering output angle?

**Independent variable:** Horn-hole radius.

**Dependent variable:** Steering-arm angle.

**Control variables:** Horn rotation, tie rod, steering arm and base.

**Procedure:** Test `10`, `20` and `30 mm` radii through the same horn angle.

**Pass condition:** Select the shortest radius that reaches the required angle without binding.

## Test 2 - Repeatable centring

**Question:** Does the mechanism return to a consistent centre?

**Independent variable:** Direction of approach.

**Dependent variable:** Returned pointer position.

**Control variables:** Same model, input angle and release method.

**Procedure:** Record ten returns from alternating sides.

**Pass condition:** All marks lie within the chosen paper-model tolerance band.

## Test 3 - Endpoint clearance

**Question:** Do commanded endpoints remain clear of physical stops?

**Independent variable:** Left or right command.

**Dependent variable:** Clearance at the stop.

**Control variables:** Same linkage and endpoint flags.

**Procedure:** Approach both endpoints three times.

**Pass condition:** Useful travel is reached with visible clearance and no deformation.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Fitting the horn before centring

This produces unequal travel and difficult trim.

## Mistake 2 - Choosing the longest horn

Extra travel costs linkage force and increases servo load.

## Mistake 3 - Judging torque without the stated voltage

Performance figures must be compared under matching conditions.

## Mistake 4 - Ignoring stall current

The BEC must support realistic servo demand.

## Mistake 5 - Using trim to correct a badly fitted horn

Centre and refit mechanically first.

## Mistake 6 - Letting the servo push a hard stop

Reduce endpoints before binding occurs.

## Mistake 7 - Calling buzzing normal

Buzzing with heat or a hard stop indicates continuing load.

## Mistake 8 - Forgetting the horn clearance envelope

The body may fit while the moving horn collides.

## Mistake 9 - Removing all clearance

Joints need enough clearance to move freely.

## Mistake 10 - Holding a real horn during testing

Keep fingers away from powered pinch points.

# Topic Summary 📝

- A steering servo is a closed-loop position actuator.
- Internal gears increase output torque and reduce motor speed.
- A sensor reports actual output position to the controller.
- Remaining position error makes the servo continue correcting.
- Servo angle is not the same as wheel angle.
- Horn radius changes linkage travel and available force.
- Torque equals force multiplied by perpendicular distance.
- Speed and torque ratings depend on voltage and conditions.
- A stalled or bound servo may draw high current and overheat.
- Centre the servo before fitting the horn.
- Use mechanical geometry first, then trim and endpoints.
- A servo saver can protect gears from shock loads.
- The mount, horn, linkage, BEC and steering stops form one system.

# New Words 📖

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Backlash | Lost movement caused by clearance between connected parts |
| Closed-loop control | Control that uses output feedback to correct error |
| Feedback | Information about the result returned to the controller |

| Servo horn | An arm or disc fitted to a servo output spline |
