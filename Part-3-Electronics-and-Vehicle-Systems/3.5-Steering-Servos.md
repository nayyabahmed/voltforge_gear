# Topic 3.5 - Steering Servos

> **"The strongest steering servo can still fail if one small linkage asks it to push where nothing can move."**

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- Explain how a steering servo uses feedback to control angular position.
- Distinguish servo angle, wheel angle, speed, torque and current demand.
- Explain how horn radius changes linkage travel and available force.
- Plan centring, horn fitting, linkage routing and endpoint checks in a safe order.
- Compare servo specifications only at stated voltage and operating conditions.
- Build and fair-test a reusable cardboard servo-steering demonstrator.

# Before We Begin

The VoltForge buggy is raised safely above the bench.
Its drive system is disconnected, so the wheels cannot propel it.

The transmitter wheel rests in the middle.
An adult powers the control system using the exact equipment instructions.

The front tyres suddenly jump to the right.

At full left steering, the servo makes a strained buzz.
When the wheel returns to neutral, the tyres stop somewhere different each time.

Three symptoms are hiding three different faults:

- the horn may have been fitted away from true centre
- the command may be pushing the linkage against a hard stop
- clearance in a joint may be creating lost movement

Buying a stronger servo would not automatically repair any of them.

Your mission is to turn the radio command from Topic 3.4 into accurate, useful and repeatable steering.
You will prove six checks before any real buggy is allowed to drive:

1. **COMMAND** - Does the intended steering instruction reach the servo?
2. **CENTRE** - Is neutral established before the horn is fitted?
3. **RADIUS** - Does the chosen horn hole provide enough travel without wasting force?
4. **ROUTE** - Can the complete linkage move freely through its path?
5. **LIMIT** - Does commanded movement stop before a hard mechanical stop?
6. **PROVE** - Does steering repeat without binding, excess slack or unusual heat?

Every hands-on activity in this topic uses people, paper, card, string or rubber bands.
No real servo, receiver or battery is needed.

# The Six-Check Steering Mission

Think of passing a message along a team.
The message is useful only when the final person performs the intended action.

In our buggy, the command path ends at the servo.
The movement path begins there:

| Path | What travels? | Route |
|---|---|---|
| Command | Steering information | Transmitter → receiver → steering channel → servo electronics |
| Energy | Electrical energy | Designed supply → receiver rail → servo motor and electronics |
| Movement | Force and motion | Servo gears → output spline → horn → tie rod → steering mechanism → tyres |

Remember Topic 3.1.
A correct command path does not prove that the movement path is free.

The servo can receive a perfect instruction and still strain against poor geometry.

# A Servo Moves to a Position

A simple DC motor is like a desk fan.
Give it suitable energy and it keeps rotating.

A standard steering servo is more like your hand placing a book at a marked spot.
It moves towards a requested position, checks where it is and corrects the remaining error.

A **servo** is a controlled actuator that moves its output towards a commanded position.

Most steering servos do not spin continuously.
They rotate through a limited angle and hold near the requested position.

That makes them useful for steering:

- centre command → tyres near straight ahead
- left command → tyres turn left
- right command → tyres turn right
- partial command → tyres move to a partial angle

The servo does not know the actual wheel angle.
Its internal sensor normally measures the servo output position.

Everything after the output spline belongs to the external mechanism.
Loose, bent or wrongly sized linkage parts can therefore spoil correct internal control.

# Feedback Is the Servo's Correction Loop

Imagine riding a bicycle along a painted line.
You look ahead, notice that you are drifting and make a small handlebar correction.

That repeated compare-and-correct action is **feedback**.

Inside a typical steering servo:

1. the receiver sends a requested position
2. the position sensor reports the actual output position
3. the controller compares requested and actual positions
4. the difference becomes a **position error**
5. the motor turns through reduction gears in the needed direction
6. the error becomes smaller
7. the controller keeps correcting near the target

This is **closed-loop control** because information about the output returns to the controller.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Point one finger at a pencil mark on the table while looking at it. Now close your eyes, let someone gently move your finger, and try to correct it without looking. What information has disappeared?

The missing information is feedback about your actual position. A servo's position sensor plays the role of your eyes: without trustworthy output information, the controller cannot compare where it is with where it was asked to be.

## What happens when movement is blocked?

Hold a spring-loaded door open while someone gently asks it to close.
The request remains, but the door cannot reach the requested position.

A blocked servo also keeps detecting position error.
Its controller may continue driving the motor, so current demand and temperature can rise.

A brief sound during an ordinary correction is not the same as continuous strain at a hard stop.
Binding, persistent buzzing or unusual heat means stop and investigate.

Never deliberately stall a real servo to see what happens.

# Meet the Physical Servo

A typical surface-RC servo includes:

- a case
- mounting lugs
- a three-core lead and plug
- a small electric motor
- reduction gears
- an output shaft with a toothed **output spline**
- a position sensor
- control electronics

The removable arm or disc on the spline is the **servo horn**.
It turns limited rotary movement into motion at a linkage connection.

The **tie rod** is the adjustable link that carries push-and-pull movement towards the steering mechanism.

# The Command and Supply Are Different

The receiver output usually provides three electrical functions:

- control signal
- positive supply
- return or ground

Conductor colours and connector details can vary.
Use the markings and manuals for the actual receiver and servo.

The control signal requests a position.
It does not supply all the energy needed to push the tyres.

That energy comes through the servo's designed supply path.
In many RC systems, a battery eliminator circuit, or **BEC**, supplies regulated power to the receiver and servo.

The BEC is studied further in Topic 3.6.

Many hobby servos accept repeating pulse-width-based commands.
Exact neutral, range, frame rate and allowed endpoints are equipment-specific.

Do not assume one internet pulse value suits every servo and controller.

# Servo Angle Is Not Wheel Angle

Swing your arm through a small angle.
Your fingertip travels farther than a point near your elbow.

A servo horn behaves the same way.
A linkage hole farther from the spline travels through a longer arc for the same servo angle.

**Servo angle** is the rotation of the output spline.
**Wheel angle** is the direction of a steered wheel relative to straight ahead.

They are connected through geometry, but they are not equal.

Wheel angle depends on:

- servo angle
- selected horn-hole radius
- tie-rod length
- steering-arm radius
- pivot locations
- linkage angles
- clearances and flexibility

## Worked Example - Horn-Hole Travel

### The question

A horn hole is `12 mm` from the spline centre.
The horn rotates through `30°`.

Approximately how far does the hole travel along its arc?

### The known values

```text
radius = 12 mm
angle = 30°
```

### The rule

```text
arc length = radius × angle in radians
```

### Convert the angle

```text
30° = 30 × π / 180
30° ≈ 0.524 radians
```

### Calculate

```text
arc length = 12 × 0.524
arc length ≈ 6.3 mm
```

### Meaning

The horn hole travels about `6.3 mm` along its curved path.

The tie rod's useful side-to-side travel may differ because its angle changes during movement.
The complete mechanism still needs a physical test.

# Torque Is Turning Effect

Try opening a stiff door by pushing close to the hinge.
Now push the same door near its handle.

The force near the handle creates more turning effect because it acts farther from the pivot.

Engineers call that turning effect **torque**.

```text
torque = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot
```

Hobby catalogues often quote servo torque in `kg·cm`.
The label is convenient, but it represents kilogram-force centimetres rather than kilograms of mass.

For calculations, newton-metres or newton-millimetres are clearer.

Approximately:

```text
1 kgf ≈ 9.81 N
```

## Worked Example - Linkage Force

### The question

A servo is rated for `0.20 N·m` of torque at a stated voltage.
The linkage hole is `20 mm` from the spline centre.

What is the ideal tangential force at the hole?

### The known values

```text
torque = 0.20 N·m
radius = 20 mm = 0.020 m
```

### The rule

```text
force = torque / radius
```

### Calculate

```text
force = 0.20 / 0.020
force = 10 N
```

### Meaning

The ideal tangential force is `10 N` at the `20 mm` hole.

At a `10 mm` radius, the ideal force would be:

```text
force = 0.20 / 0.010
force = 20 N
```

The shorter radius offers more ideal linkage force but less travel for the same servo angle.

Real force is lower because linkage angle, friction, flexibility and servo conditions create losses.

# Speed, Voltage and Current Belong Together

Imagine two people carrying the same shopping upstairs.
The person who completes the job sooner delivers energy faster.

Servo **speed** is usually stated as the time needed to rotate through a named angle under stated conditions.

A label such as `0.15 s/60°` means the manufacturer reports `60°` of unloaded movement in `0.15 s` at the named voltage.
Lower time means faster rated movement.

Do not compare a speed figure at one voltage with another product's figure at a different voltage.

Voltage can affect both rated torque and speed.
The servo, receiver and BEC must all accept the selected supply voltage.

A servo draws current while its motor works.
Demand usually rises when it:

- accelerates
- pushes a larger load
- corrects a disturbance
- holds a loaded position
- is prevented from reaching the command

**Stall current** is the current drawn when the servo is commanded to move but cannot rotate normally.
It can be much higher than idle current.

Never deliberately stall a servo to measure it.
Use manufacturer data or an adult-led protected test designed for the exact equipment.

The power system must support realistic peak demand without excessive voltage drop or overheating.

# Centre Before Fitting the Horn

Imagine fitting clock hands while the mechanism shows an unknown time.
The hands may look straight, yet have unequal movement available on either side.

A servo also needs a known electrical neutral before the horn is fitted mechanically.

The universal reasoning sequence is:

1. keep the drive system disconnected
2. remove the horn
3. set the steering command to its intended neutral
4. use the exact manual to power the compatible control system safely
5. allow the servo output to settle at commanded centre
6. switch off as the instructions require
7. fit the horn as close as possible to the designed neutral angle
8. adjust linkage length for accurate mechanical centre
9. use only small trim or sub-trim for final correction
10. establish conservative endpoints before testing full travel

The exact switch order, menu names and powered steps vary by equipment.
They require a responsible adult and the actual manuals.

Do not force a horn onto a mismatched spline.
Use the correct retaining screw.

# Radius and Route Decide Useful Movement

The horn and steering arm act as levers.

A longer servo-horn radius generally produces:

- more linkage travel
- faster linkage movement for the same servo speed
- less available linkage force
- greater torque demand for the same tie-rod force

A shorter radius generally produces:

- less linkage travel
- slower linkage movement
- more available linkage force
- easier fine control

Neither choice is automatically best.

Use the shortest radius that still reaches the required wheel angle without binding.

The tie rod must also follow a free **linkage route**.
It should:

- push and pull without bending
- avoid rubbing the chassis
- avoid severe angle changes
- stay clear of suspension movement studied in Topic 3.10
- keep joints seated
- allow length adjustment
- remain accessible for service

At extreme linkage angles, the same horn movement may produce less useful steering movement.
That changing relationship is why the complete geometry needs a physical test.

# Backlash Makes Steering Feel Vague

Turn a loose door handle gently one way and then the other.
The handle may move a little before the latch responds.

That lost reversal movement is **backlash** or free play.

Possible sources include:

- clearance between gears inside the servo
- a loose horn on the spline
- worn ball joints
- oversized pivot holes
- flexible steering arms
- loose mounting screws

Some clearance is necessary for free movement.
Zero clearance can create friction and binding.

Too much clearance makes the tyres respond late after the command changes direction.

Measure wheel response after reversing direction because backlash is easiest to see there.

# A Servo Saver Manages Impact

When a front tyre strikes a kerb, the impact can travel backwards through the steering linkage.
That reverse load can reach the servo gears.

A **servo saver** is a compliant mechanism that yields under excessive steering load while transmitting ordinary steering commands.

Too soft can make steering vague.
Too stiff may provide little protection.

Use a proven servo saver suitable for the buggy rather than inventing one for high-speed operation.

Protection is a system decision.
A stronger servo may simply transfer crash load into a cheaper steering arm or chassis mount.

# Endpoints Must Stop Before Hard Stops

A lift stops its motor before the cabin tries to travel through the top of the shaft.
Useful steering also ends before parts crush into their physical limits.

A **mechanical stop** is the physical end of movement.
An **endpoint** is the configured limit of the steering command.

The safe reasoning order is:

1. inspect the mechanism unpowered
2. move it gently by hand to find physical limits
3. check tyre, linkage, horn and suspension clearance
4. begin with reduced electronic travel
5. centre the servo and linkage
6. increase left and right travel gradually
7. stop with visible clearance before binding
8. test again under a gentle realistic steering load

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Your steering arm reaches its mechanical stop at `28°`, but the servo command continues towards `35°`. The tyre cannot turn farther, so where does the servo's extra effort go?

The extra command becomes deformation, gear load, electrical current and heat rather than useful wheel angle. More endpoint percentage cannot force a blocked mechanism to create safe steering travel.

# Choosing a Steering Servo

Do not begin with the largest torque number.
Begin with the buggy's requirements and interfaces.

| Requirement | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|
| Fit | Exact body, lug, spline and horn-envelope dimensions |
| Travel | Required wheel angle and shortest valid horn radius |
| Torque | Steering load estimate, margin and rating voltage |
| Speed | Time per stated angle at the intended voltage |
| Voltage | Compatible servo, receiver and BEC range |
| Current | Peak or stall-current evidence and BEC capability |
| Spline | Compatible horn and servo-saver availability |
| Durability | Gear material, bearings and intended surface use |
| Environment | Manufacturer-defined dust or water protection |
| Service | Cable route, fastener access and removal path |

Metal gears may improve durability, but they do not prevent every failure.
A rigid gear train can transfer an impact elsewhere.

Labels such as waterproof or water-resistant have specific conditions.
They do not mean unlimited submersion.

Compare torque and speed only at the same stated voltage and under comparable conditions.

Record missing current data as an unknown.
Do not quietly assume the BEC can supply it.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Become the Feedback Loop

This activity needs no special equipment.

## You will need

- two people
- one finger or card pointer
- a target mark on a table or sheet of paper

## Mission

Make requested position, actual position, error and correction visible.

## Steps

1. Person A points towards the target without touching it.
2. Person B says only `clockwise`, `anticlockwise` or `hold`.
3. Person A moves in large steps while far from the target.
4. Person A makes smaller corrections when close.
5. Person B gently blocks the pointer for two seconds.
6. Notice that the request remains while the output cannot reach it.
7. Release the pointer and complete the correction.
8. Swap roles.

## Connect it to the servo

| Human role | Servo role |
|---|---|
| Target mark | Commanded position |
| Pointer angle | Actual position |
| Person B observing | Position sensor and controller |
| Spoken correction | Motor-drive direction |
| Difference from target | Position error |

The human model is slow and noisy.
That limitation helps you notice each step of the closed loop.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - The Horn-Radius Lab

## You will need

- a strip of scrap card
- paper
- pencil
- ruler
- drawing pin used only by an adult, or a rolled-paper pivot
- rubber band
- a few identical coins or paper clips as gentle model loads

## Question

How does horn radius affect travel and the ability to pull the same model load?

## Steps

1. Mark holes or tie points at `10 mm`, `20 mm` and `30 mm` from one pivot.
2. Draw a centre line and equal left and right angle limits.
3. Rotate the horn through the same total angle at each radius.
4. Trace the arc travelled by each point.
5. Attach the rubber band to the `10 mm` point.
6. Stretch it to one marked distance and notice the hand effort at the pivot.
7. Repeat at `20 mm` and `30 mm` without changing the stretch target.
8. Record travel, effort and any bending.

## Fair-test record

| Variable | This investigation |
|---|---|
| Independent | Horn-hole radius |
| Dependent | Arc travel and observed effort at the pivot |
| Controlled | Horn angle, card arm, rubber band and stretch target |

## Explain the result

The longer radius travels farther.
For the same linkage force, it also asks for more torque at the pivot.

Your hand supplies that torque in the model.
A real servo motor and gears supply it in the buggy.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Select and Commission on Paper

Use three fictional labels.
They are designed for reasoning, not purchasing.

| Servo | Supply range | Torque | Speed | Size | Current data |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | `4.8-6.0 V` | `7 kgf·cm at 6.0 V` | `0.18 s/60° at 6.0 V` | Standard | Not stated |
| B | `6.0-8.4 V` | `12 kgf·cm at 7.4 V` | `0.14 s/60° at 7.4 V` | Standard | Stall stated |
| C | `4.8-6.0 V` | `5 kgf·cm at 6.0 V` | `0.10 s/60° at 6.0 V` | Micro | Not stated |

## Fictional buggy requirement

- available servo space: standard-size envelope
- planned receiver/BEC supply: `6.0 V`
- minimum required torque at `6.0 V`: `6 kgf·cm`
- desired speed: no slower than `0.20 s/60°` at `6.0 V`
- horn and saver must be replaceable

## Steps

1. Cross out any comparison that uses different voltages.
2. Mark every missing value as an unknown.
3. Decide which label proves the torque requirement at `6.0 V`.
4. Decide which label proves the speed requirement at `6.0 V`.
5. Refuse any choice that depends on converting an unstated rating.
6. Write the evidence still needed before purchase.
7. Arrange these commissioning cards in order: drive disabled, command neutral, spline centred, horn fitted, linkage centred, endpoints reduced, route checked, travel increased gradually, both sides proved.

Servo B may look strongest, but its table does not state torque or speed at `6.0 V`.
The correct engineering answer can be **insufficient evidence**.

## Step 1 - Make the base

Cut a stiff card base about `350 mm × 240 mm`.

Draw a centre line from front to rear.
Mark a rectangular servo zone and a steering zone.

## Step 2 - Make a removable servo case

Fold a small card box or make a flat card rectangle representing the servo.

Mark:

- mounting lugs
- cable exit
- spline centre
- removal direction

Fix it with reusable paper tabs rather than permanent glue.

## Step 3 - Make the servo horn

Create a stiff card arm.

Mark tie points at `10 mm`, `20 mm` and `30 mm` from its pivot.
Add a pointer and an angle scale marked `-30°`, `0°` and `+30°`.

## Step 4 - Make the steering arm

Create a second pivoting arm representing one steering output.

Add a tie-rod hole and a pointer showing model wheel angle.

## Step 5 - Add an adjustable tie rod

Use string with folded-paper loops.
Add several knots or card slots so its effective length can change.

The tie rod must push and pull without falling off.

## Step 6 - Mark commanded and actual position

Place a blue COMMAND scale beside the horn.
Place an orange ACTUAL scale beside the steering arm.

When the steering arm is blocked, the two readings should show the remaining error.

## Step 7 - Add mechanical stops

Place removable red stops at the maximum safe steering-arm angles.

The stops represent physical limits, not targets.

## Step 8 - Add endpoint flags

Place green endpoint flags just before the red stops.

Leave a visible clearance gap.

## Step 9 - Test three horn radii

Keep horn angle, steering arm and tie-rod arrangement as constant as possible.

Record:

| Horn radius | Steering-arm angle | Reaches stop? | Visible slack | Rubber-band load moved? |
|---:|---:|---|---|---|
| `10 mm` | | | | |
| `20 mm` | | | | |
| `30 mm` | | | | |

## Step 10 - Select the shortest valid radius

Choose the shortest radius that reaches your fictional required wheel angle without touching a red stop.

Write one sentence explaining why extra travel is not free.

## Step 11 - Prove repeatable centre

Move left and return to hand-set centre five times.
Mark every returned pointer position.

Repeat from the right.

A narrow cluster means more repeatable centring.
A wide or separated cluster suggests slack, flexible parts or a loose pivot.

## Step 12 - Reveal backlash

Approach the centre slowly from the left and mark the output.

Approach from the right and mark it again.
The difference represents reversal movement lost in the model.

## Step 13 - Add fault cards

Create six cards:

- command routed wrongly
- horn fitted off-centre
- horn radius too long
- tie rod rubbing the base
- endpoint beyond the stop
- loose pivot creating backlash

Store them in the paper envelope.

## Step 14 - Diagnose a hidden fault

Ask another person to place one fault card face down and alter the model to match.

Use the six checks in order:

1. COMMAND
2. CENTRE
3. RADIUS
4. ROUTE
5. LIMIT
6. PROVE

Change only the first failed item.

## Step 15 - Reflect

Write on the back of the base:

1. My selected horn radius is...
2. It provides enough travel because...
3. A longer radius would change force by...
4. My largest source of backlash is...
5. This model cannot prove real servo torque, current, temperature or electronic feedback because...

## Step 16 - Keep the artifact

Store the results and fault cards with the rig.

Keep it on your showcase shelf.
You will reuse its steering geometry in Topic 3.9.

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Protect the Force Path

The servo, horn, saver, tie rod, pivots and steering arms form one force path.

Making only the servo stronger can move the failure elsewhere.
A crash may then break a horn, strip a joint or tear the servo mount from the chassis.

A useful system needs:

- enough strength for normal steering loads
- controlled compliance for impacts
- a known replaceable part
- accessible fasteners
- safe endpoints
- evidence from progressive tests

Good design chooses where overload will cause the least dangerous and most repairable damage.

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Horn-Radius Travel

**Question:** How does horn radius affect steering-arm output angle?

**Independent variable:** Horn-hole radius.

**Dependent variable:** Steering-arm angle.

**Control variables:** Horn rotation, tie rod, steering arm, pivots and base.

**Procedure:** Test the `10 mm`, `20 mm` and `30 mm` radii through the same horn angle, repeating each three times.

**Pass condition:** Select the shortest radius that reaches the fictional required angle without touching a mechanical stop.

## Test 2 - Repeatable Centre

**Question:** Does the mechanism return to a consistent centre from both directions?

**Independent variable:** Direction of approach.

**Dependent variable:** Returned pointer position.

**Control variables:** Same model, input angle, tie-rod length and hand-set centre method.

**Procedure:** Record five returns from the left and five from the right.

**Pass condition:** Every mark lies inside the chosen paper-model tolerance band, with no separated left and right groups.

## Test 3 - Endpoint Clearance

**Question:** Do the endpoint flags stop useful movement before the mechanical stops?

**Independent variable:** Left or right command.

**Dependent variable:** Clearance between steering arm and stop.

**Control variables:** Same linkage, horn radius, endpoint flags, load and base.

**Procedure:** Approach each endpoint three times while watching every moving interface.

**Pass condition:** The required steering angle is reached with visible clearance and no rubbing, bending or joint lift.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Fitting the Horn Before Centring

An unknown spline position produces unequal left and right travel.
Establish electrical centre first, then fit the horn and adjust linkage length.

## Mistake 2 - Choosing the Longest Horn

Extra travel costs available linkage force and increases torque demand.
Use the shortest radius that still meets the wheel-angle requirement.

## Mistake 3 - Comparing Ratings at Different Voltages

Torque and speed depend on the stated supply and conditions.
Compare like with like, and mark missing evidence as unknown.

## Mistake 4 - Using Trim to Hide Bad Geometry

Large trim cannot repair a loose, bent or wrongly sized linkage.
Repair and centre mechanically before making a small final electronic correction.

## Mistake 5 - Letting the Servo Push a Hard Stop

The tyres gain no useful angle after the mechanism reaches its limit.
Reduce endpoints before binding, persistent buzzing or heat appears.

## Mistake 6 - Removing Every Bit of Clearance

Joints need enough clearance to move freely.
Aim for low backlash without creating friction or binding.

# Topic Summary 📝

- A steering servo is a closed-loop position actuator.
- Its sensor reports actual output position so the controller can reduce position error.
- The servo joins the buggy's command, energy and movement paths.
- Servo angle is not the same as wheel angle.
- Horn radius changes both linkage travel and available force.
- Torque is force multiplied by perpendicular distance from a pivot.
- Torque and speed ratings must be compared at stated voltage and conditions.
- Current demand rises with load and can become high near stall.
- Centre the servo before fitting its horn.
- Use the shortest horn radius that reaches the required wheel angle without binding.
- Set endpoints before hard mechanical stops.
- Backlash creates lost movement when steering direction reverses.
- A servo saver can protect the steering force path from impact.
- Command, centre, radius, route, limit and prove form a repeatable steering check.

# Answers 🔑

1. A standard steering servo moves towards a commanded angular position and uses feedback to hold near it. A simple DC motor normally keeps rotating while suitable power is applied.
2. The receiver sends a requested position. The sensor reports actual output position. The controller compares them, creates a position error and drives the motor and gears in the direction that reduces that error.
3. The command still asks for movement while the output cannot reach it. The motor may keep producing torque, so current demand and temperature can rise without useful motion.
4. The servo rotates its output spline. The wheel angle also depends on horn radius, tie-rod length, steering-arm radius, pivot locations, linkage angles, clearance and flexibility.
5. The hole travels through a longer arc, so linkage travel generally increases.
6. Available linkage force decreases because the same torque acts through a longer radius.
7. Supply voltage affects servo performance. Figures measured at different voltages or conditions are not a fair comparison.
8. Centring establishes a known electrical neutral. Fitting the horn afterwards gives more nearly equal useful movement to the left and right.
9. Trim or sub-trim makes a small final neutral correction after the mechanism has been centred and repaired. It should not hide bent, loose or wrongly sized parts.
10. Once the mechanism reaches a physical limit, extra command creates load, current and heat rather than useful wheel angle. A safe endpoint leaves clearance before contact.
11. Keep the mechanism, horn radius, tie-rod length, input angle and release method the same. Approach centre repeatedly from both directions, record every returned position and compare the spread with a chosen tolerance band.
12. Command, centre, radius, route, limit and prove.
