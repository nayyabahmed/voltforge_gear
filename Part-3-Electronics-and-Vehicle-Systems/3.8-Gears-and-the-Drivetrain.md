# Topic 3.8 - Gears and the Drivetrain

> **A fast motor is only useful when every tooth, shaft and joint delivers its rotation to the tyres.**

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- trace the drivetrain from the motor to the driven tyres
- calculate simple and compound reduction ratios
- explain the ideal trade-off between output speed and torque
- predict gear direction and explain why a differential is useful
- diagnose mesh, backlash, alignment and friction faults
- build and test a keepable cardboard drivetrain demonstrator

# Before We Begin

Two buggies use the same motor and battery.

Buggy A launches smoothly but reaches only a gentle speed.

Buggy B sounds fast, then its motor pinion spins against a clicking spur gear.

The motor is alive in both vehicles.

The wheel result is completely different.

In Buggy A, a large reduction trades shaft speed for wheel torque.

In Buggy B, poor gear contact fails to carry the torque at all.

The mystery is no longer inside the motor.

It lies along the mechanical route between motor and road.

Our investigation will use six checks:

1. **Route** - can we name every part that carries rotation?
2. **Ratio** - how many input turns produce one output turn?
3. **Direction** - which way should each part rotate?
4. **Mesh** - do the teeth meet with suitable contact and clearance?
5. **Alignment** - do shafts and bearings hold the intended geometry?
6. **Freedom** - can differentials and joints move without binding?

At the end, you will build a card drivetrain that can be deliberately misadjusted and diagnosed.

# Check 1 - Follow the Route

Imagine a relay race.

One runner can be extremely fast, but the team loses if a baton handover fails.

The buggy's rotating parts form a mechanical relay.

The complete route that carries motor rotation and torque to the driven wheels is the **drivetrain**.

A common two-wheel-drive route is:

```text
motor -> pinion -> spur gear -> differential -> driveshafts -> wheel hubs -> tyres
```

A four-wheel-drive buggy may add:

- a centre shaft or belt
- front and rear gear sets
- front, centre and rear differentials
- more bearings, joints and housings

The exact route depends on the vehicle.

Do not force a generic diagram onto a real buggy.

Use its exploded view and trace the actual parts.

Every handover has an interface:

- motor shaft to pinion
- pinion teeth to spur teeth
- spur gear to differential case or gearbox input
- differential output to driveshaft
- driveshaft joint to hub
- wheel hex to wheel
- tyre to ground

One loose screw, stripped tooth or slipping wheel hex can interrupt the relay.

# The First Handover - Pinion and Spur

The small gear fixed to the motor shaft is commonly the **pinion**.

The larger gear it drives is commonly the **spur gear**.

These are usually external gears, so their teeth meet around their outside edges.

Two meshing external gears rotate in opposite directions.

The motor and pinion turn one way.

The spur turns the other way.

Gear teeth are not interchangeable merely because two gears look similar.

The teeth must share a compatible tooth system, such as the specified module or pitch, and suitable tooth geometry.

Do not force together gears from unknown systems.

Matched parts or the vehicle manufacturer's approved combinations are the safer starting point.

# Check 2 - Ratio

Think about a small child and an adult walking side by side.

The child takes several short steps while the adult takes one long step.

Meshing gears also exchange different numbers of tooth steps.

A **gear ratio** compares input and output rotation.

For a small driving pinion and larger driven spur:

```text
reduction ratio = driven gear teeth / driving gear teeth
```

Always name the driver and driven gear.

The same two tooth counts give a different meaning if power flows the other way.

## Worked example - one reduction stage

**Question:** A `20-tooth` motor pinion drives a `60-tooth` spur gear. What is the ideal reduction ratio and output speed from an `18,000 RPM` motor?

**Known values:**

```text
driving pinion = 20 teeth
driven spur = 60 teeth
input speed = 18,000 RPM
```

**Ratio rule:**

```text
reduction ratio = driven teeth / driving teeth
```

**Ratio calculation:**

```text
reduction ratio = 60 / 20
reduction ratio = 3
```

This is a `3:1` reduction.

The pinion turns three times for one ideal spur turn.

**Output-speed rule:**

```text
ideal output speed = input speed / reduction ratio
```

**Output-speed calculation:**

```text
ideal output speed = 18,000 / 3
ideal output speed = 6,000 RPM
```

**Meaning:**

The simple model predicts one-third of the input speed at the spur.

Ignoring losses, it also predicts about three times the input torque at that shaft.

Real loaded speed and torque are lower than ideal predictions because the motor slows under load and the drivetrain loses energy.

# The Speed-Torque Trade-Off

Remember carrying shopping upstairs from Topic 1.4.

You can move a light bag quickly or a heavy bag slowly with a similar power limit.

Gearing reorganises speed and torque; it does not create free power.

More reduction generally provides:

- lower theoretical wheel speed
- greater ideal wheel torque multiplication
- easier acceleration from rest
- a lower motor load for the same wheel demand

Less reduction generally provides:

- higher theoretical wheel speed
- less ideal wheel torque multiplication
- slower acceleration if torque is insufficient
- greater risk of high motor current and heat

These are system tendencies, not guaranteed lap results.

Tyre grip can limit usable wheel torque.

Battery voltage can fall under load.

The motor and ESC can reach temperature protection.

The driver may lose time in corners.

That is why ratio changes require measured tests.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> A `4:1` reduction gives roughly four times the ideal output torque of a direct shaft, so has the gearbox created extra mechanical power from nothing?

No. The ideal output turns about four times more slowly, so increased torque is exchanged for reduced angular speed. Real gears also lose some energy through friction, tooth deformation, lubricant movement and bearing losses, so output power is lower than input power.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - The Turn Counter

This activity needs paper, a pencil and two circular objects to draw around.

## Question

Does the measured turn relationship match the tooth-count prediction?

## Make the model

1. Draw one small circle and one circle about three times as large.
2. Mark `12` equal positions around the small circle.
3. Mark `36` equal positions around the large circle.
4. Add a centre point and one highlighted start mark to each.
5. Place the circles so their edge marks meet.

## Predict

```text
ratio = 36 / 12 = 3:1
```

Predict how far the large disc should turn when the small disc completes three turns.

## Test

1. Move the discs mark by mark without slipping.
2. Count input turns.
3. Count output turns.
4. Repeat three times.
5. Record any counting or slipping error.

## Upgrade

Cut square card teeth only with an adult nearby, then compare the toothed model with the sliding paper discs.

The no-cut version tests ratio reasoning without special equipment.

# Compound Ratios

A single bicycle gear pair may not provide the complete mechanical advantage.

Several stages can work one after another.

A **compound gear train** contains more than one ratio stage connected in sequence.

The total ideal ratio is the product of the stage ratios:

```text
total ratio = stage 1 ratio x stage 2 ratio x ...
```

## Worked example - two stages

Stage 1 is `3:1`.

Stage 2 is `2.5:1`.

```text
total ratio = 3 x 2.5
total ratio = 7.5:1
```

The input turns `7.5` times for one ideal output turn.

If input speed is `15,000 RPM`:

```text
ideal output speed = 15,000 / 7.5
ideal output speed = 2,000 RPM
```

Check whether a published ratio is:

- only the pinion-to-spur ratio
- an internal gearbox or differential ratio
- the total final-drive ratio

Never multiply a stage twice because it appears in two tables.

# Check 3 - Direction

Turn two bottle caps while their edges touch.

When one edge moves forwards at the contact, the other edge must move backwards.

Two external gears therefore reverse rotation direction.

Add a third gear between them and direction reverses twice.

The final gear then rotates in the same direction as the first.

The middle gear is an **idler gear** when its main job is to transfer motion, change direction or bridge space without changing the ideal overall ratio.

For a simple three-gear train, the idler's tooth count appears once as driven and once as driving, so it cancels from the ideal speed ratio.

It still affects centre positions, direction, strength, friction and packaging.

# Check 4 - Mesh

Imagine a door handle with no looseness at all.

It may jam as soon as the parts warm or move slightly out of line.

Now imagine a handle so loose that it rattles before moving the latch.

Gear teeth also need controlled clearance.

**Gear mesh** describes how mating teeth contact and clear one another.

**Backlash** is the small lost movement caused by clearance between mating tooth faces.

Too-tight mesh can cause:

- high turning resistance
- whining noise
- heat in the motor and ESC
- rapid tooth or bearing wear
- tight spots as the gears rotate

Too-loose mesh can cause:

- clicking or tooth impact
- excessive backlash
- partial tooth contact
- skipping under load
- stripped or damaged teeth

The aim is not zero clearance.

The aim is the correct mesh for those gears and that manufacturer procedure.

Some RC manuals specify a narrow paper-strip method.

Others use fixed motor holes, mesh adjusters or a different check.

Do not teach one method as universal.

Use the exact vehicle and gear instructions.

Check the mesh at several positions around the spur gear because runout or damage can create a tight spot.

# Check 5 - Alignment

Slide a pencil through two paper loops.

If the loops point along the same line, the pencil moves freely.

Twist one loop and the pencil rubs or locks.

A **bearing** supports a rotating shaft while controlling its position and reducing friction.

Two good bearings can still bind when their axes do not agree.

Check:

- shaft straightness
- bearing condition
- bearing seats fully home
- parallel shaft axes where the design requires them
- gear faces aligned across their width
- axial movement or side-to-side play
- housing stiffness under load
- dirt, damage and correct lubrication

Do not hide poor alignment with motor power.

An unpowered drivetrain should reveal smooth resistance and repeatable tight spots.

# Check 6 - Freedom

The drivetrain must transmit torque without forcing every connected part to move identically.

Two places need controlled freedom: the differential and the driveshaft joints.

## Why the driven wheels need different speeds

Walk around a table with one hand near the table edge and your other shoulder farther away.

The outside side of your body travels along a larger circle.

In the same time, it must travel farther.

The outside wheel of a turning buggy follows a longer path than the inside wheel.

If both driven wheels are locked to the same speed, one or both tyres must scrub or slip.

A **differential** is a gear mechanism that can deliver drive while allowing its two outputs to rotate at different speeds.

In straight running with equal tyres and grip, the output speeds can be equal.

In a turn, the outside output can speed up while the inside output slows.

Differential behaviour also depends on its design, lubricant, friction and traction conditions.

Treat it as a precision donor or purchased module unless the project has a separate reason to design one.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Place two coins side by side and roll them around two drawn curves sharing the same centre. Why must the coin on the outside curve complete more turns if both coins have the same diameter?

The outside curve has a larger radius and therefore a longer distance around the same corner. Equal-diameter wheels cover the same distance per turn, so the outside wheel needs more turns. A differential permits that speed difference while keeping the wheels connected to the drive system.

## Driveshafts and joints

A rigid straight shaft works only when its endpoints stay aligned.

Buggy suspension moves the wheel relative to the differential.

Steering also changes the front hub angle.

A **driveshaft** carries torque between those moving modules.

Its joints must work through the full planned movement.

Check:

- joint angle
- plunge or length change
- pin and outdrive engagement
- retention
- clearance from chassis and suspension parts
- binding at full compression, full droop and steering lock

A shaft that fits at normal ride height may bind or fall out at an extreme position.

Never prove this by applying more motor power.

Move the suspension and steering slowly by hand with power disconnected.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Differential Walk

This activity needs no equipment.

## Question

Why do equal wheels need different speeds in a turn?

## Method

1. One learner stands as the centre of a turn.
2. A second learner stands one arm's length away as the inside wheel.
3. A third learner stands another arm's length outward as the outside wheel.
4. Both wheel learners complete one circle in the same time.
5. Count their steps.
6. Repeat at a slower, controlled pace.

## Evidence

The outside learner should travel farther and usually take more steps.

```text
longer path in same time -> greater distance per second -> more wheel turns per second
```

# Losses Leave Clues

Every handover loses some input energy.

The losses may appear as:

- heat
- noise
- vibration
- tooth wear
- tyre scrub
- flexing parts

Useful diagnostic clues include:

| Clue | Possible cause | Safe next check |
|---|---|---|
| Tight spot once per spur turn | Runout, damaged tooth or off-centre gear | Mark position and rotate unpowered |
| Constant heavy drag | Tight mesh, misaligned bearings or rubbing seal | Isolate one stage with power disconnected |
| Clicking under light hand load | Loose mesh or damaged tooth | Inspect engagement and tooth condition |
| Wheel stops at one suspension position | Driveshaft binding or interference | Move slowly through full travel |
| Large delay before output moves | Excess backlash, loose hub or slipping connection | Mark input and output, then reverse gently |
| Rough feel from one side | Bearing, joint or differential fault | Compare left and right modules separately |

Preserve the clue before dismantling everything.

Mark the tight position and take a photograph.

Then isolate one stage at a time.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Remove and disconnect the battery before touching gears, shafts, joints or wheels.
> Confirm the system cannot re-energise accidentally.
> Keep gear covers fitted for powered operation.
> Keep fingers, hair, loose clothing, jewellery and tools away from rotating parts.
> Never stop a powered drivetrain by hand.
> Any real powered test requires direct adult supervision, the exact manuals and a clear stop procedure.

# Topic Mini Project - Adjustable Card Drivetrain 🛠️

Build a reusable unpowered model that changes ratio, direction and mesh.

The artifact includes:

- two interchangeable gear pairs
- one removable idler
- adjustable shaft supports
- a turn counter
- a simple corner-path differential board
- six fault cards

## Materials

- corrugated cardboard or cereal-box card
- paper
- two pencils, paper straws or tightly rolled-paper shafts
- small round objects to trace
- ruler, pencil and pens
- glue or tape
- scissors
- two bottle caps or card washers, optional
- string, optional

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult what you plan to build, and build with them nearby.
> Use age-appropriate scissors on a clear table.
> Use blunt paper or pencil shafts and keep their ends away from faces.
> Do not add a motor, battery, propeller, metal gear or powered drive to this model.

> **🎬 Watch the build**
>
> - BBC Bitesize KS3 Design and Technology: search `gears mechanical systems` with an adult to review driver, driven gear and ratio.
> - [Pololu - Motors and Gearboxes](https://www.pololu.com/docs/0J21/5.c): compare its real `30:1` gearmotor example with your card model.

## Step 1 - Make the base

Cut a stiff card rectangle about the size of an open exercise book.

Draw a left-hand input zone and right-hand output zone.

Mark a straight shaft reference line through each zone.

## Step 2 - Make the first gear pair

Trace one small and one large circle.

Mark `12` equally spaced teeth on the small gear and `36` on the large gear.

If cutting teeth, use broad square card tabs rather than tiny precision shapes.

The model needs countable engagement, not high-speed accuracy.

## Step 3 - Make a second gear pair

Create an `18-tooth` driver and `36-tooth` driven gear.

Label the pairs `3:1` and `2:1` only after calculating them.

## Step 4 - Add turn marks

Colour one tooth on every gear.

Draw a full-turn arrow around each centre.

These marks make counting repeatable.

## Step 5 - Make adjustable supports

Build two folded card towers with holes for the shafts.

Fix the output tower to the base.

Mount the input tower on a taped paper slider so centre distance can change.

Mark three positions:

```text
TIGHT | TEST | LOOSE
```

## Step 6 - Check shaft alignment

Measure the distance between shaft references at the front and back of the towers.

Adjust until the shafts appear parallel.

Rotate each shaft without gears fitted.

It should not bind in its supports.

## Step 7 - Fit the 3:1 pair

Place the `12-tooth` driver and `36-tooth` driven gear on their shafts.

Set the slider to the middle test position.

Rotate slowly by hand.

Do not force card teeth that catch.

## Step 8 - Count the ratio

Turn the input three complete turns.

Count the output turns.

Repeat three times and record the result.

## Step 9 - Swap the pair

Fit the `18` and `36` pair.

Predict the output before turning.

Run the same counting method.

## Step 10 - Add the idler

Make a third gear of any workable tooth count.

Add a removable shaft hole between input and output.

Predict ratio and direction, then test.

## Step 11 - Explore mesh

Move the input tower to `TIGHT`.

Record resistance and tooth marks without forcing it.

Move to `LOOSE`.

Record lost motion and skipping.

Return to the smoothest unpowered card position and label it `MODEL PASS`.

That label applies only to the card model.

It does not define real RC gear mesh.

## Step 12 - Add a backlash pointer

Tape a paper pointer to the input and output shafts.

Reverse the input gently.

Observe how far it moves before the output responds.

Mark the lost movement.

## Step 13 - Make the corner board

On the unused end of the base, draw two concentric quarter-circle wheel paths.

Make two equal wheel discs with turn marks.

Roll them along the paths and compare turns.

Label the longer path `OUTSIDE WHEEL - MORE TURNS`.

## Step 14 - Add the drivetrain route

Draw or attach cards for:

```text
motor -> pinion -> spur -> differential -> driveshaft -> hub -> tyre
```

Use a string or arrow to trace the baton handovers.

## Step 15 - Make fault cards

Create six cards:

- wrong ratio label
- direction prediction reversed
- mesh too tight
- mesh too loose
- shafts not parallel
- wheel paths forced equal

Store them face down.

## Step 16 - Run a mystery

Ask another learner to choose one hidden fault.

Diagnose it using:

```text
route -> ratio -> direction -> mesh -> alignment -> freedom
```

They must record the observation that supports the answer.

## Step 17 - Fair-test two ratios

**Independent variable:** gear pair.

**Dependent variable:** measured output turns for six input turns.

**Controls:** same shafts, supports, mesh method, operator and counting marks.

Repeat each pair three times.

Compare the average with the ideal tooth-count prediction.

## Step 18 - Reflect

Write three statements on the base:

1. More reduction changed output turns by...
2. The idler changed direction but did not change the ideal first-to-last ratio because...
3. Card gears cannot prove real RC strength, wear or safe powered mesh because...

## Step 19 - Keep the artifact

Write your name, date and revision on the base.

Keep the Adjustable Card Drivetrain on your showcase shelf.

Use its output wheel when Topic 3.9 adds steering movement.

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Isolate One Stage

A drivetrain may contain several gear meshes, bearings and joints.

Turning the complete system reveals a problem but not always its location.

Use staged isolation:

1. Disconnect power and make the system safe.
2. Mark the position of any tight spot.
3. Remove or disconnect one approved module at a time.
4. Turn each remaining section by hand.
5. Compare left and right modules where possible.
6. Record where the symptom disappears.
7. Inspect that interface before changing parts elsewhere.

Do not dismantle a sealed differential without its service instructions, correct tools and adult supervision.

Small washers, shims and gear orientation can matter.

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Ratio

**Question:** Does measured card-model ratio match the tooth-count prediction?

**Independent variable:** `12:36` or `18:36` gear pair.

**Dependent variable:** Output turns for six input turns.

**Control variables:** Same supports, shafts, mesh position, turn marks, operator and counting method.

**Procedure:** Predict each result, run three trials and compare the average with the ideal ratio.

**Pass condition:** Direction is correct and the measured output is within one card-tooth step of the prediction in every trial.

## Test 2 - Mesh

**Question:** Which card-model centre position transfers motion without binding or repeated skipping?

**Independent variable:** `TIGHT`, `TEST` or `LOOSE` slider position.

**Dependent variable:** Input resistance, visible clearance, skips and output response.

**Control variables:** Same gear pair, shafts, supports, operator, number of turns and no added load.

**Procedure:** Turn slowly through ten input turns at each position and record evidence without forcing the model.

**Pass condition:** The chosen model position completes ten turns without binding or skipping and is clearly labelled as not defining real RC mesh.

## Test 3 - Alignment and Freedom

**Question:** Can the model distinguish parallel supports from a misaligned or constrained route?

**Independent variable:** Support alignment or inside/outside wheel-path condition.

**Dependent variable:** Turning resistance, tight spots or difference in wheel turns.

**Control variables:** Same shafts, gears, wheel discs, path endpoints and movement rate.

**Procedure:** Compare aligned with deliberately offset supports, then roll equal discs along the two corner paths.

**Pass condition:** The learner identifies the misaligned condition and explains why the outside path requires more equal-wheel turns.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Calling a Larger Reduction Faster

A larger numerical reduction usually means fewer output turns per input turn.

Always state input, output and ratio meaning.

## Mistake 2 - Mixing Incompatible Teeth

Matching outside diameter does not prove matching module, pitch or tooth form.

Use a verified matched system.

## Mistake 3 - Ignoring an Internal Ratio

Multiply every real stage once to obtain total reduction.

Do not confuse stage ratio with final-drive ratio.

## Mistake 4 - Setting Mesh Too Tight or Too Loose

Zero clearance can bind, while excessive clearance can skip and damage teeth.

Extra power turns alignment or friction errors into current, heat and wear.

# Topic Summary 📝

- The drivetrain is the full mechanical route from motor to driven tyres.
- A reduction ratio compares driven teeth with driving teeth.
- More ideal reduction lowers output speed and multiplies output torque.
- Gearing exchanges speed and torque; it does not create power.
- Compound stage ratios multiply.
- Each external gear mesh reverses rotation direction.
- An idler changes direction and geometry but cancels from the ideal first-to-last ratio.
- Useful mesh needs verified tooth compatibility, contact and controlled clearance.
- Bearings and housings must keep shafts aligned.
- A differential lets driven wheels turn at different speeds around a corner.
- Driveshafts must remain free throughout suspension and steering travel.
- Noise, heat, backlash and tight spots are evidence of losses or faults.

# New Words 📖

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Backlash | Small lost movement caused by clearance between mating gear teeth or connected parts |
| Bearing | A component that supports a rotating shaft and helps control friction and position |
| Compound gear train | Two or more connected ratio stages working in sequence |
| Differential | A gear mechanism that delivers drive while allowing two outputs to rotate at different speeds |
| Driveshaft | A shaft and joint assembly carrying torque between moving drivetrain modules |
| Drivetrain | The complete mechanical route carrying rotation and torque from motor to driven tyres |
| Gear mesh | The contact and clearance relationship between mating gear teeth |
| Gear ratio | The relationship between input and output rotations |
| Idler gear | An intermediate gear used mainly to transfer motion, change direction or bridge distance |
| Pinion | The small gear commonly fixed to the motor shaft in an RC drivetrain |
| Reduction | A ratio arrangement giving fewer output turns than input turns |
| Spur gear | The larger driven gear commonly meshed with the motor pinion |

# Answers 🔑

2. `60 / 15 = 4`, so the pair gives an ideal `4:1` reduction.
3. Ideal output speed becomes one quarter of input speed, while ideal output torque becomes about four times the input torque. Real output is lower because of losses and motor behaviour under load.
4. Multiply them: `3 x 2.5 = 7.5`, giving a total ideal reduction of `7.5:1`.

11. Motor power can hide small drag, move exposed parts and convert binding into damaging current, heat and wear. Unpowered isolation is safer and preserves the fault evidence.
