# Topic 3.2 - Voltage, Current, Resistance and Power

> **"A fast buggy needs more than electricity.
> It needs the right electrical push, flow, restriction and rate of energy transfer."**

---

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- Explain voltage, current, resistance and power in plain language.
- Distinguish complete, open and short-circuit paths.
- Use `V = I × R` and `P = V × I` in simple calculations.
- Explain why a stalled motor or poor connector may become hot.
- Rehearse safe meter placement without powering RC equipment.
- Build and test a reusable electricity relationship trainer.

# Before We Begin

Your VoltForge buggy is raised safely on a stand.
The wheels spin quickly.

Place the buggy on rough ground in your imagination.
It accelerates slowly, the motor works harder and one poor connector becomes warm.

Nothing about the battery label changed.
So what changed inside the electrical system?

The answer needs four ideas.
The battery provides an electrical difference, the circuit allows charge to move, every path resists that movement and the system transfers energy at a measurable rate.

Engineers call those ideas **voltage**, **current**, **resistance** and **power**.
They help us explain why a buggy moves, slows, heats or stops without blaming parts at random.

We will explore them with paper models, calculations and a virtual circuit.
Real RC batteries remain disconnected throughout this topic.

# Four Jobs in One Electrical Story

Imagine a marble track with a raised start, a stream of marbles, a narrow gate and a paddle wheel.

- The height difference gives the marbles a reason to move.
- The number passing each second describes the flow.
- The narrow gate opposes the flow.
- The turning paddle shows how quickly energy is transferred.

The electrical names are:

| Everyday picture | Electrical quantity | Symbol | Unit |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Difference that can drive movement | Voltage | `V` | volt (`V`) |
| Rate of flow past a point | Current | `I` | ampere (`A`) |
| Opposition to the flow | Resistance | `R` | ohm (`Ω`) |
| Rate of energy transfer | Power | `P` | watt (`W`) |

An analogy is a thinking tool, not a perfect copy.
Electric charge is already present in the conductors, and electrical effects travel through the circuit differently from marbles rolling down a track.

The analogy earns its place because it stops four related quantities becoming one vague idea called "electricity".

# The Path Must Be Complete

A delivery van cannot finish a circular route if a bridge is missing.
Current also needs a complete conducting route.

A **circuit** is a connected path through which electric charge can move.

Three path conditions matter immediately:

| Condition | What has happened | Buggy symptom or risk |
|---|---|---|
| Complete circuit | The intended route is unbroken | Useful current may flow through the load |
| Open circuit | A switch or connection breaks the route | Current stops in that path |
| Short circuit | An unintended low-resistance route bypasses the load | Current may become dangerously high |

A **load** is a component that transfers electrical energy into another form.
The motor changes electrical energy into rotation and heat.
The steering servo changes it into controlled movement and heat.

An open switch can stop current safely.
A damaged connection between positive and negative may create a short circuit.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Never deliberately short a battery, cell, power supply or charged capacitor.
> A short circuit can cause intense current, heat, burns, fire or battery damage.
> Keep real RC batteries physically disconnected throughout this topic.

# Voltage - The Electrical Difference

Picture two identical balls.
One rests on the floor and one rests on a shelf.

The raised ball has energy available because its height differs from the floor.
The chosen floor is the reference point.

**Voltage** is electric potential difference between two points.
It describes energy transferred per unit of electric charge.

Voltage is measured in **volts**, written `V`.

A voltage always compares two points.
Asking for "the voltage here" without a reference is like asking for shelf height without choosing a floor.

> **[F5 force and motion overlay - Figure 3.2.3: two identical balls on ramps sharing the same floor reference. One starts on a low ramp and one on a high ramp. Add vertical dimension arrows from the floor, downhill motion arrows and a small paired circuit showing two measurement points. Label "difference from reference" rather than "amount stored". The reader should notice that voltage is a difference between points, not a substance inside one terminal.]**
>
> *Figure 3.2.3 - Both height and voltage need two points before their difference has meaning.*
>
> *Alt text: Two ramp heights and two electrical measurement points show that voltage is a difference measured from a reference.*

Common labels include `1.5 V` on one alkaline cell and `5 V` on a USB supply.
Battery-pack values and safe charging belong to Topic 3.3.

## More voltage does not automatically mean a better buggy

A suitable motor may spin faster when its applied voltage rises under similar conditions.
That does not make higher voltage automatically safe.

Speed also depends on motor design, load, gearing, battery condition, control and friction.
Too much voltage can exceed component ratings and increase heating.

Compatibility comes before performance.

# Current - The Rate of Charge Flow

Stand beside a doorway when school finishes.
Counting pupils who pass each second gives a rate of flow.

**Electric current** is the rate at which electric charge passes a point.

Current is measured in **amperes**, usually called **amps** and written `A`.
One ampere means one **coulomb** - a fixed amount of electric charge - passes per second.

The current in a motor circuit changes with its operating condition.

- A freely spinning motor usually draws relatively little current.
- An accelerating motor draws more.
- A motor prevented from turning may draw much more.

The last condition is a **stall**.
The motor tries to create turning force but cannot rotate normally.

The reader should notice that the supply did not force a fixed current into both cases.]**
>
> *Figure 3.2.4 - Current draw depends on the connected system and load; a stopped motor can demand far more than a free-running one.*
>
> *Alt text: A free-running motor with a small current arrow is compared with a stalled motor showing a large current arrow and heat warning.*

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Turn a bicycle wheel while it is lifted from the ground.
> Now imagine turning it while someone holds the tyre firmly.
> Which situation demands more effort from you?

The held wheel demands more turning force.
An electric motor responds to heavier load by drawing more current, and near stall that extra current can heat it quickly.

Never stall an RC motor as an experiment.
Topic 3.7 develops safe motor tests.

## A current rating is not a command

A source marked `10 A maximum` does not force `10 A` through every connected device.
The circuit and its operating condition determine the current drawn, while the rating states a limit the source can supply under stated conditions.

That distinction prevents a common specification mistake.

# Resistance - Opposition to Current

Try breathing gently through a wide drinking straw.
Now imagine a narrower straw of the same length.

The narrow path opposes the airflow more strongly.

**Resistance** is the opposition a component or material offers to electric current.
It is measured in **ohms**, written `Ω`.

For wires of the same material and temperature, resistance is affected by:

- length
- cross-sectional area
- material
- temperature
- connection quality

A long, thin wire normally has more resistance than a short, thick wire made from the same material.

> **[F7 comparison array - Figure 3.2.5: four labelled conductor samples drawn to the same scale. Show short/thick, long/thick, short/thin and a connector with corrosion at its contact. Add equal starting current arrows and show relative opposition with progressively tighter hatch marks. Avoid claiming exact values. The reader should notice which single physical feature changes in each comparison.]**
>
> *Figure 3.2.5 - Length, cross-section and contact condition can change resistance even when two wires look broadly similar.*
>
> *Alt text: Four conductors compare the effects of length, thickness and a poor contact on electrical resistance.*

Some resistance is deliberate.
A resistor may limit current to protect an LED.

Some resistance is unwanted.
A loose connector, corroded contact or undersized wire can waste energy as heat.

# Ohm's Law - Connecting Voltage, Current and Resistance

Imagine a door pushed with the same effort.
A stiff hinge allows less movement than a free hinge.

In a simple resistive circuit, more electrical push produces more current, while more resistance produces less current.
The formal relationship is **Ohm's law**:

```text
V = I × R
```

The relationship can be rearranged:

```text
I = V ÷ R
R = V ÷ I
```

> **[F7 comparison array - Figure 3.2.6: three equation cards built around the same V-I-R triangle. Card 1 covers V and reveals I × R. Card 2 covers I and reveals V ÷ R. Card 3 covers R and reveals V ÷ I. Beside them, show a simple source-resistor loop with the measured quantities labelled at the correct places. The reader should notice that the unknown decides which form is useful.]**
>
> *Figure 3.2.6 - Rearranging a relationship changes the calculation, not the physical circuit.*
>
> *Alt text: Three Ohm's law cards show how covering the unknown reveals the needed multiplication or division.*

## Worked Example - Predicting current in a test resistor

### The question

A `6 V` source is connected across a `12 Ω` test resistor in a simplified model.
What current does Ohm's law predict?

### Known values

```text
V = 6 V
R = 12 Ω
```

### The rule

```text
I = V ÷ R
```

### The calculation

```text
I = 6 ÷ 12
I = 0.5 A
```

### The meaning

The model predicts `0.5 A`, which is also `500 mA`.

The calculation does not prove that the source, resistor or wires are suitable.
Their ratings and temperatures still need checking.

## Why a motor is not one fixed resistor

Ohm's law is reliable for components that behave resistively under the conditions being considered.
An operating motor also produces electromagnetic effects that change with speed, load and switching.

A resistance reading across an unpowered motor is therefore not enough to predict every running current.
Use suitable manufacturer data and controlled tests instead.

# Power - How Quickly Energy Is Transferred

Remember carrying shopping upstairs from Topic 1.4.
Carrying the same bags in half the time transfers energy twice as quickly.

**Electrical power** is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred.
Power is measured in **watts**, written `W`.

For a direct-current example:

```text
P = V × I
```

## Worked Example - Electrical input power

### The question

An ESC receives `7.4 V` while the system draws `5 A`.
What electrical input power reaches it at that moment?

### Known values

```text
V = 7.4 V
I = 5 A
```

### The rule

```text
P = V × I
```

### The calculation

```text
P = 7.4 × 5
P = 37 W
```

### The meaning

The electrical input power is `37 W` at that moment.
Not all of it becomes useful wheel power because the ESC, motor, wires, gears and bearings also warm or create other losses.

Imagine ten pushes enter a mechanism but only eight help turn its output.
**Efficiency** compares the useful output energy with the total input energy.

## Energy is not power

A full bath and a fast-running tap describe different things.
The bath contains an amount, while the tap has a rate of flow.

Power is a rate.
Energy is the total transferred over time.

```text
Energy = Power × Time
```

A high-power motor can use stored energy quickly.
A battery with more energy may run the buggy longer, but it may also add mass.

Topic 3.3 explores battery capacity and stored energy.

# Why a Tiny Resistance Can Make Serious Heat

A slightly rough wheel bearing may feel harmless when turned slowly.
At racing speed, the repeated rubbing wastes far more energy.

Electrical contacts behave similarly.
Even a small unwanted resistance can dissipate serious heat when a large current passes.

For a poor connection with `0.05 Ω` resistance carrying `20 A`:

```text
V_drop = I × R
V_drop = 20 × 0.05
V_drop = 1 V
```

The power turned into heat at that connection is:

```text
P_loss = V_drop × I
P_loss = 1 × 20
P_loss = 20 W
```

Include the note "calculation only - do not reproduce". The reader should notice how a tiny contact area can create a local hot spot.]**
>
> *Figure 3.2.7 - A small numerical resistance can matter when high current concentrates heating at one poor contact.*
>
> *Alt text: A connector cross-section compares clean broad contact with a loose corroded hot spot carrying the same high current.*

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> In the same `0.05 Ω` connector, imagine current rises from `10 A` to `20 A`.
> The current only doubles.
> Do you expect the heat-producing power to double or increase by more?

The loss rises from `5 W` to `20 W`, so it becomes four times larger.
Substituting Ohm's law into the power equation gives `P_loss = I² × R`, which makes current especially important in drive wiring.

Do not reproduce the calculation physically.
It explains why hot connectors are a stop signal, not a normal feature.

# Reading RC Electrical Labels

A rating is an answer to one question, not permission to connect parts.

| Label | Question it helps answer | What it cannot prove alone |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage range | Can this device accept the planned supply voltage? | That the rest of the system is compatible |
| Continuous current | What can it handle under stated conditions? | That cooling in your chassis is adequate |
| Peak current | What brief current may be allowed? | That it can carry that current continuously |
| Power | How quickly is energy transferred? | Useful mechanical power at the wheels |
| Resistance | How strongly does this path oppose current? | Complete motor behaviour under load |

Look for conditions beside every number:

- nominal, minimum or maximum
- continuous or peak
- temperature and cooling
- test voltage
- duration
- measurement method

The number `10` without a unit and condition is unfinished engineering information.

# Measuring Without Guessing

A **multimeter** is an instrument that can measure several electrical quantities.
Its connection changes with the measurement.

| Measurement | Where the meter goes | Circuit condition |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage | Across two points | Powered only in an approved supervised procedure |
| Current | In series, so current passes through the meter | Power removed while the circuit is opened and meter connected |
| Resistance | Across an isolated component | Unpowered and discharged |

Add a red crossed-out inset showing a current meter placed directly across a battery. The reader should notice that meter mode, socket and placement form one decision.]**

Connecting a meter set for current directly across a source can create a low-resistance path.
Measuring resistance on a powered circuit can damage equipment or produce a false result.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Use only extra-low-voltage educational circuits under responsible adult supervision.
> Check the exact meter manual, lead sockets, selector setting, range and connection before every measurement.
> Never probe mains electricity, household sockets, chargers, exposed power supplies or damaged batteries.
> Do not measure RC drive current in this topic.

The safest measurements here are virtual.
The paper-meter activity lets you rehearse the decisions without electrical energy.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Become the Circuit

This activity needs no special equipment.

## Solo version

Choose four points in a room and name them SOURCE, SWITCH, LOAD and RETURN.

Walk the complete loop and tap one finger against your thumb at each point.
Count how many complete loops you make in ten seconds.

Now stop at the SWITCH.
The path is open, so no complete loop is possible.

Finally, imagine a shortcut from SOURCE straight to RETURN that avoids the LOAD.
Point to it, but do not act it out with electrical equipment.

## Team upgrade

Use folded paper tokens and four people or chairs.
Pass the tokens around the complete loop at a steady rate.

Add a rule that the LOAD may pass only one token every two seconds.
Notice how one restriction changes the rate around the whole series loop.

## What you should discover

The source may still exist when an open path stops the flow.
Resistance changes the rate, while a short circuit creates the wrong route.

The model does not copy moving charge exactly.
It makes path, rate and restriction visible.

# Topic Mini Project - Electricity Relationship Trainer 🛠️

Build a reusable card dashboard for voltage, current, resistance and power.

Two rotating relationship wheels will reveal the correct formula.
Scenario cards will make you choose the quantity, unit, calculation and buggy meaning.

## What the artifact teaches

The trainer embodies four ideas:

- electrical quantities have different jobs
- units carry meaning
- relationships can be rearranged
- a calculated answer still needs an engineering interpretation

It does not measure a circuit.
It trains the reasoning that should happen before measurement.

## Materials

- cereal-box or packaging card
- scrap paper
- pencil and coloured pens
- ruler
- age-appropriate scissors
- tape or glue
- two paper fasteners, or folded-paper tabs
- one reused envelope

No electronic parts are required.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult or guardian what you plan to build before starting, and build with them nearby.
> Use age-appropriate scissors on a clear table and cut away from fingers.
> Do not use a craft knife.
> Keep real batteries and powered circuits away from this card project.

## Step 1 - Make the dashboard

Cut a card base about `300 mm × 220 mm`.

Divide the left side into four labelled zones:

```text
VOLTAGE | CURRENT | RESISTANCE | POWER
```

Give each zone its symbol, unit and one plain-language meaning.

## Step 2 - Make the V-I-R wheel

Make two card circles of equal size.
Cut one window in the top circle and join them at the centre with a paper fastener or folded tab.

Arrange the lower circle so the window can reveal:

```text
V = I × R
I = V ÷ R
R = V ÷ I
```

## Step 3 - Make the P-V-I wheel

Repeat the method for:

```text
P = V × I
V = P ÷ I
I = P ÷ V
```

Write `rate of energy transfer` beside `P`.

## Step 4 - Add unit gates

Make four flaps labelled `V`, `A`, `Ω` and `W`.
Under each flap, write the quantity name.

A calculated number cannot leave the board until its unit gate is chosen.

## Step 5 - Add the buggy meaning box

Draw a large box labelled:

```text
WHAT DOES THIS ANSWER MEAN FOR THE BUGGY?
```

Possible endings include:

- this is predicted current for a simple resistor, not a motor
- this value is electrical input power, not wheel output
- the voltage sits outside the stated device range
- the number lacks a continuous or peak condition

## Step 6 - Make scenario cards

Create at least eight cards:

1. `V = 6 V`, `R = 12 Ω`; find `I`.
2. `I = 2 A`, `R = 3 Ω`; find `V`.
3. `V = 5 V`, `I = 0.4 A`; find `P`.
4. `P = 20 W`, `V = 10 V`; find `I`.
5. Identify a complete circuit.
6. Predict the symptom of an open circuit.
7. Explain why a short circuit is unsafe.
8. Choose correct paper-meter placement.

Write a model answer on the back of every card.

## Step 7 - Add fault cards

Make six small fault cards:

- unit missing
- multiplied instead of divided
- power confused with energy
- motor treated as a fixed resistor
- peak rating treated as continuous
- current meter placed across a source

Store them in the envelope.

## Step 8 - Run the trainer test

Shuffle the scenario cards.

For each one:

1. identify the known quantities
2. name the unknown
3. select the relationship
4. calculate
5. attach the unit
6. explain the buggy meaning

Ask another person to plant one fault card.
Find and correct the mistake without changing unrelated parts of the answer.

## Step 9 - Reflect and keep it

Write four sentences on the back:

1. Voltage is not current because...
2. Resistance affects current because...
3. Power is not stored energy because...
4. The marble analogy stops being useful when...

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Values Need Context

An engineer does not write `10` and expect everyone to understand.

A useful electrical value includes:

- quantity
- number
- unit
- operating condition
- measurement or calculation method
- acceptable range

`10 A continuous under stated cooling` means something different from `10 A peak for a brief interval`.

Units and conditions turn numbers into evidence.

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Quantity identification

**Question:** Can the learner distinguish voltage, current, resistance and power?

**Independent variable:** Quantity card shown.

**Dependent variable:** Meaning, symbol and unit stated.

**Control variables:** Same four cards, time limit and no notes.

**Procedure:** Shuffle and reveal every quantity card.

**Pass condition:** All four meanings, symbols and units are correct.

## Test 2 - Relationship calculation

**Question:** Can the learner choose and use the correct relationship?

**Independent variable:** Scenario card.

**Dependent variable:** Correct result, unit and interpretation.

**Control variables:** Same trainer, calculator rule and six-card set.

**Procedure:** Solve six shuffled numerical cards and explain each result.

**Pass condition:** At least five calculations are correct and all six answers include units.

## Test 3 - Circuit diagnosis

**Question:** Can the learner identify complete, open and short-circuit paths?

**Independent variable:** Paper circuit condition.

**Dependent variable:** Correct condition, predicted symptom and safe response.

**Control variables:** Same source and load symbols; unpowered paper diagrams only.

**Procedure:** Inspect three diagrams without touching electrical equipment.

**Pass condition:** All three conditions and safe responses are correct.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Calling voltage "the amount of electricity"

Voltage is a difference between two points.
Current describes a rate of charge flow.

## Mistake 2 - Believing a source forces its maximum current into every device

The connected circuit and operating condition determine current drawn.
A source current rating states a limit, not a command.

## Mistake 3 - Forgetting the unit

The number `5` could mean `5 V`, `5 A`, `5 Ω` or `5 W`.
An answer without its unit is incomplete.

## Mistake 4 - Treating a motor as one fixed resistor

Motor current changes with voltage, speed, load, temperature and controller action.
One unpowered resistance reading cannot describe every running condition.

## Mistake 5 - Treating peak current as continuous current

A brief peak rating and a continuous rating answer different questions.
Time, temperature and cooling conditions matter.

## Mistake 6 - Putting a current meter across a source

A current meter belongs in series under an approved procedure.
Putting it across a source may create a dangerous low-resistance path.

# Topic Summary 📝

- A complete intended circuit allows current to flow through the load.
- An open circuit breaks that route.
- A short circuit creates an unintended low-resistance route and can be dangerous.
- Voltage is electric potential difference measured in volts.
- Current is the rate of charge flow measured in amperes.
- Resistance is opposition to current measured in ohms.
- Ohm's law links voltage, current and resistance for suitable resistive conditions.
- Power is the rate of energy transfer measured in watts.
- Power and stored energy are related but not identical.
- Motor current changes with operating load and may rise sharply near stall.
- Small unwanted resistance can create serious heating at high current.
- Meter setting, sockets and placement must match the quantity.
- Every useful value needs a unit and operating condition.

# Answers 🔑

1. A complete circuit has an unbroken intended path; an open circuit contains a break that stops current in that path.
2. A short circuit bypasses the intended load with a low-resistance route, so current and heating may become dangerously high.
3. Voltage is electric potential difference between two points. Its unit is the volt (`V`).
4. Current is the rate at which electric charge passes a point. Its unit is the ampere (`A`).
5. The stalled motor must produce high turning force while it cannot rotate normally, so it may draw far more current and heat quickly.
6. The long, thin wire normally has greater resistance when material and temperature are the same.
7. `I = V ÷ R = 9 ÷ 18 = 0.5 A`.
8. `P = V × I = 8 × 3 = 24 W`.
9. Some input power becomes heat and other losses in the ESC, motor, wiring and drivetrain.
10. A resistance meter applies its own small test signal; external power or other circuit paths can cause unsafe or misleading results.
11. Heating rises strongly with current. A small resistance carrying high current can dissipate substantial power at one contact.
12. It should include the quantity, number, unit, operating condition, method and acceptable range.
