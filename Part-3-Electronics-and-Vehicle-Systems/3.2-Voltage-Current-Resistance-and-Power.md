# Topic 3.2 - Voltage, Current, Resistance and Power

> **"Voltage provides the push, current describes the flow, resistance opposes it, and power tells us how quickly energy is being used."**

---

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- describe voltage as an electrical difference that can drive charge
- describe current as the rate of electrical charge flow
- explain how resistance limits current
- calculate one missing value using `V = I x R`
- calculate electrical power using `P = V x I`
- identify complete, open and short circuits
- recognise why measurements need correct meter settings and connections
- connect electrical quantities to the battery, ESC, motor and wiring of the buggy
- build a keepable card trainer that solves realistic RC examples

# Before We Begin

Imagine two identical toy cars at the top of two ramps.

One ramp is low and gentle.
The other is high and steep.

The car on the steeper ramp has a stronger reason to move, even before it starts.
That difference is a useful first picture for **voltage**.

Now imagine marbles passing through a tube.
The number passing a point each second is like **current**.
A narrow or rough section that makes the flow harder is like **resistance**.

Your VoltForge buggy needs all three ideas.
The battery provides an electrical push, the wiring carries current, and every motor winding and connector resists the flow a little.

The rate at which the system transfers energy is its electrical **power**.

These four quantities help us choose compatible parts instead of trusting plug shapes or guesses.

---

# Start with a Complete Path

A train cannot complete a circular route if one rail has been removed.
Electric current also needs a complete conducting path.

A **circuit** is a connected route through which electrical charge can move.

Three circuit conditions matter immediately:

| Condition | What the path is like | Expected result |
|---|---|---|
| Complete circuit | Intended route is unbroken | Current can flow |
| Open circuit | A switch, wire or connection breaks the route | Current stops |
| Short circuit | An unintended low-resistance route bypasses the load | Current may become dangerously high |

In the buggy, the motor is an intended **load**.
A load is a component that transfers electrical energy into another useful form.

The motor changes electrical energy into rotation and heat.
The servo changes it into controlled movement and heat.

An open switch can stop the system safely.
A damaged connector that joins positive directly to negative can create a short circuit.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Never deliberately short any battery, cell, power supply or charged capacitor.
> A short circuit can cause intense current, heat, burns, fire or battery damage.
> Keep real RC batteries disconnected throughout this topic.

> **[Signature visual: four side-by-side water-loop and circuit pairs. Pair 1 shows height difference as voltage. Pair 2 shows flow rate as current. Pair 3 shows a narrow section as resistance. Pair 4 shows a water wheel turning quickly as power. Labels state where the analogy helps and where it stops.]**

The water picture is useful, but electricity is not water inside a wire.
It is a model that helps us separate four related ideas.

# Voltage - The Electrical Difference

Think about a ball on a flat floor and the same ball on a shelf.
The raised ball has energy available because of its position relative to the floor.

**Voltage** is an electrical potential difference between two points.
It describes how much energy is transferred per unit of electric charge.

Voltage is measured in **volts**, written `V`.

A voltage only makes sense between two points.
We do not ask for the height of a shelf without choosing a floor or other reference.
In the same way, a meter compares one electrical point with another.

Common examples include:

| Source | Typical label | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Single alkaline cell | 1.5 V nominal | The labelled operating voltage of one cell |
| USB supply | 5 V | Regulated voltage between its power terminals |
| Two-cell LiPo pack | 7.4 V nominal | Two 3.7 V nominal cells connected in series |

**Nominal** means the convenient named value used to describe a device.
The real measured voltage may vary during use.

Battery voltages, safe charging and cell limits belong to Topic 3.3.

## Voltage does not mean speed by itself

A higher voltage can make a suitable motor spin faster under similar conditions.
But it can also exceed the limits of the motor or ESC.

Speed also depends on:

- motor design
- load
- gearing
- battery condition
- controller behaviour
- friction

Therefore, `more volts = better buggy` is not an engineering rule.
Compatibility comes first.

---

# Current - How Much Charge Passes

Stand beside a doorway at the end of school.
Counting pupils who pass each second tells you a flow rate.

**Electric current** is the rate at which electric charge passes a point.

Current is measured in **amperes**, usually called **amps** and written `A`.

A current of `1 A` means one coulomb of charge passes each second.
You do not need to count individual charges to use the idea.

The important question is how much current the circuit draws under its actual load.

An RC motor may draw:

- little current while spinning freely
- more current while accelerating the buggy
- much more current when the wheels are stuck

The final case is called a **stall** condition.
The motor tries to create torque but cannot rotate normally.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Turn a bicycle wheel while it is lifted from the ground.
> Now imagine trying to turn it while someone holds the tyre firmly.
> Which situation demands more effort from you?

The stalled wheel demands more torque.
An electric motor commonly responds by drawing more current, which produces more heating.

Never stall an RC motor as an experiment.
Topic 3.7 explains safe motor tests.

# Resistance - Opposition to Current

Try breathing gently through an open drinking straw.
Now imagine a much narrower straw of the same length.

The narrower path opposes the airflow more strongly.

**Resistance** is the opposition a component or material offers to electric current.

Resistance is measured in **ohms**, written `Ω`.

Resistance depends on factors including:

- material
- length
- cross-sectional area
- temperature
- quality of connections

A long, thin wire usually has more resistance than a short, thick wire of the same material.

Remember Topic 2.9.
A poor solder joint or loose connector may add unwanted resistance.
Current passing through that resistance creates heat and wastes energy.

Some resistance is deliberate.
A resistor in an LED circuit limits current to protect the LED.

Some resistance is unwanted.
Corroded connectors, undersized wires and damaged contacts can reduce buggy performance.

---

# Ohm's Law - Connecting V, I and R

Voltage, current and resistance are connected.
For many simple components under steady conditions, the relationship is:

```text
V = I x R
```

This relationship is called **Ohm's law**.

The symbols mean:

- `V` = voltage in volts
- `I` = current in amperes
- `R` = resistance in ohms

It can be rearranged:

```text
I = V / R
R = V / I
```

## Worked Example - Current in a Simple Test Circuit

### The question

A `6 V` supply is connected across a `12 Ω` test resistor.
What current does the simplified calculation predict?

### Known values

```text
V = 6 V
R = 12 Ω
```

### The rule

```text
I = V / R
```

### The calculation

```text
I = 6 / 12
I = 0.5 A
```

### The meaning

The predicted current is `0.5 A`, or `500 mA`.

This does not automatically prove that the resistor, wiring or source is suitable.
Their power, current and temperature ratings must also be checked.

## Ohm's law has boundaries

An RC motor is not a simple fixed resistor.
Its effective behaviour changes with rotation, load, temperature and electronic switching.

Ohm's law remains valuable for understanding circuits and voltage drops.
But a motor's real current should come from suitable specifications and controlled measurements, not from one resistance reading alone.

# Power - How Quickly Energy Is Transferred

Remember carrying shopping upstairs from Topic 1.4.
Doing the same work in less time requires more power.

**Electrical power** is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred.

Power is measured in **watts**, written `W`.

For a direct-current example:

```text
P = V x I
```

## Worked Example - Input Power to a Motor System

### The question

An ESC receives `7.4 V` while the system draws `5 A`.
What electrical input power is being supplied at that moment?

### Known values

```text
V = 7.4 V
I = 5 A
```

### The rule

```text
P = V x I
```

### The calculation

```text
P = 7.4 x 5
P = 37 W
```

### The meaning

The electrical input power is `37 W` at that moment.

Not all `37 W` becomes useful wheel power.
Some becomes heat in the ESC, motor, wires, gears and bearings.

The ratio of useful output to input is **efficiency**.

---

# Energy Is Not the Same as Power

A fast-running tap and a bathtub full of water describe different things.
One is a rate, while the other is an amount stored or delivered over time.

Power is the rate of energy transfer.
Energy is the total amount transferred.

```text
Energy = Power x Time
```

A high-power motor can use energy quickly.
A battery with more stored energy may run the buggy longer, but it may also add mass.

Battery capacity and energy are explored in Topic 3.3.

---

# Series and Parallel Paths

Imagine two torch cells placed end to end.
Their electrical pushes add when they are connected correctly in **series**.

In a series circuit:

- components share one route
- the same current passes through each series component
- voltage differences divide across the components

Imagine two separate lanes connecting the same start and finish.
That is closer to a **parallel** arrangement.

In a parallel circuit:

- branches share the same voltage between their endpoints
- current can divide between branches
- total current is the sum of branch currents

Battery pack construction is specialist work.
Do not build or modify RC battery packs.

We use series and parallel ideas to read diagrams and specifications, not to experiment on lithium cells.

---

# Voltage Drop - A Difference Along the Path

Picture pushing a heavy trolley along a rough corridor.
Some of your effort is lost overcoming rough patches.

Real wires and connectors have resistance.
Current through resistance causes a **voltage drop**.

For a simplified resistive section:

```text
V_drop = I x R
```

Suppose a poor connection has `0.05 Ω` resistance and carries `20 A`.

```text
V_drop = 20 x 0.05
V_drop = 1 V
```

One volt is lost across one small connection.

Its heat production would be:

```text
P = V x I
P = 1 x 20
P = 20 W
```

That is why a high-current connector can become dangerously hot even when its resistance looks numerically small.

Do not reproduce this example physically.
It is a calculation showing why inspection and correct connectors matter.

# Reading an RC Specification Label

Electrical labels answer different questions.

| Label | Question it helps answer | What it does not prove alone |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage range | Can the device accept this supply voltage? | That every other part is compatible |
| Continuous current | What current can it handle under stated conditions? | That cooling in your buggy is adequate |
| Peak current | What short-duration current may be allowed? | That it can sustain that current |
| Power | How quickly energy may be transferred? | Mechanical output power at the wheels |
| Resistance | How strongly does this path oppose current? | Full motor behaviour under load |

Ratings need conditions.
Airflow, temperature, duty cycle and measurement method can change the result.

Use manufacturer instructions for the exact component.
Never select a battery, ESC or motor from one attractive number.

# Measuring Without Guessing

A **multimeter** can measure electrical quantities when connected and configured correctly.

The three common jobs are different:

| Measurement | Meter connection | Circuit condition |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage | Across two points | May be powered under a supervised procedure |
| Current | In series with the path | Requires correct current socket and range |
| Resistance | Across the isolated component | Must be unpowered and discharged |

Connecting a meter set for current directly across a battery may create a short circuit.
Measuring resistance on a powered circuit can damage the meter or give false results.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Use only extra-low-voltage educational circuits with a responsible adult.
> Before every measurement, check the lead sockets, selector setting, range and connection method.
> Never probe mains electricity, household sockets, chargers, exposed power supplies or damaged batteries.
> Do not measure RC drive current until a later supervised test plan provides suitable equipment and containment.

The safest measurement in this topic is a virtual circuit or an unpowered resistance check on an isolated classroom resistor.

# Fair Electrical Tests

An electrical test should change one planned variable at a time.

Suppose we investigate how resistance changes predicted current.

| Fair-test part | Choice |
|---|---|
| Independent variable | Resistance value |
| Dependent variable | Calculated or simulated current |
| Control variables | Same voltage and circuit arrangement |

If voltage and resistance both change, we cannot tell which change caused the result.

Also record units.
The number `5` could mean `5 V`, `5 A`, `5 Ω` or `5 W`.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Human Circuit

## You will need

- four people if available
- four paper labels
- ten small counters, dry pasta pieces or folded paper tokens

## Steps

1. Label four positions SOURCE, SWITCH, LOAD and RETURN.
2. Arrange them in a loop.
3. Pass tokens around the complete loop at a steady rate.
4. Count how many pass the LOAD in ten seconds.
5. Open the loop by asking the SWITCH to stop passing tokens.
6. Observe that the source may still exist while the flow stops.
7. Add a rule that the LOAD may pass only one token every two seconds.
8. Observe how a restriction changes the flow rate.

## What the model teaches

The tokens make rate of flow visible.
The broken hand-off represents an open circuit.
The passing restriction represents resistance.

The model does not show real charges accurately.
Its purpose is to separate path, flow and restriction.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - Relationship Card Sort

## You will need

- scrap paper
- pencil

## Steps

1. Make four cards labelled VOLTAGE, CURRENT, RESISTANCE and POWER.
2. Make four unit cards labelled V, A, Ω and W.
3. Match every quantity to its unit.
4. Add one plain-language explanation to each pair.
5. Arrange voltage, current and resistance around `V = I x R`.
6. Arrange voltage, current and power around `P = V x I`.
7. Ask another learner to explain one relationship without reading your notes.

## Challenge

Add cards for an open circuit, complete circuit and short circuit.
Place each beside the flow condition it predicts.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Virtual Resistor Investigation

## You will need

- PhET Circuit Construction Kit DC or Tinkercad Circuits
- adult-approved computer or tablet access
- results table

## Steps

1. Build a simple virtual source, resistor and switch loop.
2. Set a fixed safe simulated voltage.
3. Record the current.
4. Double the resistance while keeping the voltage unchanged.
5. Record the new current.
6. Repeat with three resistance values.
7. Plot or describe the pattern.

## Fair-test record

- independent variable: resistance
- dependent variable: current
- controls: voltage, circuit layout and measurement method

## Expected discovery

For the simple model, increasing resistance reduces current when voltage stays fixed.

# Hands-On Activity 4 - Specification Detective

## You will need

- printed example labels supplied by an adult, or fictional labels you create
- pencil

## Steps

1. Underline every number.
2. Circle its unit.
3. Write the quantity represented by that unit.
4. Mark each value as nominal, minimum, maximum, continuous or peak if stated.
5. Put a question mark beside any number without conditions.
6. Explain why connector shape would add no missing electrical information.

# Hands-On Activity 5 - Paper Meter Placement

## You will need

- a hand-drawn source, switch and load circuit
- two paper meter probes
- a card labelled MULTIMETER

## Steps

1. Set the paper meter to VOLTAGE.
2. Place its probes across the drawn load.
3. Set it to CURRENT.
4. Break the drawn path and place the meter in series.
5. Set it to RESISTANCE.
6. Cross out the source to show that the circuit must be unpowered.
7. Explain the danger of placing a current meter directly across a source.

This is a rehearsal only.
No real electricity is used.

# Topic Mini Project - Electricity Relationship Trainer 🛠️

Build a reusable card calculator and scenario board for voltage, current, resistance and power.

Two sliding windows will reveal the correct formula.
Scenario cards will test whether you can choose the relationship, keep units and explain the result.

## What the artifact teaches

The trainer embodies these ideas:

- quantities are different even when they interact
- units carry meaning
- one relationship can be rearranged
- an answer needs an engineering interpretation

It does not measure real circuits.
It trains the reasoning needed before measurement.

## Materials

- cereal-box card
- scrap paper
- pencil and coloured pens
- ruler
- scissors suitable for card
- tape or glue
- two paper fasteners, or folded-paper tabs as a no-buy alternative
- one envelope

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult or guardian what you plan to build before starting, and build with them nearby.
> Use age-appropriate scissors on a clear table and cut away from fingers.
> Do not use a craft knife.
> Keep all real batteries and powered circuits away from this card project.

> **🎬 Watch the build**
>
> - Science Buddies: search "paper circuit" to see how labelled paths can represent circuit ideas.
> - PhET Interactive Simulations: search "Circuit Construction Kit DC" with an adult to see the quantities change before building your paper trainer.

## Step 1 - Make the base

Cut a card rectangle about `300 mm x 220 mm`.

Write this title at the top:

```text
VOLTAGE - CURRENT - RESISTANCE - POWER
```

## Step 2 - Create the quantity panels

Divide the left half into four boxes.

Give each box:

- quantity name
- symbol
- unit name
- unit symbol
- one plain-language meaning

Use a different colour for each quantity.

## Step 3 - Make the Ohm's law triangle

Cut a small triangle from card.

Write `V` at the top.
Write `I` and `R` along the bottom.

Cover one symbol with your finger.
The remaining layout reminds you whether to multiply or divide.

The triangle is a memory tool, not a replacement for understanding.

## Step 4 - Make the power relationship card

Create a second card showing:

```text
P = V x I
```

On its back, write:

```text
V = P / I
I = P / V
```

## Step 5 - Add unit gates

Make four small flaps labelled:

- V
- A
- Ω
- W

Under each flap, write the matching quantity.

A calculated number cannot leave the board until its unit flap is chosen.

## Step 6 - Add the engineering meaning box

Draw a box labelled:

```text
WHAT DOES THE ANSWER MEAN FOR THE BUGGY?
```

Every solved card must end with one sentence in this box.

Examples include:

- predicted current is below the stated test limit
- power is electrical input, not wheel output
- this voltage does not match the device range

## Step 7 - Make scenario cards

Create at least eight cards.

Use examples such as:

1. `V = 6 V`, `R = 12 Ω`, find `I`.
2. `I = 2 A`, `R = 3 Ω`, find `V`.
3. `V = 5 V`, `I = 0.4 A`, find `P`.
4. `P = 20 W`, `V = 10 V`, find `I`.
5. Label a complete circuit.
6. Identify an open circuit symptom.
7. Explain why a short circuit is unsafe.
8. Choose correct paper-meter placement.

Write answers on the back.

## Step 8 - Add RC module cards

Make small cards for BATTERY, ESC, MOTOR, SERVO and WIRE.

Match each to useful rating questions.

For example:

```text
ESC
What voltage range?
What continuous current?
Under what cooling conditions?
```

## Step 9 - Test the formula choice

Shuffle the numerical cards.

For each one:

1. identify the known values
2. state the unknown value
3. select the rule
4. calculate
5. attach the correct unit
6. explain the answer

## Step 10 - Test the circuit language

Ask another person to choose one circuit-condition card.

Explain:

- whether the path is complete
- whether useful current should flow
- what the safe next action would be

## Step 11 - Diagnose a wrong answer

Deliberately create one fault:

- missing unit
- multiplied instead of divided
- confused power with energy
- treated a motor as a fixed resistor
- assumed a peak rating was continuous

Find and correct the fault.

## Step 12 - Reflection

Write three sentences on the back:

1. Voltage is not current because...
2. Resistance changes current because...
3. Power matters to the buggy because...

Then add one limitation of the water analogy.

## Step 13 - Keep the artifact

Store the scenario cards in the envelope.
Attach it to the back of the trainer.

Keep the completed trainer on your showcase shelf.

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Values Need Context

An engineer does not write `10` and expect everyone to understand.

A useful electrical value includes:

- quantity
- number
- unit
- operating condition
- measurement or calculation method
- acceptable range

`10 A continuous with the specified cooling` is different from `10 A peak for a brief interval`.

This habit prevents specifications from becoming decoration.

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Quantity identification

**Question:** Can the learner distinguish voltage, current, resistance and power?

**Independent variable:** Quantity card shown.

**Dependent variable:** Correct meaning, symbol and unit stated.

**Control variables:** Same four cards, same time and no reference notes.

**Procedure:** Shuffle and reveal every card.

**Pass condition:** Four correct meanings, symbols and units.

## Test 2 - Relationship calculation

**Question:** Can the learner choose and use the correct relationship?

**Independent variable:** Scenario card.

**Dependent variable:** Correct result with unit and interpretation.

**Control variables:** Same trainer, calculator rules and six-card set.

**Procedure:** Solve six shuffled numerical cards and explain each answer.

**Pass condition:** At least five correct calculations and all six answers include units.

## Test 3 - Circuit diagnosis

**Question:** Can the learner identify complete, open and short-circuit conditions?

**Independent variable:** Drawn circuit condition.

**Dependent variable:** Correct condition, expected symptom and safe response.

**Control variables:** Same source and load symbols, unpowered paper diagrams only.

**Procedure:** Inspect three diagrams without touching real equipment.

**Pass condition:** All three conditions and safe responses are correct.

# Common Beginner Mistakes ❌

## Mistake 1 - Calling voltage "the amount of electricity"

Voltage is a potential difference, not an amount flowing past a point.

## Mistake 2 - Treating current as something a battery forces into every device

The connected system and its operating condition affect the current drawn.

## Mistake 3 - Forgetting units

An electrical number without a unit is incomplete.

## Mistake 4 - Assuming resistance is always bad

Some resistance is useful and deliberate, while some is unwanted loss.

## Mistake 5 - Applying a fixed-resistor calculation directly to a motor

Motor behaviour changes with speed, load and temperature.

## Mistake 6 - Confusing power with stored energy

Power is a rate; energy accumulates over time.

## Mistake 7 - Treating a peak rating as continuous

The time and conditions attached to a rating matter.

## Mistake 8 - Measuring resistance on a powered circuit

Isolate and discharge the component before a supervised resistance measurement.

## Mistake 9 - Placing a current meter across a source

That arrangement may create a dangerous low-resistance path.

## Mistake 10 - Believing a matching connector proves compatibility

Shape does not confirm voltage, current, polarity or signal arrangement.

# Topic Summary 📝

- A circuit needs a complete intended path for current to flow.
- An open circuit breaks the path.
- A short circuit creates an unintended low-resistance route and can be dangerous.
- Voltage is an electrical potential difference measured in volts.
- Current is the rate of charge flow measured in amperes.
- Resistance is opposition to current measured in ohms.
- Ohm's law connects voltage, current and resistance in simple resistive conditions.
- Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer and is measured in watts.
- `P = V x I` calculates direct-current electrical input power at a moment.
- Real motors do not behave like one fixed resistor.
- Meter setting, lead socket and connection method must match the measurement.
- Values need units and operating conditions.
- Real RC batteries remain disconnected until Topic 3.3 establishes their safety rules.
