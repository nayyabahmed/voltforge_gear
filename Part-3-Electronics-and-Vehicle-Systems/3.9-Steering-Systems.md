# Topic 3.9 - Steering Systems

> **A steering system succeeds when both front tyres follow the corner instead of fighting it.**

# Learning Objectives 🎯

By the end of this topic you will be able to:

- trace movement from the servo output to both front wheels
- measure steering centre, toe and wheel angle consistently
- explain why the inside front wheel usually turns more sharply
- separate useful steering throw from binding and collision
- diagnose free play, asymmetry and bump steer
- build and fair-test a keepable cardboard steering geometry rig

# Before We Begin

Your buggy is lifted onto a stand with its drive disabled.

The steering appears to work.

Both front wheels turn left and right.

Place it on the floor, however, and three clues appear.

It makes a neat circle to the left but a much wider circle to the right.

At full lock, the inside tyre scrubs sideways instead of rolling cleanly.

When you press the front suspension down, the wheels change direction even though nobody moves the transmitter.

The servo is moving.

The steering system is not yet steering well.

This topic follows six checks:

1. **Centre** - do the command, linkage and wheels share a repeatable middle position?
2. **Route** - can movement travel through every lever, link and pivot?
3. **Toe** - where do the wheel planes point when the buggy is straight?
4. **Angle** - does each wheel receive a suitable angle for its path?
5. **Clearance** - does useful movement end before a collision or bind?
6. **Travel** - does suspension movement change the steering without a command?

At the end, you will build a card rig that makes each fault visible.

# Check 1 - Find a True Centre

Imagine hanging a picture frame.

If the frame is already crooked when you call it level, every later measurement is misleading.

A steering system also needs a reliable starting position.

The **steering centre** is the intended straight-ahead condition of the complete system.

It is not merely the centre mark on the transmitter.

It includes:

- the transmitter control at neutral
- the receiver sending a neutral command
- the servo centred within its useful range
- the horn or servo saver installed sensibly
- the steering rack or bellcranks centred
- the tie rods adjusted mechanically
- the front wheels pointing to the chosen starting toe

Remember Topic 3.5.

Electronic trim is a small correction tool.

It should not hide a horn fitted far from centre or unequal link lengths.

Large electronic correction can leave more steering travel on one side than the other.

That explains our first clue: a buggy can turn more tightly in one direction even when the servo seems healthy.

# Check 2 - Follow the Movement Route

Think of opening a gate using a rope around a post.

Your pull begins in one direction.

The rope and hinge redirect it into rotation.

Buggy steering also redirects movement several times.

A common route is:

```text
servo -> horn or servo saver -> drag link -> bellcranks or rack -> steering links -> steering arms -> knuckles -> wheels
```

The exact parts vary between buggies.

Trace the actual exploded drawing or manual for the vehicle in front of you.

A **bellcrank** is a pivoting lever that redirects motion.

A **steering link**, often called a tie rod, carries push and pull between steering parts.

A **steering arm** is the lever on the wheel knuckle where the link attaches.

The **steering axis** is the line around which the knuckle turns.

It may pass through kingpins, hinge points or ball joints.

Every joint needs controlled freedom.

Too much looseness creates lost motion.

Too little clearance creates friction or binding.

The small unwanted movement between changing direction is **free play**.

Free play adds through the route.

A tiny gap at six joints can become a visible wobble at the tyre.

To find it, hold the servo output still and move one wheel gently left and right.

Watch the chain one joint at a time.

The first part that moves without moving its neighbour identifies a gap.

Do this only with all power disconnected.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Disconnect and remove the battery before touching or adjusting steering links.
>
> Powered servos create pinch points and can move without warning.
>
> Any later powered check must be adult-led, with drive power disabled and fingers clear of horns, gears, links and wheels.

# Check 3 - Measure Toe from One Datum

Place your feet side by side and look down.

Point your toes slightly towards each other.

Now point them slightly apart.

RC engineers use the same everyday word for the direction of paired wheels.

Viewed from above:

- **toe-in** means the front edges of the tyres point slightly towards each other
- **toe-out** means the front edges point slightly away from each other
- **zero toe** means the two wheel planes are parallel

Toe is a difference between front and rear spacing at the same height.

It is not the same as camber, which is wheel lean viewed from the front and is developed in Topic 3.10.

Consistent measurement matters more than false precision.

Use:

- the same ride height
- the same wheel-centre height
- the same reference marks on both tyres or wheel discs
- the same settling method
- the same measuring tool

Soft tyres can bulge.

Moulded tread can vary.

Wheels can wobble slightly as they rotate.

A setup board or rigid wheel discs can give more repeatable evidence than measuring random tread blocks.

If a real buggy is being set, begin with the model manufacturer's recommended value and procedure.

Do not copy a racing setup from a different vehicle or surface.

# Hands-On Activity 1 - Toe Detective

## Question

Can you identify toe from repeatable measurements rather than appearance alone?

## Materials

- two pieces of scrap card
- two pencils or paper fasteners for pivots
- ruler or a strip of paper
- pen

## Make the model

1. Draw a straight chassis centre line on scrap card.
2. Add a front axle line at right angles.
3. Make two matching wheel cards.
4. Mark a reference point at the front and rear of each wheel at centre height.
5. Pivot each wheel card at its centre.

## Predict

Set the wheels by eye to what you think is zero toe.

Write down whether you expect the two spacings to match.

## Test

1. Measure between the front reference marks.
2. Measure between the rear reference marks.
3. Record both values.
4. Classify the setup as toe-in, zero toe or toe-out.
5. Adjust one wheel and repeat.

## Improve the evidence

Use one paper strip as a go/no-go gauge instead of reading tiny ruler divisions.

The no-equipment version uses finger-width marks on a folded strip of paper.

Your model demonstrates the measurement logic.

It does not tell you the correct toe for a real buggy.

# Check 4 - Give Each Wheel the Angle Its Path Needs

Walk around a lamp post with a friend.

You stay close to the post while your friend stays two metres farther away.

You both sweep through the same turn, but your paths are not the same length or radius.

The buggy's front tyres face the same problem.

In a left turn, the left front tyre follows the inside path.

The right front tyre follows the outside path.

For ideal slow rolling, each wheel plane points towards a shared turning centre.

The inside wheel therefore needs a greater steering angle.

This relationship is called **Ackermann steering geometry**.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Put two pencils side by side and pretend they are front wheels turning left.
>
> First keep them parallel, then point both towards one spot behind the inside of the turn.
>
> In which arrangement do the pencil centre lines agree on where the buggy is turning?

Parallel wheel angles point towards different centres, so at least one tyre must slip or scrub sideways.

Different inside and outside angles can make their extended wheel lines meet near one common centre.

## Ideal geometry and real tyres

The textbook construction is a useful starting model.

It is not a promise of perfect track behaviour.

Real tyres bend and slip.

Speed changes the forces.

Loose soil behaves differently from smooth tarmac.

The chassis may use partial or adjustable Ackermann to suit its purpose.

Manufacturer manuals may describe increasing or decreasing Ackermann in different mechanical ways because their linkages differ.

Follow the exact manual for a real car.

Use the model here to understand the evidence, not to invent an adjustment rule.

## Worked example - two wheel paths

**Question:** A simplified buggy has a `300 mm` wheelbase and a `240 mm` front track.

Its centre follows a `600 mm` radius at very low speed.

What ideal angles do the inside and outside front wheels need?

**Known values:**

```text
wheelbase L = 300 mm
track t = 240 mm
centre radius R = 600 mm
half-track = 120 mm
```

**Find each wheel-path radius:**

```text
inside radius = R - t/2
inside radius = 600 - 120
inside radius = 480 mm

outside radius = R + t/2
outside radius = 600 + 120
outside radius = 720 mm
```

**Use the simplified angle rule:**

```text
steering angle = arctan(wheelbase / wheel-path radius)
```

**Calculate:**

```text
inside angle = arctan(300 / 480)
inside angle ≈ 32.0°

outside angle = arctan(300 / 720)
outside angle ≈ 22.6°
```

**Meaning:**

The simplified low-speed model predicts about `9.4°` more angle at the inside wheel.

The result explains the direction of the difference.

It does not specify the best racing setup for a real tyre and surface.

# Hands-On Activity 2 - The Two-Circle Turn

## Question

Why does the inside front wheel need a sharper angle?

## Materials

- string or two long strips of paper
- chalk, removable tape or paper markers
- a friend if available

## Method

1. Mark one turning centre.
2. Make one circular path close to it.
3. Make a second path farther away.
4. Walk side by side through one quarter turn.
5. Compare the distance each person or marker travels.
6. Point your feet along each path at the end.
7. Sketch both directions.

## Evidence

The outer path is longer and has a larger radius.

The inside direction changes more sharply over the same part of the turn.

## Solo desk version

Draw two concentric quarter-circles around a cup.

Slide one small wheel card along each line.

At three positions, draw the direction each card points.

This activity shows why the angles differ.

It does not prove a real tyre's best Ackermann setting.

# Check 5 - Stop Before Contact

**Steering throw** is the useful movement available from one side to the other.

More throw can reduce turning radius only until another limit appears.

Possible limits include:

- the tyre touching the chassis or body
- the steering arm touching a suspension part
- a ball joint reaching the end of its angle
- a link passing over centre
- a driveshaft binding at a large combined steering and suspension angle
- the servo saver compressing continuously
- the servo reaching an electronic endpoint before the mechanism is ready
- the mechanism hitting a hard stop before the servo reaches its command

Useful travel ends before hard contact.

The safe order is mechanical first, electronic second.

With power disconnected, move the mechanism gently through its intended envelope.

Find the first physical limit on each side.

Then an adult can use the exact transmitter and servo instructions to set endpoints within those mechanical limits.

There is no universal button sequence.

Remember the suitcase analogy from Topic 1.5.

A tyre needs more than enough space to exist at ride height.

Its **movement envelope** includes every position reached by steering and suspension together.

Check the four corners of that envelope:

| Suspension position | Steering position |
| --- | --- |
| droop | full left and full right |
| ride height | full left and full right |
| compression | full left and full right |

Rotate the wheel by hand at each position if the drivetrain permits it safely.

Look and feel for rubbing, stiffness and driveshaft distress.

# Check 6 - Watch Steering Through Suspension Travel

Hold a compass point still and swing the pencil around it.

The pencil follows an arc.

Suspension arms and steering links also swing through arcs.

If their pivot positions and lengths do not cooperate, suspension movement can pull or push the steering arm.

The wheel changes toe without a steering command.

This is **bump steer**.

> **🤔 Think about it.**
>
> Hold two strips of paper together at one end with a finger.
>
> Swing them through an arc, first with equal lengths and then with one much shorter.
>
> Do their free ends stay the same distance apart throughout the movement?

Different arcs do not preserve the same relationship at every position.

In a buggy, that mismatch can steer the knuckle as the suspension compresses or rebounds.

No colour-only meaning is used. The reader should notice that bump steer is created by relative arcs, not by the servo sending a command.]**
>

Bump steer is measured, not guessed.

Keep steering centred.

Set the buggy at repeatable positions:

- full droop
- ride height
- part compression
- full intended compression

Record toe or pointer position at each one.

Repeat the movement to check that the result is not free play.

Changing steering-link height, inner pivot position or outer pivot position can change bump steer.

So can changing suspension geometry.

The useful adjustment and acceptable amount are model-specific.

Begin from the manufacturer's setup and change one variable at a time.

# Hands-On Activity 3 - Arc Mismatch Detective

## Question

How does steering-link geometry affect toe change through suspension travel?

## Materials

- scrap card
- two paper strips
- three paper fasteners, pencils or folded-paper pivots
- pen
- ruler as an optional upgrade

## Build

1. Mark a chassis pivot and a knuckle pivot on card.
2. Make one strip represent the suspension arm.
3. Make a second strip represent the steering link.
4. Add a small toe pointer to the knuckle.
5. Mark droop, ride-height and compression positions.

## Fair test

**Independent variable:** inner steering-link pivot position.

**Dependent variable:** toe-pointer change through travel.

**Controls:** same arm, knuckle, outer pivot and three height positions.

## Procedure

1. Set the first inner pivot.
2. Centre the toe pointer at ride height.
3. Move to droop and mark the pointer.
4. Move to compression and mark the pointer.
5. Repeat twice.
6. Change only the inner steering-link pivot.
7. Repeat all measurements.

## Pass condition

The rig moves freely and one setting gives a smaller repeatable pointer change than the other.

The model demonstrates arc interaction.

It cannot specify a real buggy's safe pivot position or strength.

# Topic Mini Project - Adjustable Steering Geometry Rig 🛠️

Build a top-down card front end with a centred input lever, adjustable steering links, three Ackermann positions and a separate bump-steer slider.

The rig becomes a steering evidence station for your showcase shelf.

## Materials

- corrugated packaging card
- cereal-box card
- plain paper
- two pencils, drinking straws or rolled-paper tubes
- string or thread
- removable tape
- glue stick
- ruler
- protractor if available
- pens in two colours
- scissors
- paper fasteners if already available

Folded paper hinges can replace paper fasteners.

No part needs to be bought specially.

> **⚠️ SAFETY**
>
> Show a responsible adult what you plan to build and build with them nearby.
>
> Use age-appropriate scissors on a clear table and cut away from your body.
>
> Do not add a motor, battery or powered servo to this card rig.

> **🎬 Watch the build**
>
> - BBC Bitesize KS3 Design and Technology: search **linkages mechanical systems** and identify the levers and pivots before building.
> - Tekno RC EB48 manual: find its **Ackermann** and **bump steer** setup diagrams with an adult, then compare their real adjustment points with your paper model.
> - Search an official RC kit manual for **steering assembly exploded view** and trace the parts rather than copying an unverified internet build.

## Step 1 - Set the datums

Draw a chassis centre line.

Draw the front axle line at exactly `90°` to it.

Add a rear axle reference line so Ackermann lines have a target.

## Step 2 - Choose dimensions

Choose a convenient card wheelbase and front track.

Record both values on the base.

These are model dimensions, not a real purchase specification.

## Step 3 - Make the wheel cards

Cut two identical wheel cards.

Mark each wheel centre, centre plane and front edge.

## Step 4 - Make the knuckles

Attach each wheel card to a small knuckle card.

Let each knuckle pivot around a marked steering axis.

## Step 5 - Add three steering-arm holes

Mark three possible link holes on each steering arm.

Label them A, B and C.

Keep the pattern mirrored left to right.

## Step 6 - Build the input

Make a central sliding rack or paired card bellcranks.

Add a straight-ahead centre mark.

## Step 7 - Make adjustable links

Use folded paper strips, thread loops or overlapped card strips.

Add length marks so both links can be matched.

## Step 8 - Establish mechanical centre

Place the input on its centre mark.

Set both knuckles symmetrically.

Adjust the links until the chosen starting toe is repeatable.

## Step 9 - Measure toe

Add matched front and rear reference points at wheel-centre height.

Use a paper gauge to compare the spacing.

## Step 10 - Add conservative endpoints

Mark left and right input limits before any part folds, locks or touches.

These are mechanical evidence limits.

## Step 11 - Test setting A

Move both steering links to hole A.

Apply the same marked left input.

Measure inside and outside wheel angles.

## Step 12 - Extend the wheel lines

Lay string along each wheel centre plane.

Extend both lines backwards.

Mark where they meet relative to the rear axle line.

## Step 13 - Repeat settings B and C

Return to centre each time.

Keep input, toe and measuring method controlled.

Record both wheel angles and line intersection.

## Step 14 - Test right-turn symmetry

Repeat the chosen setting to the right.

Compare wheel angles, endpoint position and free movement.

## Step 15 - Add the travel board

Beside the top-view rig, make the small arc-mismatch mechanism from Activity 3.

Label droop, ride height and compression.

## Step 16 - Add six fault cards

Make cards labelled:

- horn or input off-centre
- unequal link lengths
- excessive toe
- equal wheel angles
- endpoint collision
- bump-steer arc mismatch

## Step 17 - Run a mystery

Ask someone to add one fault without telling you.

Use the six checks in order.

Record the first evidence that reveals the fault.

## Step 18 - Run a fair Ackermann comparison

**Question:** Which arm-hole setting gives a useful inside-outside angle difference while moving freely?

**Independent variable:** steering-arm hole A, B or C.

**Dependent variables:** inside angle, outside angle and line-intersection position.

**Controls:** same base, track, wheelbase, toe, input mark and measurement method.

**Procedure:** test each setting three times from centre.

**Pass condition:** the inside angle is greater, movement is repeatable, and no card part binds or collides.

## Step 19 - Reflect

Write three labels on the base:

1. **What the rig proves** - geometry, movement order and measurement method.
2. **What it suggests** - which paper setting best approaches the chosen path model.
3. **What it cannot prove** - real tyre grip, strength, servo load, speed or the best track setup.

Finish this sentence:

> My steering improved when I changed ______ because the evidence showed ______.

## Step 20 - Keep the artifact

Write your name, date and revision on the underside.

Keep the rig with your servo trainer from Topic 3.5 and drivetrain model from Topic 3.8.

Together they show command, mechanical transmission and wheel direction.

# Thinking Like an Engineer - Separate Geometry from Tuning

The card rig answers geometry questions cheaply.

It can show:

- whether movement reaches both knuckles
- whether the mechanism has a repeatable centre
- whether toe measurement uses one datum
- whether inside and outside angles differ
- whether links collide or pass over centre
- whether different arcs create bump steer

It cannot show:

- real joint strength
- servo torque or current
- tyre deformation and slip
- steering feel at speed
- safe radio endpoints
- the best setup for a particular surface

This boundary is good engineering.

A prototype earns trust by answering a narrow question honestly.

# Cheapest Valid Prototype

The complete steering geometry rig costs `£0` when made from packaging card and household scraps.

It should be built before buying adjustable links, aluminium bellcranks or a different servo.

Those parts cannot repair misunderstood geometry.

# What to Buy, Reuse and Make

## Reuse

- sound donor knuckles and steering arms
- a proven servo saver
- straight steering links with smooth joints
- original spacers and ball studs
- manufacturer setup dimensions

## Make

- card geometry templates
- a toe gauge
- chassis mounting plate
- clearance envelope cards
- setup record sheet

## Buy only after measuring

- replacement ball joints of the correct size
- straight turnbuckles of the required range
- suitable hinge pins and retaining hardware
- steering rack or bellcranks compatible with the donor geometry
- a servo only after torque, size, voltage and interface requirements are known

Do not buy an upgrade because its material sounds impressive.

Buy only when the evidence identifies the problem it must solve.

# Test Plan

## Test 1 - Ackermann relationship

**Question:** Which steering-arm position gives a suitable inside-outside angle relationship in the model?

**Independent variable:** steering-arm hole.

**Dependent variables:** inside wheel angle, outside wheel angle and line intersection.

**Control variables:** wheelbase, track, starting toe, input travel, card wheels and measuring method.

**Procedure:** return to centre, apply one marked input and measure each setting three times.

**Pass condition:** inside angle exceeds outside angle, movement is free and repeated readings agree within the chosen paper-model tolerance.

## Test 2 - Left-right symmetry

**Question:** Does equal input produce similar useful travel in both directions?

**Independent variable:** turn direction.

**Dependent variables:** inside and outside angles, endpoint clearance and return-to-centre error.

**Control variables:** linkage setting, toe, input magnitude and measurement method.

**Procedure:** perform five left and five right cycles, always approaching centre from the same direction before measuring.

**Pass condition:** both directions move freely and their corresponding results remain within the tolerance chosen before the test.

## Test 3 - Bump steer

**Question:** Which inner-link pivot gives the smallest repeatable toe change through travel?

**Independent variable:** inner steering-link pivot.

**Dependent variable:** toe-pointer change at droop, ride height and compression.

**Control variables:** suspension arm, knuckle, outer pivot, steering centre and height stops.

**Procedure:** measure all three positions three times for each pivot.

**Pass condition:** no binding occurs and one setting produces the smallest repeatable range of pointer movement.

Do not skip from a sketch to powered driving.

Each stage should retire one uncertainty.

# Topic Summary 📝

- A steering system carries movement through a route of levers, links, pivots and knuckles.
- A reliable mechanical centre comes before electronic fine adjustment.
- Toe compares front and rear wheel spacing at one repeatable height.
- Inside and outside tyres follow different-radius paths through a corner.
- Ackermann steering geometry gives the inside front wheel more angle in an ideal slow turn.
- Real tyre slip, speed, surface and packaging make the final setup vehicle-specific.
- Useful steering throw ends before binding, collision or driveshaft distress.
- Bump steer is toe change caused by suspension movement rather than a steering command.
- Free play and asymmetry are found by tracing the route one joint at a time.
- Fair tests change one geometry variable while holding the others steady.

# Answers 🔑

1. One common route is servo, horn or servo saver, drag link, bellcranks or rack, steering links, steering arms, knuckles and tyres. The exact route must be traced from the real vehicle.
2. Large trim can conceal unequal mechanical geometry and leave more useful travel on one side than the other. The horn, rack and links should be centred mechanically before small electronic correction.
3. With the wheels viewed from above, toe-in means their front edges point towards each other, zero toe means their planes are parallel, and toe-out means their front edges point away.
4. Suitable controls include the same ride height, wheel-centre measurement height, tyre or disc reference marks, settling method and measuring tool. Any four earn the answer.
5. The inside tyre follows a smaller-radius path around the shared turn. For ideal slow rolling, it must point more sharply towards the common turning centre than the outside tyre.
6. Track width places the tyres on opposite sides of the buggy centre line. Half the track was subtracted from the centre radius for the inside path and added for the outside path.
7. Real tyres deform and slip, speed and surface change forces, and the chassis imposes packaging limits. The manufacturer's tested setup is therefore the starting point for a real buggy.
8. Signs include tyre or arm contact, a joint reaching its angular stop, a link passing over centre, driveshaft binding, continuous servo-saver deflection or a hard mechanical stop. Any three earn the answer.
9. Bump steer is toe change as suspension moves without a steering command. It occurs when steering-link and suspension arcs do not preserve the same relationship through travel.
10. Keep the linkage setting, starting toe, input magnitude and measurement method unchanged. Apply equal marked inputs left and right, repeat several cycles, and compare angles, clearances and return to centre.
11. The rig can demonstrate geometry, movement order, measurement and relative changes. It cannot prove real strength, servo load, tyre grip, steering feel at speed, safe electronic endpoints or the best surface setup.
12. Centre, route, toe, angle, clearance and travel.
